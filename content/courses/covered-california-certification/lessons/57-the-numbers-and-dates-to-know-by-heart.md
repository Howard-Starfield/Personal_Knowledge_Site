# The Numbers and Dates to Know by Heart

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Sort a figure into published, the training's own, or one consumer's case, and say which kind it is before you say it out loud
- Name the windows a consumer waits on, and for each one say what it governs, where it runs from, and whose figure it is
- Quote the poverty-line thresholds and the metal tier shares without turning an average into a promise
- Run the money conversation without a flat penalty figure, and send the consumer to the body that owns the number
- Tell a figure that is looked up apart from a figure that is remembered, and say why the difference matters
- Hold a number that two different rules share, and keep the two apart when the consumer quotes it back

## Stage 1: Three kinds of number, and the test before you say it

The usual way a figure goes wrong on the phone is not that the number was
forgotten. It is that the number was there and the rule behind it was not. So
the first thing to settle is not the number. It is where the number came from.

| the kind | what it is | what you do with it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Published** | the official Covered California site states it | quote it as policy |
| **The training's own** | the training says it and the official record publishes no number of days for it | use it, label it as the training's figure, and read the deadline on the record to the consumer |
| **One consumer's case** | a date or an amount taken out of a worked example | never memorise it; it is evidence that the figure moved |

And the test, which is worth memorising because it does the work on its own:

> **Before you say a number, ask where it came from. A page on the official site, the training, or one case.**

Three answers, three different things to say. If the answer is a page on
coveredca.com, the figure is published and you can say that Covered California
publishes it. If the answer is the training and the record is silent, the
figure is the training's own: you may use it, but you say that it is the
training's figure, and you tell the consumer what their own record says. If the
answer is a case, the figure is not yours to say at all. It is evidence that a
number moved, and the next consumer's numbers will be different.

A worked example of the test. A consumer asks how long they have to send a
document the marketplace has asked for. The training's answer is 95 days for
most documents and 155 days for proof of income. The official record publishes
no number of days at all for a document request. So the answer is the
training's own figure, said as such, and the date the consumer should work to
is the one showing on their record rather than the 95.

There is a sibling lesson that sorts much of the same material a different way,
by how far the training hedged rather than by where the figure came from. The
two agree about the case figures and contradict each other about nothing that
matters. Use this one when the question is what you are allowed to say; use the
other when the question is what the training actually asserted.

One thing that is not a fourth kind: a rule that shares its number with a
different rule. Sixty days is one. Ninety days is another. Thirty days is a
third. The number is rarely the mistake - the rule behind the number is. Stage
5 is about those.

## Stage 2: The windows, and whose figure each one is

These are the clocks a consumer is actually waiting on. The last column is the
one that matters, so read a row whole: a window without a start point is not a
window, and a window without a source is a number to be careful with.

| the figure | what it governs | where it runs from | whose figure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 60 days | the general special enrolment window after a qualifying life event | the qualifying event | published |
| 60 days before or 60 days after | the window for losing job-based cover | the loss of that cover | published |
| 90 days | the special enrolment window after losing Medi-Cal | the loss of Medi-Cal cover | published |
| 90 days | filing an appeal | the date the eligibility decision was mailed | published |
| 90 days | the time the hearing and the decision can take | the date a valid appeal request is received | published |
| 90 days | the written notice an employer has to give before an HRA plan year starts | the start of the plan year | published |
| 10 days | a Medi-Cal enrollee's deadline to report a change | the change | published |
| 30 days | a Covered California enrollee's deadline to report a change | the change | published |
| 14 days | the notice to give before cover ends | the requested end date | published, and the training calls it advice rather than a rule |
| 30 business days | the time to answer a complaint | the complaint | published |
| 95 days | the reasonable opportunity period for most documents | the document request | the training's own |
| 155 days | the reasonable opportunity period for proof of income | the document request | the training's own |
| 60 days | the extension on a reasonable opportunity period, and only while the item is still open | the reasonable opportunity period | the training's own |
| 30 calendar days | getting a termination for a missing document reinstated | the date the cover ended | the training's own |
| 10 business days | the wait before an 834 resend can be escalated | the last transaction feed | the training's own |
| 1 to 10 business days | the first contact on an escalation | the transfer to the priority support unit queue | the training's own |
| 1 to 30 business days | the resolution of an escalation | the first contact | the training's own |
| 45 days | the case review the training gives the consumer | the request | the training's own, and never said definitively |
| 12 months | how long eligibility lasts on the children's programme | the start date | the training's own |

Two things to take from the table.

The first is which of them the official record publishes. Published: the
special enrolment windows after a qualifying life event and after losing
Medi-Cal, the 90 days to file an appeal, the 10 days a Medi-Cal enrollee has
to report a change, the 30 days a Covered California enrollee has to report
one, the 14 days' notice before cover ends, the 30 business days to answer a
complaint, and the 90 days an employer has to give notice before an HRA plan
year starts. The training's own: the document period and its extension, the
reinstatement window, the wait before a resend can be escalated, the escalation
timeframes, the case review figure, and the length of an eligibility period.

The second is what the last column changes in a call. A published figure can be
said flatly. A figure that is the training's own has to arrive with its label
attached, because a consumer can go and check the site and find nothing there -
and the representative who quoted an unpublished number as though it were
published is the one who gets the call back.

Two details that need their own line.

**The 14 days is published and it is advice at the same time.** The official
record says a consumer has to give at least 14 days' advance notice. The
training calls the 14 days advice rather than a rule, and says why it exists:
it lets the carrier stop billing for the following month. A consumer who calls
on 31 October is entitled to a 31 October end date. If the carrier bills for
November, the refund comes from the carrier afterwards and it takes a little
time. So the window is real and the consequence of missing it is small.

**The length of an extension is not settled, and the training says so.** The
recording calls it a 60-day extension and gives the route - go to manual
verifications and give them 60 days - while also saying in as many words that
there is no number anybody has to stand by. An earlier session describes two
extensions of 30 days each, to be requested before the 95 days run out. The
knowledge article is where the rule lives. The one thing that is not in doubt
is the condition: once the item is overdue there is no extension.

And a note on the count. Four of these windows are counted in business days,
and each one sits on a complaint or an escalation clock. Everything else is
counted in calendar days. If you are not sure which kind of day a figure is in,
say that out loud rather than guessing.

## Stage 3: The thresholds and the money

### The poverty-line thresholds

| the line | what it governs | whose figure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 138% of the federal poverty level | Medi-Cal for most adults | published |
| 266% | Medi-Cal for a child under 19 | published |
| 213% | Medi-Cal for a pregnant individual | published |
| over 213% up to 322% | full-scope Medi-Cal in pregnancy, through the Medi-Cal Access Program | published |
| 100% to 300% | the tribal route to zero cost sharing | the training's own |
| above 400% | no financial help | published |

The line that applies is picked by the person, not by the household's average.
A household can have a child who qualifies and an adult who does not, and that
is the whole point of a mixed-programme family. The trap is averaging the
household up to one number and then treating it as the answer. The application
shows you where the household sits; it is the eligibility result on the
application that shows what each person actually gets.

Two of these carry an extra caution.

The **322%** is not only the top of the pregnancy band. It is also the ceiling
of the children's programme in three counties - San Francisco, San Mateo and
Santa Clara - for a child whose household income sits above the children's
Medi-Cal line and up to that figure. That programme runs for **12 months**,
and the 12 months is the training's own figure.

The **100% to 300%** band is the training's own, and it is a band rather than a
line: above 300% a member of a federally recognized tribe can take a limited
cost-sharing plan instead. The two records do not agree about who may have that
one at all - the training puts it above 300%, the official record publishes it
as available at any household income - so do not tell a member between 100% and
300% that they cannot have it.

### The metal tiers

| the tier | the share of the average annual cost of covered care the plan pays |
| --- | --- |
| Bronze | 60% |
| Silver | 70%, rising to 94% |
| Gold | 80% |
| Platinum | 90% |

Read that column carefully. It is what the plan is expected to pay, not what
the member pays on a bill, and it is an average across everyone in the
category rather than a promise about one person's treatment. What a member
actually owes depends on the care, on the plan's deductible, and on whether the
provider is in network. Silver is not "the 70% plan": it comes as a set of
levels - 70, 73, 87 and 94 - and which one a household gets depends on its
income and size.

There is one more percentage in this area, and it is the one that outranks all
of them: a floor on the share of premium dollars that insurers have to spend
on health care, set at 80% to 85%, with 85% in the large-group market and 80%
in the small-group market. That is federal law, and coveredca.com does not
publish it, so a representative who says "the site says that" about it is
misattributing a federal rule to a state site. It is the training's statement
of a federal floor, and the rebate for falling short of it is what the floor
is for.

The training's spoken tier percentages did not hold still in one session - 60%
for Bronze, up to 94% for Silver, 80% for Gold, and then a 60% example
attached to the Gold sentence - so where the training and the comparison view
disagree, the comparison view is the source.

### The money

This is the part where a representative can do the most damage with one
sentence, so the numbers come with their bodies attached.

| what the body published | the year | the figures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the training's rough figures | none attached | up to $900 for one person, up to $2,700 for a family of four |
| Covered California, the December 2025 release | the 2026 cycle | at least $900 per adult, $450 per dependent child, $2,700 for a family of four |
| Covered California, the penalty page | the threshold is labelled tax year 2026 | at least $950 per adult, $450 per child under 18, and an affordability threshold of 8.05% of projected household income |
| the Franchise Tax Board, as the training quoted it | tax year 2025 | $950 per adult, $475 per dependent child, an affordability threshold of 7.28%, and $2,850 for a family of four |

Four rows, four different positions, and only one of them is the official
record's own.

The training's rough figures are the training's, and the training said plainly
in the same breath that you never want to say $900 - because a consumer charged
more than that afterwards comes back to the marketplace and asks why. The
December 2025 release's $2,700 is built from $900 for each adult plus $450 for
each child, so it describes a family of four made up of two adults and two
children rather than a flat amount for every household of four, and it is
already superseded.

On the penalty page, the tax year label attaches to the affordability test and
not to the amounts. The amounts themselves carry no year at all. That is the
detail that catches people out, because the 8.05% sits right next to the $950
on the same page and the two look like they belong together.

The Franchise Tax Board column is **the training's account** of what that body
publishes. This package holds no Franchise Tax Board publication of its own,
and its own verification pass could not reach one, so the figures are
internally consistent - $950 and $475 for two adults and two children is
$2,850 - but they are the training's statement rather than a record anyone can
check. Say which column you are quoting.

And the rule that outranks every number in the table: **name the body and the
year, never quote a flat number, and point the consumer at the Franchise Tax
Board's Penalty Estimator.** That body computes and collects the penalty, not
Covered California, and the estimator gives the household its own figure. If a
consumer wants to know what they will pay, that is the route.

## Stage 4: The figures that are not for memorising

Two whole families of numbers are looked up rather than remembered, and saying
so out loud is part of the job.

**The plan cost-sharing tables.** Deductibles, out-of-pocket caps, copays and
coinsurance are looked up, not memorised, and the reason is on the record: the
figures move with the plan year, and the training's slides and the site's plan
pages did not carry the same dollars for the same year. The slides had a
$5,800 standard Bronze combined deductible and a $6,650 high deductible health
plan deductible. The plan pages, retrieved on 13 October 2026, showed $5,800
with the $450 pharmacy deductible shown separately and $7,200 for the high
deductible health plan. Two sets of dollars for one plan year is the whole
reason: read the figure off the plan the consumer is actually looking at.

**The case figures.** A binder payment, a household income, a premium change -
these are evidence that a number moved, not numbers to carry. A consumer whose
premium moved from one figure to another is the proof that premiums move; the
two figures themselves belong to that consumer and to nobody else.

The test that catches both: **if you would be embarrassed to be wrong about it
in a way the consumer could check, it is looked up.** A deadline you quote on
the phone is either published or it is the training's own, and you know which
because Stage 1 told you. A dollar amount off a plan table is neither, and no
amount of memorising will make it so.

## Stage 5: The collisions

The same number answering two different questions is where a representative
gets a consumer wrong, because carrying one meaning into the other feels like
remembering.

### Sixty days, twice

Sixty days is the window to apply after a qualifying life event, and it is also
the extension you give on a reasonable opportunity period. A consumer with a
document request is not waiting out an enrolment window; a consumer with a life
event is not asking for an extension. The trap is carrying one meaning into
the other, and it is easy to do because both routes are live in the same week.

### Ninety days, four times

Ninety days is the appeal filing window, counted from the date the eligibility
decision was mailed; the time the hearing and the decision can take; the
special enrolment window after losing Medi-Cal cover; and the written notice
an employer has to give at least 90 days before an HRA plan year starts. None
of the four is the same rule as any of the others, and they do not all run
from the same date.

### Ten business days

The training's 10 business days is the wait after the last transaction feed
before a resend can be escalated. The official record carries a 10 business
days too, and it is about something else entirely: a health plan has to offer
the member a return behavioural-health appointment within 10 business days of
a referral or a first visit. The two rules meet only in the number. A
representative who answers a resend question with the behavioural-health
figure has answered a question nobody asked.

### Thirty days, three ways

Thirty days is the window to report a change. Thirty calendar days is the
reinstatement window, and it runs from the date the cover ended rather than
from the call. Thirty business days is how long a complaint takes to be
answered. What separates them is the word calendar, the word business, and
where the clock starts - and on a call the consumer will not hear the
difference unless you say it.

### The 95-day figure

The 95 days is the training's own. The official record publishes no number of
days at all for a document request. That is a distinction worth keeping in
your head, because it changes what you promise: a published deadline is
something the consumer can check, and an unpublished one is something you
have to read off their record.

And one more that is easy to miss: **45 days** is the case review figure the
training gives the consumer, and it is never said definitively - the county may
need that long and sometimes needs longer than that. It is not the same 45
days as the one that decides how old an employer statement may be when it is
submitted.

The rule behind all of these: **the number is not the mistake, the rule is.**
When a consumer quotes a figure back at you, the useful question is not "is
that right" but "which rule do you mean".

## Stage 6: Holding the numbers

Memory aids, each with a reason. An aid that is only a string of digits is
worthless, because the digits are not what fails.

- **Business days are the exception, not the rule.** Only four windows count in
  business days, and each sits on a complaint or an escalation clock.
  Everything else is calendar days. So when a figure arrives with the word
  business in it, it is on somebody else's clock, not the consumer's deadline.
- **One window runs from the end of the cover, not from the event.** The 30
  calendar days of reinstatement runs from the date the cover ended. The
  enrolment window runs from the event. Two clocks, two start points, and the
  difference between them is the whole answer.
- **The figure you read off the record rather than from memory.** The document
  period, the extension and the deadline showing on the case are what the
  consumer works to. The numbers in your head are how you check that the
  record is right.
- **The poverty line is picked by the person.** 138 for an adult, 266 for a
  child, 213 and 322 in a pregnancy. Never the household average.
- **Tiers are what the plan pays, and they are averages.** 60, 70 rising to 94,
  80, 90 - and the 80 to 85 per cent of premium is what insurers must spend on
  care.
- **A penalty figure has a body and a year, or it has nothing.** Then the
  estimator.
- **Ask which rule before you correct a figure.** The number is rarely what
  the consumer got wrong.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Special enrolment after a life event | 60 days from the event | published |
| Special enrolment after losing Medi-Cal | 90 days from the loss | published |
| Filing an appeal | 90 days from the mailing | published |
| Reporting a change | 30 days for Covered California, 10 for Medi-Cal | published |
| Ending cover | 14 days' notice | published, and advice rather than a rule |
| A complaint | 30 business days | published |

| An extension | only while the item is still open; the length is the training's and is not settled | the training's own |
| Reinstatement | 30 calendar days from the end date | the training's own |
