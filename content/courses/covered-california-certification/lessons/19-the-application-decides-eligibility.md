# The Application Decides: Citizenship, Household, Income, and Employer Coverage

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the emphasis the training placed on them.

## What you will be able to do

- Name the five inputs the application weighs before it returns an eligibility result.
- Decide who is asked about citizenship or immigration status, and who is not.
- Build the household from the tax return instead of from who lives in the house.
- Place a household income figure against the federal poverty level for the 2026 plan year.
- Apply both halves of the employer-coverage test and say what follows when it fails.
- Explain why the result belongs to the application and not to you.

## Step 1: The five things the application weighs

The application is a decision engine with five inputs. You supply answers; it supplies the result. The training ran through them in the same order every time.

1. **Immigration status.** Is the person a citizen, a national, or lawfully present? If this fails, nothing after it matters.
2. **Household membership.** The tax household sets the family size and decides whose income is counted.
3. **Household income.** The income for the year, measured as modified adjusted gross income, compared against the federal poverty level.
4. **Employer coverage.** Whether a job-based offer is affordable and also meets minimum value.
5. **Tax filing status.** Whether the applicant will file a return, or be claimed on someone else's.

> ★ **L19-1 — Ask the questions, take the answers, submit, and read the result back.** The training was emphatic that a representative does not decide eligibility. You may suspect a person will not qualify; you still enter what the consumer tells you, let the application work, and then explain what came back.

Two things deliberately sit outside this list. Health status is not a factor in financial eligibility: the factors the training named were household income, household size, the age of the household members, and where the household is located, which sets the pricing region. And immigration information is collected for one purpose only, which is deciding eligibility.

## Step 2: Citizenship and immigration status

**Who gets asked.** Only people who are applying for coverage are asked about citizenship or immigration status. A spouse or a tax dependent who appears on the application without applying is not asked, and you do not collect that information for them. The training's wording was that for anyone not applying for coverage, none of their information is required. The official record puts it the same way: only the people applying need to share a Social Security number or immigration information, and the rest of the household can still be listed.

**Where the answer comes from.** The application checks what the consumer answered against federal records held by the Social Security Administration and the Department of Homeland Security. Where that electronic check does not settle it, the consumer may be asked for a physical document. Either way the consumer supplies the answers; you do not research a status on their behalf.

**What the question looks like.** It is a drop-down for each person, and the person answers it. The options run from US citizenship and US national status through the categories of lawful presence. For documents, the training pointed to two lists: a primary document from one list, and a supporting document from the other.

**Two households, two labels.** This is the point the training returned to twice, so it is worth having straight:

- A **mixed immigration status family** is a household whose members do not all share the same immigration status. Such a family reports its actual size, and the system applies a special formula published by the Internal Revenue Service that recalculates household size and household income for the purpose of financial assistance. You do not perform that calculation yourself.
- A **mixed-program family** is the official name for a household where one member qualifies for Medi-Cal and another qualifies for Covered California. Different programmes, different results for different people. The training was careful that the two ideas are not confused, and the official site uses the mixed-program family wording.

> ★ **L19-2 — A person who is not lawfully present may still apply, and a family member who files jointly is still listed.** The training's point was that a person can apply and see whether anything is available, and that anyone in the same tax filing household stays on the application even when they are not applying for cover themselves.

**A change for 2027, not a rule for today.** The training noted that the list of qualifying immigration categories narrows for the **2027** plan year, with several categories described as going away. The official record marks every 2027 change as contingent on court decisions, so carry this as a change for 2027 and not as the rule in force for 2026.

**The question consumers actually ask.** Whether answering these questions will expose them. The training's answer was that receiving financial assistance generally does not make a person a public charge and does not by itself affect a path to permanent residence or naturalization. The two exceptions named were government-funded long-term institutional care, and giving false information on the application.

## Step 3: Who counts as household

> ★ **L19-3 — The household is the tax household.** The training's version: it does not matter who is physically living in the house. The question is who the person files taxes with.

A household usually contains the tax filer, their spouse where there is one, and the filer's tax dependents. The official record states it the same way, with one addition: a spouse or domestic partner, and anyone claimed as a tax dependency, are in the household whether or not they apply for insurance.

**What this decides.** Household size selects the poverty line, and therefore the assistance band the family lands in.

**Income, not just membership.** Every relevant household member's income goes on the application, including people who are not applying for cover. The training's example was a family of six where only one person was applying: because all six file together, all six incomes belong on the application. A household member listed as not applying is not asked for status information, and their income still counts.

**Why the training did not want you to work out the tax answer.** A tax filing question is a tax question, and the instruction was to refer uncertain tax questions to a tax professional rather than settle them on the call. Do not advise on filing status you are not certain of.

## Step 4: Income and the poverty line

**The measure.** Financial eligibility is worked out from modified adjusted gross income. The training described that as adjusted gross income with three categories added back: the exempt foreign earned income along with the foreign housing exclusion, the non-taxable Social Security benefits, and the interest that is tax-exempt and was earned or accrued in that year. The definitions come from the Internal Revenue Service rather than from the marketplace, so the marketplace is applying a rule it did not write.

**The estimate.** The application asks for an estimate of household income for the year, and the consumer's answer is what you enter. The training made the point that whatever shows on the tax return should show on the application. It also described the tolerance built into the estimate: an estimate that lands within ten percent of the actual figure is accepted without fuss. The illustration was a household expecting $50,000, where anything between $45,000 and $55,000 sits inside that window.

**The chart.** Household income is then compared with the federal poverty level for the household size, and the result lands the household in a band. The bands that came up in the training, for the 2026 plan year:

| Household member    | Income band                                  | Where it lands                                                                          |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Adult aged 19 to 64 | up to and including 138% of the poverty line | Medi-Cal                                                                                |
| Child under 19      | up to and including 266% of the poverty line | Medi-Cal                                                                                |
| Pregnant            | above 138% up to and including 213%          | Medi-Cal for pregnant individuals                                                       |
| Pregnant            | above 213% up to and including 322%          | Full-scope Medi-Cal, or else Covered California with premium assistance, but never both |

There are exceptions for children, for pregnant individuals, and for other special groups, which is why the chart is not a single line.

> ★ **L19-4 — A blank-looking gap on the chart is not a no.** The training's example was a household that looked as though it had fallen into an empty area and had still qualified for premium assistance. Read the boundaries of each band rather than reading the picture.

## Step 5: The tax-filing condition

To receive financial assistance through Covered California, the applicant has to agree to file a return for the year the assistance covers, or to be claimed as a dependent on someone else's return. That agreement is a condition of the assistance, not of the coverage itself. Someone who only wants Medi-Cal is not caught by it.

Two more conditions came with it:

- **Married consumers generally file jointly.** A couple who files separately usually cannot obtain financial assistance.
- **There are exceptions.** Domestic abuse and spousal abandonment were the situations the training named, where filing as single or as head of household may be available. These are exceptions to the general rule, and they are tax questions, so refer them.

**Worked example.** A consumer asks whether you can simply enter single on the application so that the household comes out smaller. The training's answer was to stay on one application and report the actual filing status, and let the household reflect the truth. It contrasted that with inventing a second application to game the size of the household.

## Step 6: Employer coverage and the two-part test

A job-based offer does not automatically close the door on Covered California. Two separate tests have to be applied, and the consequence follows from the pair.

**Test one, is the offer affordable.** The lowest-cost employee-only option must cost no more than a stated share of household income. The official figure in effect for the **2026** plan year is **9.96%**, and the official page warns that the percentage changes every year, so check the figure for the year you are working in. The training also displayed **10.22%** as the figure for the following year, **2027**, and used it in its own worked examples. That is exactly the trap: a percentage from the wrong year still produces a confident, wrong answer.

**Test two, does it meet minimum value.** The plan has to cover 60% or more of the total cost of medical services and has to cover hospital and doctor services adequately. A plan that meets this is the equivalent of a Bronze 60.

**Both tests, together.** When the offer is affordable and meets minimum value, the consumer is generally not eligible for financial assistance through Covered California. When either test fails, that restriction does not apply. The training was explicit that both have to be true before assistance is ruled out, and that a personal preference for one plan over another does not replace either test.

**The family is tested separately.** The employee-only offer and the family offer are two different calculations, compared against the same share of household income. Family coverage usually costs more, so it is common for the employee-only cover to be affordable while the family cover is not. The official record sets out the three outcomes:

| Employee-only cost | Family cost     | Who can get financial help   |
| ------------------ | --------------- | ---------------------------- |
| 9.96% or less      | 9.96% or less   | Nobody in the family         |
| 9.96% or less      | more than 9.96% | The family, not the employee |
| more than 9.96%    | more than 9.96% | The employee and the family  |

**What the consumer can still buy.** Cover at full price through Covered California stays available even when assistance is not. And the consequence of taking assistance that was not owed is not a small one: the training said a consumer who collects financial assistance they were not eligible for may have to give back some or all of it.

**Form 1095-A.** Covered California sends the consumer a Form 1095-A in January, either by post or as a message telling them to look in the inbox of their account. It shows proof of cover, the amount of federal premium assistance the consumer received during the year, and whether a state mandate penalty applied. A consumer on a catastrophic plan does not get one from Covered California; they may get Form 1095-B from the insurer instead, and if no tax form arrives at all they can still file.

**The catastrophic plan.** It is available to people under 30, and to people aged 30 and over who have filed an exemption for general hardship. The premium is low, but the plan pays nothing for most care until the out-of-pocket maximum is reached, so the cover is narrow and the exposure is large. For **2026** the out-of-pocket maximum is **$10,600** for self-only cover. Preventive services are covered, as are the opening three consultations with a doctor that are not preventive; everything else is paid at nothing until the maximum is met. No financial assistance is available on this plan. The training's worked example: a $12,000 bill leaves the consumer responsible for $10,600 and the insurer for $1,400.

## Step 7: Why the application decides

Three reasons, all of them practical.

**It is the only thing that holds the rules.** The determination is produced by the eligibility system working from the answers and using the current thresholds. The training's phrase was to let the eligibility results speak for themselves, and to stop pre-judging who will qualify. This is the same rule the course records as F4: never predetermine eligibility, even when you are nearly certain.

**A pre-judgement is a promise you cannot keep.** The training's example was a consumer who appeared unlikely to qualify on income and who turned out to qualify once the real figure was known. Guessing builds a promise that the results then break, and the consumer experiences that as being misled.

**You are still useful when the result is unwelcome.** When a result comes back showing a person is not eligible because of status, the training's answer was to explain the result plainly, say what the eligible family members qualify for, and point the person who is ineligible to the county for anything else they might get, or to their own insurer for private cover. You are not deciding; you are explaining a decision and routing the person to the next door.

> ★ **L19-5 — Asking a question is not a predetermination.** If you are unsure about a tax answer, a document, or what a result means, refer it to the right place rather than answering from memory. The training's instruction was consistent throughout: ask, submit, and explain the outcome.
