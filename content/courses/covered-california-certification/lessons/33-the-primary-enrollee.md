# The Primary Enrollee and the Rest of the Household

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the emphasis the training placed on them.

## What you will be able to do

- Say who the primary enrollee is on a case, and why that settles what every other caller may do.
- List what a spouse on the same case may do, and name the one thing they may not do.
- List what a non-primary adult enrollee may do, and what you must never tell them.
- Coach a caller who is not sure whether they are the primary.
- Separate what the recording settles from what the automatic transcription garbled.

## Step 1: One primary on every case

Every case is built around a single **primary enrollee** — the person the account belongs to. Everyone else in the household is attached to that person's case, and the training treated the role as a set of rights rather than a definition: the primary may act on the case freely, and the other people on it act on it more narrowly.

The training answered a question from the class by posting three knowledge articles into the chat and asking the room to hold on to them. Together they cover the three kinds of caller you meet:

| Who is on the line           | The training's summary                                                         |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| The primary                  | May act on the case without restriction                                        |
| A spouse on the same case    | Has the primary's powers, with one carve-out                                   |
| A non-primary adult enrollee | Has a short list of powers, and gets no personal information about the primary |

The third article is the one on non-primary adult enrolment. The transcript gives its reference number as 627; the number is the shaky part, the title is not.

★ **Whoever the case is built around is the primary, and everyone else is on their case.** That single fact settles both halves of this lesson: how much the caller is allowed to do, and how much you are allowed to tell them.

## Step 2: What a spouse on the same case may do

> ★ **A spouse has the primary's powers, with a single carve-out: a one-time verbal authorisation for a third party to speak on the primary's behalf.**

**The carve-out in one paragraph.** The training's worked example was the telephone being passed around. When the primary is on the line and asks to hand the phone to somebody else, that is allowed — the primary is the one giving the one-time authorisation. When the spouse makes the same request, it cannot be granted, because a spouse cannot hand on the primary's authority. The permission has to come from the primary's own mouth.

**The rest of the list.** Alongside the carve-out, the training named what a spouse may do:

- reset the account
- make changes to the case
- cancel the coverage
- add an authorized representative
- add a plan

**The official record says the same thing in a different role.** The privacy policy on the official site says consumers may delegate their application to an authorized representative, and lists what that delegation covers: filling out and submitting the application, making changes to cases, terminating cases, renewing plans, and selecting a plan. A spouse's list is therefore not a special favour. It is the ordinary set of case actions, reached through a relationship rather than through a delegation form.

## Step 3: The narrow rights of a non-primary adult enrollee

> ★ **A non-primary adult enrollee gets a short list of rights, and nothing at all about the primary's personal details.**

Who this is: people on the application who are there the way a dependent of the children on the application is there. The training's example was a 22-year-old daughter or son enrolled on the case.

**What they may do:**

- end their own coverage, and only their own
- provide documents
- get their own 1095-A

**What they may not do:**

- end anybody else's coverage. A 22-year-old cannot ring in and ask for her parents' coverage to be stopped.
- be given personal information about the primary. The training's example was the parents' social security number, and it is not yours to read out. The account's password and login details fall the same way, because they belong to the primary and not to the person on the line.

**Where the recording is unreliable.** The automatic transcription garbles the middle of this passage. It runs a line saying these enrollees cannot report a change and cannot view documents or get the 1095-A straight into a line saying they can get all of that, and the clause about resetting the consumer's password sits inside the same muddle. Trust the summary the training gave a minute later instead: they cannot be given any personal information about the primary. When you need the precise wording, read the knowledge article the class was given rather than the recording.

## Step 4: When the caller is not sure who the primary is

The training took two versions of the same call through the class.

**Scenario one — the caller knows they are the spouse.** She rings to add a plan for her partner. You open the case, and the name on the case tells you who the primary is, which is not her. That is not a problem; it only tells you whose details you need. You authenticate the **primary**: their first and last name and their date of birth. (How the call itself is authenticated is covered in the lesson on authenticating a caller; what belongs in this lesson is what the caller may do once you know who they are.)

**Scenario two — the caller believes they are the primary.** This is the one that catches people out.

> ★ **Do not tell a caller who is not the primary that they are not the primary. Ask them a question that lets them name the other person.**

The training's coaching was to ask whether somebody else might be the primary — could there be someone else who holds that role? is your spouse the primary, perhaps? — and then to contrast the two wordings and ask the room to hear the difference. A question hands the caller the chance to produce a name. A flat statement hands them nothing, and puts you in the position of contradicting a consumer who is sure of themselves.

If the caller cannot name anybody who might be the primary, they have failed authentication, however firmly they insist. The training's line for that was an apology, a plain statement that the account cannot be verified just now, and a suggestion that they sign in to their online account to see who the primary is on the case. If they have no online account, they have to work it out another way or come in and see someone; either way they need the primary's information.

## Step 5: Where the recording left the answer open

Two things in the session could not be read cleanly, and neither should be quoted as a rule:

- **The article number.** The transcript gives 627 for the non-primary adult article. The title is reliable; the number is not, and the article is better found by its title than by its number.
- **Changing the primary.** Asked at the top of the session, the answer was that a spouse may change the primary once the caller has been authenticated, which follows from a spouse having the primary's powers. Later, in the middle of the scenario walkthrough, a question about designating the primary drew a flat no. The wording of that second question is garbled, and the two answers do not sit together easily. The defensible position is the one the training's own summary supports: a spouse has the primary's powers, and you do not re-designate the primary on the case as part of a routine call. If a consumer needs the primary changed, check the mechanics against the live system before you promise it.

## Step 6: The three callers at a glance

|                                                                          | The primary | A spouse                                   | A non-primary adult enrollee          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| Everything the primary may do                                            | Yes         | Yes, apart from the one-time authorisation | No                                    |
| End their own coverage                                                   | Yes         | Yes                                        | Yes, and only their own               |
| End somebody else's coverage                                             | Yes         | Yes                                        | No                                    |
| Reset the account                                                        | Yes         | Yes                                        | No — the login belongs to the primary |
| Give a one-time authorisation for a third party to speak for the primary | Yes         | No                                         | No                                    |
| Be told personal information about the primary                           | Yes         | Yes                                        | No                                    |

## Review

Close the lesson and write down, from memory:

1. Who the primary enrollee is, and why every other person on the case is measured against them.
2. The single thing a spouse may not do, and the worked example that shows it.
3. The five actions the training said a spouse may take.
4. The three things a non-primary adult enrollee may do, and the two they may not.
5. Why you may not read a parent's social security number down the phone to an adult child on the case.
6. The difference between the two wordings the training contrasted when a caller thinks they are the primary.
7. What you say to a caller who cannot name anybody who might be the primary.
8. The two things the recording left open, and how you handle each.

Then check the two most likely to be missed: that the spouse's carve-out is about giving a one-time authorisation and nothing else, and that a non-primary adult enrollee can end their own coverage but not anybody else's.
