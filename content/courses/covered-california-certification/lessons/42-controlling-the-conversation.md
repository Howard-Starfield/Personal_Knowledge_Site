# Controlling the Conversation

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Say what the training means by call control, and name the goal that every call is aiming at.
- Set out what poorly controlled calls cost the caller, the service and you, and what good control buys each of you.
- Get the caller's issue stated early, and use probing questions when they cannot describe it precisely.
- Keep a caller with you through a long explanation, a silence, and an off-topic question.
- Choose between open-ended and closed-ended questions on purpose, and close a call with a closing statement.

## Step 1: What call control is for

Call control is guiding the conversation along a deliberate, consistent process of questioning so that the call arrives at the right answer. The training described it as deciding on a sound path and then following it: a plan of questioning that you keep to, rather than a set of improvised reactions to whatever the caller says next.

The goal of the call is a solution to the caller's problem, or an answer to the caller's question. That is the whole destination. Control is not about the representative sounding authoritative, and it is not about finishing quickly. It is about the caller leaving with something.

A useful analogy: a route with a destination. You know where the caller wants to get to, so you pick the road that gets them there. Control is what stops you taking a detour every time you pass something interesting on the way — and control is also what stops you from ignoring the detour the caller actually needs.

The training put the skill into four parts, and this lesson follows them: what happens when control is poor, what it buys when control is good, the strategies for steering a call, and a map of the call you can navigate by.

## Step 2: What poor control costs and good control buys

The training looked at this from three sides, because a badly handled call is expensive for everybody involved.

| Side               | What poor control costs                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | What good control buys                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| The caller         | More of their time, because the call runs long; a longer wait for a representative to become free; the impression that the representative and the service are unable or unwilling to help, which turns into anger, frustration and escalations; being left unsure what the answer was, or having to work harder to get one | Confidence in the answer; less frustration and anger; less time and effort wasted; more satisfaction with the call                                                       |
| The service        | Longer calls, so other callers wait longer; service levels that slip; callers who are angry; a bad impression of the representative that spreads to the organisation, its service and its products; escalations, and coverage that is lost altogether                                                                      | Service levels that hold; callers who are content; staff who are happier in their work                                                                                   |
| The representative | A call you no longer want to help with; a call that takes too long; an upset consumer to manage; escalation for the wrong reason, because the consumer thinks you are not confident in what you are telling them; less time between calls as the queue backs up                                                            | Looking capable and confident, with a good attitude; more confidence from the caller in what you say; calls that are more productive, more satisfying and more efficient |

Read the left-hand column as a chain. A call that is not steered drifts, and a call that drifts runs long, and a call that runs long makes the queue worse, and a worse queue makes the next caller angrier still. That is how one badly handled call turns into a service problem.

The training worked this through on a recorded call to a bank about a fee on a statement. The representative sounded bored, asked abrupt questions with a rude manner, never repeated or paraphrased what the caller had said, showed no thanks and no empathy, did not really listen, interrupted, assumed he already knew what the problem was, made the same mistake twice, tried to hand the caller to somebody in another department when that was not what she needed, and never opened the history on the account until the caller forced him to. The caller's complaint turned out to be a charge the representative had failed to waive the first time, and the note saying so was sitting on the record the whole time.

Every one of those is a control failure, and each of them had a cheap alternative: ask, then listen; check the record; paraphrase; give the caller options; do not interrupt.

## Step 3: Starting the call out right

The single biggest thing you can do for control is to get the caller's issue on the table early. Everything afterwards depends on knowing what you are solving.

- **Ask for the issue as soon as the call opens.** The sooner the problem is stated, the sooner you can aim at it.
- **Use probing questions when the explanation is vague.** Many callers cannot describe the problem in specific terms. Probing questions help them clarify it, and a clarified issue is a solvable issue.
- **Handle a talkative caller at a pause.** When somebody is going on and on, wait for a natural break and then interject with a short statement that names the next step — say that you need a couple of pieces of information first and that you will help with those, and then ask for the first one. It redirects without dismissing.
- **Manage the interaction when the question goes off topic.** Callers ask very open-ended, unrelated questions, about your day or the weather. Give a brief, friendly answer and then ask a pertinent question that moves the call forward.

None of these is about cutting the caller off. It is about replacing an unfocused conversation with a focused one that still reaches them.

## Step 4: Keeping the caller with you

A caller cannot see what you are doing, may not know how a call is supposed to run, and does not know what you need from them or why you need it. Silence on your side of the line reads as being ignored, so say what you are about to do and why.

When there is a pause while you work, you have two good ways to fill it:

- Paraphrase what the caller has just told you. It proves you were listening and it lets them correct you early.
- Say what you are doing and why you are doing it.

What you should not do is narrate everything on your screen as you work through it. The training called that a stream of consciousness and warned against it. It is dull, it is long, and it hands the caller a running commentary they did not ask for. Leave out negative remarks about the systems or about other departments while you are at it — the caller does not need your opinion of the equipment.

Three habits of active listening do a lot of the work:

- **Minimal encouragers.** Small interjections such as I see, okay and sure show that you are listening and encourage the speaker to keep going.
- **Repeat back, summarise and paraphrase.** Saying what you heard in your own words checks your understanding and shows the caller they were heard.
- **Use the caller's name.** It is a small thing that keeps a conversation human.

And then the questions. The training was careful that neither kind is better in the abstract; each has a job, and you pick by what the next step of the call needs.

| Kind of question | Usually answered with                                  | Use it to                                                                            |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Closed-ended     | Yes or no, or very few words                           | Direct the conversation, get specific information, confirm facts                     |
| Open-ended       | A longer answer, often beginning with what, why or how | Draw out more information, develop the conversation, find the issue or the situation |

Use both. An open question when you are still hunting for the problem; a closed one when you have found it and need it pinned down.

## Step 5: Tools, options and the close

The training named three tools, and using them is part of control rather than an optional extra:

- **Your ears.** Listen actively. What the caller tells you is itself an important tool.
- **What the caller tells you.** The information they give you is what you work from.
- **The record.** Any history on the consumer's case, and the knowledge articles you can search for in the service platform.

These keep the goal in front of you and let you solve the issue rather than guess at it.

When you have to deliver bad news, or a negotiation turns difficult, give the caller options. Options let the person process the information and move towards a solution in their own time. Hand over a single outcome with no choice and you get stress instead of movement.

Finally, close properly with a closing statement. It marks the end of the call and it carries three things: the issues the caller raised, the steps you have taken to resolve them, and a recap of the next steps still needed.

Underneath all of it sits the call-flow map, the training called it one of the most important tools for control. A map of the call you can walk through has five steps:

| Step  | What happens                               |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------ |
| One   | Greet the caller and work out who they are |
| Two   | Find out why they rang                     |
| Three | Say the problem back to them               |
| Four  | Work through the assistance                |
| Five  | Document the call, recap it and close      |

Mapping the call flow means deciding and writing down a well-thought-out, consistent process of questioning, so that you have a route rather than a reaction. It is what lets you notice, halfway through a call, that you have skipped a step.
