# The Shopping Tool, Eligibility, and Plan Types

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Move a consumer around the site's main sections and open the two local-help doors.
- Say what "eligible" actually means, which facts it turns on, and who decides it.
- Build the household the shopping tool asks for, including a dependent who lives elsewhere.
- Explain why the shopping tool's answer is an estimate and the application is the decision.
- Read a results page: the filters, the sort order, the provider filter's limit, and the plan names.
- Set out what a coverage level trades between the monthly premium and what care costs.
- Tell an HMO, a PPO and an EPO apart, and say why the best plan is not found by looking at price.

## Stage 1: The site, and the doors in it

Six headings run across the top of the site, and two buttons sit above them: sign in, and shop and compare (00:07:46–00:08:06).

| Heading                        | What a consumer finds there                               |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Coverage basics**            | The coverage levels, and help working out which one suits |
| **Eligibility and enrollment** | Who qualifies, and the steps to apply                     |
| **Costs and financial help**   | Premiums, deductibles, and the help that may be available |
| **Managing your account**      | An existing application, renewals, and changes            |
| **Apply now**                  | The start of an application                               |
| **Get help**                   | Contact routes and the local-help doors                   |

Two local-help doors are worth knowing by name before a consumer asks for a person.

**Find local help** takes a ZIP code and returns the local agent's phone number, street address, and first and last name (00:12:13–00:12:24). The training's point was about what the listing does _not_ give you: an agent's hours of operation, and the languages that agent speaks. The official enroller locator carries fields for languages spoken and office hours, including a "call for hours" fallback where none are published, so treat the listing as the start of the answer and confirm anything beyond contact details with the office itself (coveredca.com, find an enroller, retrieved 13 October 2026).

**The local Medi-Cal office** route opens the state health-care services site, where a consumer can find their county office's phone number (00:12:54–00:13:02).

> **Why it matters.** A consumer who wants a person is often really asking for
> one who speaks their language, or who is open at the hour they can call. If the
> listing cannot tell you that, say so plainly rather than sending the consumer
> to a number that may not help them.

## Stage 2: What eligibility means, and who decides it

Eligibility is not a verdict a representative hands the consumer. It is worked out when the application is processed, and it turns on facts about the household (00:11:05–00:11:21):

- the household itself, and how many people are in it
- income
- anybody the consumer claims as a tax dependent
- where the consumer lives — a California resident is the starting point
- citizenship or immigration status
- whether the consumer already has health insurance
- and other factors the application applies

The official record lists the same main factors: income, where you live, citizenship or immigration status, and current health insurance (coveredca.com, eligibility). Two things from it are worth using on a call:

- **One application, two programmes.** A single application screens the household for Covered California and for Medi-Cal at the same time and returns a result for each person. Different people in one household can qualify for different programmes — the official name for that is a **mixed-program family**, and it is normal rather than a mistake (coveredca.com, eligibility).
- **Only the people applying have to give their Social Security number and immigration information.** Other household members can still be listed (coveredca.com, eligibility).

> **The line to hold.** You can explain what the application will consider. You
> cannot tell a consumer what their result will be before the application has
> been processed — and the shopping tool does not change that.

## Stage 3: The tax household

The household in the shopping tool and in the application is the **tax household**, not the group of people sharing a kitchen. It is you, your spouse or domestic partner, and anybody you claim as a tax dependency.

The rule that catches people out: **a claimed dependent counts even when they do not live with you, and even when they are not applying for coverage.** A child living with their other parent, insured through that parent, and claimed by the consumer is still in the consumer's household for this purpose (00:23:47–00:24:01).

And the other half of it: **the person who claims the dependent is the head of the household and the primary on the application** (00:25:19–00:25:29). If the child's other parent claims the child instead, then the consumer is a household of one.

When parents take turns claiming a child from year to year, each of them has to update their own application every year to add or remove that dependent, and that change is made with Covered California (00:26:32–00:27:01).

> **The question to expect.** "I live with seven roommates — are we eight?"
> The answer is that the household follows the tax return, not the address.
> People who live together but claim nobody are each their own household.

## Stage 4: The shopping tool gives an estimate, not an application

Before the results appear, the tool asks for the coverage year, the ZIP code, the household's income, the household's size and the ages of its members, and which of them need coverage (00:27:40–00:28:29).

Then a warning appears, and it is the whole point of the tool: **this is not an application for coverage; it is a tool that gives an estimate.** The application is what returns the actual eligibility results (00:27:40–00:28:29).

That distinction has a consequence on the call. A consumer can find what looks like a $0 plan in the tool and still see different numbers once the application is processed, because the application's figures are the ones that count (00:29:19–00:29:52).

The official record gives the tool its name: **Shop and Compare**, described as the fastest way to see what you might pay, and as a personalised estimate rather than a decision. It can also check whether the household would qualify for Medi-Cal instead (coveredca.com, coverage basics).

> **The sentence that saves the call.** "This shows you an estimate. To find
> out what you actually qualify for and what you would actually pay, we need to
> submit the application."

## Stage 5: Reading, filtering and sorting the results

**The filters.** Two preference filters sit on the results page: a lower monthly premium with higher costs when care is used, or a higher premium with lower costs when care is used. A consumer does not have to answer either (00:30:09–00:30:33).

**The provider filter, and its limit.** A consumer can type in a provider or hospital to see the plans that include them. The training was explicit that this filter is not something to rely on (00:31:42–00:32:01), and it gave the reason: providers move in and out of network at their own pace, and the site's list does not update the moment they do (00:48:54–00:50:03). A provider who does not come up is not necessarily out of network, and one who does come up is not proof either. The advice to the consumer is to ring the provider and confirm before choosing the plan (00:49:56–00:50:12).

**Sorting.** The sort order can be changed — for a consumer chasing the smallest monthly payment, the sort is changed from lowest estimated yearly cost to lowest premium.

**Volume.** The session's walkthrough returned 36 plans for one ZIP code; narrowing by provider brought that to 15, and then to eight or nine. Up to three plans can be compared side by side (00:37:50–00:37:57; 01:01:23–01:01:36).

**Networks are local.** The same walkthrough showed Cal Optima in one ZIP code and LA Care in another, with Cal Optima absent from the second (01:24:42–01:24:56). A plan being available in the state is not the same as a plan being available where the consumer lives.

**What the plan names mean.** Bronze 60, Silver 70, Silver 87, Gold 80, Platinum 90, and the minimum coverage plan (00:33:50–00:34:05; 01:03:04–01:03:21). The official record adds the piece that catches people: Silver is a **range**, 70% to 94% of average annual cost, because enhanced Silver plans exist for households that qualify on income and size (coveredca.com, coverage levels).

## Stage 6: Premium against cost sharing

Bronze carries the lowest monthly payment; Platinum carries the highest monthly payment and the lowest cost when care is used (00:13:02–00:13:17). That is the trade in one line: **the lower the monthly premium, the more is paid when care is received.**

The minimum coverage plan sits at the cheapest end of the monthly premium, and the consumer pays out of pocket for everything until the deductible is met — the training's phrase for how that feels is having insurance but not really having it (00:35:37–00:36:12; 01:09:39–01:10:03).

The walkthrough gave a worked example worth keeping. A Silver plan showed lower copayments than a Gold plan and cost less each month — and then the consumer saw the deductible. The Silver plan had one and the Gold plan did not, and "I do not want a deductible" was a deal breaker (01:03:47–01:04:33). The lesson drawn was that deductibles can be deal breakers, and that the comparison narrows the field rather than settling it (01:06:53–01:07:10).

There is also a control on the coverage levels page that walks a consumer from one level to another, and it is worth putting a consumer in front of it (00:16:46; 00:17:23–00:17:28).

> **What this stage is for.** Not choosing for the consumer. Getting the
> consumer to say what their care looks like, so the comparison is theirs.

## Stage 7: HMO, PPO and EPO, and the question price cannot answer

| Plan type | What it does about providers and referrals                                                                              | Where it sits on premium               |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| **HMO**   | Only covers in-network care, except emergencies. A referral from the primary care doctor is needed to see a specialist. | Usually the lowest monthly premium     |
| **PPO**   | Doctors may be seen in or out of the network. No referral is needed to see a specialist.                                | Higher monthly premium than HMO or EPO |
| **EPO**   | Only covers in-network care, except emergencies — but no referral is needed to see a specialist.                        | Costs usually fall between HMO and PPO |

Those three rows are the official plan-type cards (coveredca.com, coverage levels). The training built the same picture from the consumer's side: an HMO is restricted to in-network providers that accept the plan, and the primary care doctor is the way in (00:14:10–00:14:25; 00:40:27–00:41:30; 00:41:47–00:43:01). The sharpest consequence of that restriction: if the consumer's own doctor does not accept the HMO, the consumer is out of network and the care is not covered (00:41:06–00:41:30).

The training's image for an EPO was a child of an HMO and a PPO — it has the characteristics of both (00:45:47–00:46:08). The part that separates an EPO from an HMO is the referral, not the network.

Two hedges the training put on the PPO row: some provider offices still ask for a referral even when the plan does not require one, and it is the office that decides how it handles that — the consumer's provider has to check with the insurance company (00:43:47–00:45:17). And emergencies are treated differently from ordinary out-of-network care: with an HMO, emergency care out of network may still be covered, depending on the plan and on what the emergency was (00:46:56–00:47:26).

**"Which plan is best?"** is the question the consumer will ask, and it is not a price question. The training's answer is to ask the consumer how they expect to use their coverage — how often, whether they see specialists often, whether they pick up a lot of prescription drugs, or whether they see their doctor about once a year for a routine checkup (00:16:21–00:16:44; 00:37:32–00:37:50).

The answer can differ inside one household. One spouse with health complications may be better served by a Gold plan while a healthier spouse takes a lower-premium level (01:05:58–01:06:18). The official record says it as plainly as it can be said: there is no single right choice (coveredca.com, coverage levels).

> **Where the line is.** Narrowing is the work; recommending is not. Two
> households with the same income can correctly end up on different plans, and
> the tool cannot tell you which one this consumer should buy.

## Stage 8: Where the shopping tool stops

- **It does not decide eligibility.** The application does. The tool estimates.
- **It does not price a specific procedure for a specific person.** Plan design
  numbers can be read off the comparison; what a consumer will actually pay for
  a given surgery is a question for the insurance company.
- **A retroactive start or end date is not a tool setting.** The training's
  condition for changing one is a provable error by Covered California; without
  that, the representative goes to the next level of support for a second
  opinion, and then tells the consumer the earliest date available (01:14:26–01:15:49).
- **The enrolment date decides the start date.** Enrolling before the end of
  31 October starts coverage on 1 November; after that the start date becomes
  1 December, which leaves a gap in cover (01:13:36–01:14:00).

   Spanish. What can the local-help listing give you, and what can it not?
