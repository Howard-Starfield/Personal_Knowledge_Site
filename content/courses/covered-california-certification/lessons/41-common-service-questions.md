# Common Service Questions Before the Midterm

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Tell a premium, a copayment, coinsurance and a deductible apart, and say which of them a cost sharing reduction does not lower.
- Say what the public site shows a consumer, what it does not cover, and how old a dependent child has to be before a parent's plan drops them.
- Open a call the way the training expects, put a caller on hold without losing them, and run a call that uses an interpreter.
- Decide when to file an application even though the consumer has no qualifying event, and explain what the result might tell them.
- Find the answer in the right place: the consumer's case record, the knowledge articles, or a quick sort to the county.

> **Where this lesson comes from.** This lesson is drawn from the team working through the **midterm review** out loud — a question game run between the training sessions, not a delivered lesson. Two things follow from that. The review and the game word the same point differently, so a question here can read oddly for a reason that has nothing to do with the rule. And where the team judged an item badly worded and said so, this lesson marks what the team keyed instead of presenting it as a rule you can quote. Where this lesson and the official record differ, the official record is what a consumer relies on.

## Step 1: The words for money

Four words get tangled on nearly every call, so pin them down before anything else.

| Word        | What it actually is                                                                                                              |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Premium     | The amount paid each month to keep a plan in force.                                                                              |
| Copayment   | A flat dollar figure paid for a covered service.                                                                                 |
| Coinsurance | The percentage paid on a covered bill once the deductible is met.                                                                |
| Deductible  | What is paid out of pocket before the plan starts sharing the cost, and the figure that rolls up into the out-of-pocket maximum. |

An analogy that holds: the premium is the rent on the room, the deductible is the part of the bill you cover before the company starts helping, the copayment is a flat ticket price for one visit, and coinsurance is your share of whatever is left over.

A cost sharing reduction applies to Silver plans and depends on where the household sits against the federal poverty level. It lowers what you spend when you actually use care — the deductible, copayments and coinsurance. The monthly premium is the piece of the money picture it does not lower.

> The training reviewed a question in exactly this area and then said the wording was poor and that the item was unlikely to appear. The reduction depends on the Silver variant a household gets: a Silver 73 carries a different premium from a Silver 70. Treat "the monthly premium" as the answer the review keyed, not as a rule you can quote without the caveat.

The four metal tiers differ along two axes at once: what you pay each month, and how much of a bill you carry when you use care.

| Metal tier | Share of the average annual cost the plan usually covers |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Bronze     | 60%                                                      |
| Silver     | 70% to 94%, depending on the household                   |
| Gold       | 80%                                                      |
| Platinum   | 90%                                                      |

A Bronze plan keeps the monthly figure low and leaves more of each bill with you; a Platinum plan does the reverse. Preventive care — an annual check-up or a recommended screening — is free on every tier. The minimum coverage plan sits outside those four metal tiers: the training described it as the catastrophic option, giving a household without financial help three covered non-preventive visits, after which the member pays the full price of every visit until they reach the out-of-pocket maximum.

## Step 2: What the site shows and who can be covered

The public site is published in two languages, English and Spanish, while phone service is offered in a good deal more than that. A consumer can also drop an email address into the homepage to sign up for deadline reminders and other alerts, so keep that answer ready for anyone who asks how they will not miss a date.

Essential health benefits are the set of benefits the Affordable Care Act decides are necessary, and every plan carries the same set. Two points catch people out:

- Adult dental and vision are **not** part of that set. They can be bought separately, with their own premium.
- Paediatric dental and vision **are** part of it, and ride along with the health plan for children. The training drew the line at the ages of zero to eighteen; a young adult who wants dental coverage buys their own plan.

Who can ride on a plan is a separate question from what the plan covers. A dependent child can stay on a parent's plan up to the age of twenty-six. Past that age the child has to have a disability — and proving that disability is the child's job with the insurance company, not yours with the service centre. Your job is to record what they say, not to interrogate them or demand proof.

Plan type decides how you get care once you are enrolled:

| Plan type | Referral to a specialist | Network                                                                       |
| --------- | ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| HMO       | Required                 | In network only, apart from emergencies; usually the lowest monthly premium   |
| PPO       | Not required             | In or out of network; a higher monthly premium                                |
| EPO       | Not required             | In network only, apart from emergencies; usually between HMO and PPO on price |

When a household lands on more than one programme at once, the official record calls it a mixed-program family: one parent may qualify for a Covered California plan with financial help while a child qualifies for Medi-Cal instead. One application checks eligibility for both programmes, so there is a single set of results to explain. If a consumer wants a child on a Covered California plan instead of Medi-Cal, that is a decision the family makes directly with the county — you are not the one who declines Medi-Cal for them, and you do not speak for the county.

One application also means a single result set per person. When you quote what somebody might qualify for, four things decide it: income, the ages of the people in the household, the size of the household, and where they live.

## Step 3: Starting the call and asking for a hold

There is no fixed opening script to read word for word, and the training was clear that the closing is the same — you choose your own wording. What is required is the content of the greeting:

- Thank the caller for calling Covered California.
- Name Covered California, so they know who they have reached.
- Give your own name, or a nickname if you prefer one.

If you use a nickname, use the same one every time. A caller who hears a different name from you on the next call will reasonably conclude they have reached a different person, and the record stops making sense.

A useful working habit, which the training described as one way experienced representatives open: get an idea of why the caller rang before you pull up anything. A sentence or two of context first, then the identification you need to open the record.

Putting a caller on hold has one approved shape: ask whether it is all right to do it, say what you are about to do and why, and tell them you will be back. Asking without giving a reason is halfway to acceptable, and simply telling someone to wait is not acceptable at all.

> The training also noted that on the assessment itself it had said the shorter hold phrasings would be accepted, while on real calls the reason still has to be given. Treat the full version — permission, reason, and a promise to return — as the one to use.

When an interpreter is on the line, the arrangement is simple and easy to get wrong:

- Speak to the consumer, not to the interpreter, and let the interpreter do the interpreting in both directions.
- Do not ask the interpreter to relay a message to the consumer.
- Do not use health insurance jargon. Interpreters are fluent in two languages; they are not specialists in the vocabulary of this programme.
- Speak loudly enough to be picked up, but without shouting.
- Give the interpreter the time to interpret.
- Do not hand the call to a bilingual colleague. You use an interpreter.

## Step 4: When there is no qualifying event

A qualifying event — marriage, a birth, adoption, foster placement, a move, losing other coverage — opens a window for changing a plan outside the annual enrolment period. Plenty of callers ring with no event at all, and the training was unambiguous about what to do: submit the application anyway and let the results speak.

You are not saying the consumer qualifies, and you are not saying they do not. You are completing the application so that the eligibility system does the deciding. The results can land the household in Medi-Cal, in Medi-Cal for Pregnant Individuals, in the Medi-Cal Access Program, or in a Covered California plan with or without financial help. Any of those is a real answer to somebody who rang with no event, which is why the application is worth filing.

Two situations get confused with events and should not be:

- **Pregnancy on its own is not the event. The birth is.** A consumer who is pregnant but has not given birth cannot be enrolled through a special-enrolment period on that basis. Still file the application, because the household may qualify for one of the pregnancy programmes instead.
- **Becoming a citizen is an update to an application, not an event.** Gaining lawful presence is what opens a window for somebody who is not already enrolled.

A worked example: a mother qualifies for a Covered California plan with help, and her son qualifies for Medi-Cal, because household size and income land them differently. That is a mixed-program family, and the single application produces a result for each of them.

## Step 5: The systems behind the answer

Three places hold what you need, and confusing them wastes a call.

- **The consumer's case record** holds the account history and what has already happened.
- **The knowledge articles** hold the policy and the task guides you are expected to work from.
- **A quick sort** sends the consumer to their county when the answer is really a county answer.

A quick sort is not a last resort. If you cannot find a consumer in the system, or you cannot be sure there is no existing application, sorting to the county first is a legal requirement rather than a courtesy. When you do refer a consumer to their county, give them the county's number from the Medi-Cal information on the site — never the number from the quick sort pop-up, and never a number you have taken on trust from a previous caller.

Two more pieces of timing that come up repeatedly:

- **Authentication happens in the eligibility system, not in the case system.** You do not authenticate a consumer in the sales platform; the case system is where you document the call and find the articles.
- **The reasonable opportunity period is ninety-five days, and the service centre calls it ROP.** A consumer who has been asked for documents has that long to send them, and it can be extended by up to sixty days more as long as the deadline has not already passed. Once it has passed, there is no extension. Extending the timeframe does not give the period a new name: it is still a reasonable opportunity period. A later training session splits the base figure — 95 days for most documents, and 155 days for proof of income (session 2026-10-22 07-56-48) — and the official record publishes no number of days at all, so these are the training's own figures and the deadline is read off the record rather than from memory. Lesson 53 sets out the whole rule, including what happens when the period runs out.

A caller who asks to end their coverage early is not asking about mid-month termination, which is not on the table. The choices are three month-ends: the end of the current month, then the end of the month after it, and the end of the month after that. If a consumer raises mid-month termination anyway, two things have to be true before you escalate it: the carrier has to agree first, and the consumer has to understand that even if the carrier agrees, nobody is obliged to refund the premium for the part of the month they are leaving.
