# The Renewal Call

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the instructor's own emphasis in the training.

## What you will be able to do

- Work a renewal call in the order the training worked it, and say which step unlocks which.
- Explain to a consumer why a mid-year income increase raised their premium, and what happens to the subsidy they have already used.
- Say why you cannot quote a January premium until the renewal application has been submitted end to end.
- Compare plans with a consumer, and say which deadline decides and why there is no backdating.
- Warn a consumer about the auto-renewal, and about auto-pay not following the new price.
- Walk a consumer through account creation, and say what is actually invalid when an access code keeps failing.
- Recognise a special enrollment period on a renewal call, and hand a Medi-Cal case to the county with the right instruction.
- Leave a record that shows what the consumer asked, what you did, and what you left for them.

## Step 1: The renewal call is a sequence, not a topic

A renewal call is not one question with one answer. The training's longest session — nearly four and a half hours — carried one call through an income change, a renewal application, a plan comparison, an account-creation problem, a special-enrollment question and a county hand-off, and the representative finished it inside a single conversation.

The order is the thing to learn. Each step unlocks the next, and a representative who does them out of order has to start again.

1. **Confirm the change and which way it went.** Income up, or income down.
2. **Explain the money.** What the change does to the subsidy and to the premium.
3. **Submit the renewal application whole**, because the January figure does not exist until you do.
4. **Read the new price back**, and compare plans against it.
5. **Check the deadlines** — the auto-renewal, the change cut-off, and the carrier's own auto-pay.
6. **Offer the help the consumer did not ask for** — account access, dental and vision, the special-enrollment window, the county.

> ★ **L45-1 — One call, not one topic.** The training's judgement on a good renewal call was that the consumer came to submit an application, and the representative made sure they were taken care of within that one call: account access, a plan, the mixed-household explanation, and the deadline for a 1 November start. The reason given was that it minimises the chance of the consumer ringing back the next day for something that could have been handled on the first call.

## Step 2: When income goes up, the subsidy goes down

The consumer in the training's worked example had been paying **$285.83** a month. After they reported a higher income on the application, the premium became **$472.61** from 1 November. Nothing had gone wrong with their plan and no penalty had been applied. This is what the subsidy system does.

**The mechanism.** When you report a higher income on the application, the tax credits you are eligible for through the Affordable Care Act are adjusted down. Less subsidy, and more of the premium comes out of the consumer's own pocket.

**Why the number jumps so hard.** The application works out the subsidy already received for the year up to October, and the subsidy left for the remaining months of the year on the new income. Whatever subsidy is left is spread over the months that remain. So one month of higher income changes every remaining month, and it bites hardest in the first months, because a whole year's worth of subsidy has to be recovered.

**Why the figure looks wrong to the consumer.** The training's consumer said the obvious thing: the figure on the application is not what they take home. The representative's job was to explain the mechanism, not to apologise for the number. The number is gross income, and the premium now reflects it.

**The other side of the same rule.** If the income goes down, the consumer may receive more subsidy, and the premium moves the other way.

**Why honest reporting matters.** The training put it plainly: if you underreport your income and receive more subsidy than you are eligible for, the government will find out at tax time, and you will have to pay back what was overpaid.

**Self-employment.** Fluctuating income is hard to report. The training's advice was that people who are self-employed look at last year's numbers and see whether they are on track for the same. The representative confirmed the consumer had made the adjustment correctly, and then showed them what it had done to the current payment.

> ★ **L45-2 — The subsidy moves against the income.** The training's summary was that an increase in income means you are more likely to receive less tax subsidy applied to your monthly premium, so the amount you pay each month may go up, and the reverse holds when the income falls.

## Step 3: A change is not a change until the whole application is submitted

This is the most consequential habit in the session, and it is a small one.

The training's instruction: a change does not fully take until the entire application is submitted. Take it right through to the very end.

A representative who edits the income on the application and then stops has changed nothing. The consumer goes away believing they have reported the change, and the price on the record is still the old one.

**Worked example.** A consumer asked whether they could go back in and change the income again when a piece of work ended in early December. The answer was yes — and the point was made at the same time: the application is not changed in full until the whole thing has been sent.

## Step 4: You cannot see January until the renewal application is in

The consumer asked what looks like a detail and is not: what will I pay in January?

The answer was that you still need to do the renewal application for 2027. Without that submitted, the January figure cannot be shown, because the renewal is what applies the new year's subsidy and the new premium.

**Why the order matters.** Doing the renewal application does three things at once:

1. It puts the new year's income in front of the subsidy calculation.
2. It produces the January premium, with the new subsidy applied and any premium increase taken into account.
3. It opens the comparison, because the consumer can look at other plans to change from 1 January.

**The order in the training's call.** The representative did not quote a January price. She asked permission, did the renewal application, and only then read the result back: the Health Net bronze plan on a better PPO, a base price of **$516.26** a month for the current year, **$43.65** of remaining tax credit applied towards it, and **$475.03** a month from 1 January. The consumer then had a real number to set other plans against.

**A trap inside the trap.** The current premium was $472.61 and the January premium was $475.03. That is not the same premium, and it is not a price cut — it is a different year's subsidy on a different year's price. The training pointed out that the November and December increase does not mean the number stays where it is going into January.

## Step 5: Comparing plans on the call

**How many at once.** The comparison screen lines up to **three** plans at a time. The button sits in the bottom corner of the screen, and it sets them out side by side.

**What to line up first.** The monthly premium and the deductible. The training's worked comparison: the gold plan has no deductible, because it is a higher level than silver; the silver tier carries a marginally smaller deductible than the bronze tier.

**Where the real cost hides.** The training's advice was to scroll down to the cost of services section and open the drop arrows, so the comparison is not three premiums side by side. On a bronze plan the consumer pays full price out of pocket for every service until they have reached the deductible — **$7,800** a year in the training's example — and if a doctor charges **$100** to be seen, that is what they pay, every time. The next level of bronze in the same example was **$266.60** a month, **$12** more, with set copays: **$60** for a primary care visit and **$20** for generic prescriptions, and a deductible a little lower at **$5,800** a year for medical and **$454** for prescriptions.

**The deadline that decides.** Make the selection by **31 October** and coverage begins 1 November. If the consumer waits until 1 November, coverage does not start until December, because there is no backdating. And you can accept and pick a plan before coverage starts, because the coverage does not begin until 1 November anyway.

**The carrier's own clock.** Whatever insurer the consumer chooses, it takes **10 days** for that carrier's system to update the enrolment. It is not instantaneous.

**Dental and vision.** Dental enrolment needs an active health plan first — you cannot add dental without one. Vision is not enrolled through the exchange at all: there are two companies the consumer has to enrol with directly.

## Step 6: The deadline, the auto-renewal and the auto-pay

**Auto-renewal.** The system has an auto-renewal mode, so what the consumer is currently enrolled in is renewed for January. The consumer does not have to make a decision that day, and the training said so plainly. If they want to change it, they have until **31 December**, and the new coverage starts 1 January.

**The auto-pay trap, which is the sharpest one in the session.** Auto-pay with the carrier does not adjust itself to the new price. If the consumer has auto-pay set up, you have to tell them to let the carrier know about the price change, because the amount being taken will not move on its own. If they do not, the payment stays at the old figure, they underpay the new premium, and the carrier has grounds to cancel the coverage for non-payment.

That is why the training raises it every time: it surprises people.

> ★ **L45-3 — Auto-pay does not follow the premium.** The training's instruction was that you need to let the carrier know about the price change because it does not automatically adjust to the new price, and the consequence of not doing so is an underpayment and a carrier cancellation for non-payment.

## Step 7: When the renewal call turns into an account-access call

A renewal call very often becomes an account-access call, because the consumer wants to see the renewal on the screen.

**First check: does the consumer already have a username?** If they do, the view access code option disappears from the case, and no access code can be generated. That is the first thing to check, and it can save the whole conversation.

**If they do not have one, generate a code and read it out.** Read each character out and name it, so the consumer can hear which one you mean — the training's example ran through a six, then a lowercase N like Nancy, a lowercase I like Igloo, a capital S like Sam, a lowercase D like David and a lowercase H like Henry. The reason is that a lowercase L and a capital I can look the same. If you cannot tell them apart, paste the code into a document or a spreadsheet and look at the actual character. And if you lose the code or misread it, generate a new one; that is not a problem.

**The step-by-step walk.** The consumer walks through it beside you, one screen at a time, because the pages look self-explanatory and are not.

1. Go to coveredca.com, sign in, and create an account.
2. On the account-creation page there are three routes on offer, and the consumer picks the one that links an existing application rather than starting something brand new or asking for their own information.
3. Choose yes to having an access code, and give them the code.
4. Select next. The next page asks for the consumer's personal details — given name, family name, date of birth — and those have to be entered exactly as they are on the application.
5. The page also offers a social security number field, and the consumer has to **skip** it.
6. Choose **mail** for the contact method, not email and not text.

**Why those two instructions matter.** Entering the social security number makes the system say the consumer already has an online account, because the system can create duplicate accounts and duplicate CalHEERS cases. The same pop-up appears if an email address or a text number tied to a different application is chosen instead of mail. The consumer then asks, in effect, what is my account — and the training noted that this had just happened to a caller.

**Plan B, and why the training did not take it.** The alternative the representative used was to give the consumer the case number, and it worked. The training's preference was the opposite order: guide the consumer step by step instead of skipping over the steps, because a consumer who follows the steps creates the account successfully the first time.

**When the code keeps failing.** It is not your mistake. The representative does not misspeak and does not give the wrong code. What is invalid is how the consumer is filling in the second page — the personal information. They have to enter their personal details — given name, family name, date of birth — exactly as in the application.

## Step 8: Special enrollment inside a renewal call

A renewal call can carry a qualifying life event, and then a second deadline appears.

**The training's example.** A consumer's other coverage, a COBRA plan, was ending 31 October 2026, so the consumer was losing health coverage. The application asked whether the household had lost coverage because of a strike or lockout, and the answer was no.

**The window.** With coverage ending 31 October 2026, the special enrollment period ran to **30 December 2026**. What that means in practice is that if the consumer goes ahead and chooses a plan, they can change their mind and change the plan any time up to that date.

**The other things the application asks.** The renewal application carries a question about whether the consumer has had a qualifying life event — recently moved, had a baby, got married, lost coverage because they lost their job, and the like — and a separate question about whether the reporting changes also apply to 2026, which is the consumer's own choice to answer.

## Step 9: The county hand-off

A renewal call can run into Medi-Cal, and Medi-Cal belongs to the county.

**The boundary.** Medi-Cal cases are handled by the county. The representative does not have access to the county system; the county has access to ours. The training stated it in one line: I do not have access to their system.

**What the representative can still do.** There may be a request for proof of income on the Covered California side — in the training's example, to be uploaded by **29 January 2027** — and the consumer needs the account they have just created to upload it. The path is:

1. On the home screen, scroll down to the household summary.
2. Click the red corner, and then the link that says view actions needed and alerts, under everyone's name.
3. Click upload eligibility document. That page gives the steps and a list of the documents that are acceptable.
4. If the consumer has no income document, there is a hyperlink at the bottom of that list to a **self-attestation form**, which counts as a legal document in its own right — the consumer's own statement of what they make.

**Then hand off.** Once the document is uploaded, the training recommended the consumer call the county to check whether they want anything else, and the representative gave the county office number — the San Joaquin County office on 209-468-1000 in the training's example. The consumer calls the county. The representative does not.

**The second hand-off.** The consumer in the training's example also had a CalFresh reapplication. That goes to the county as well, and the representative gave the county's number for it.

## Step 10: One call, and the record you leave

**The habit.** On a renewal call the representative offers the extra things rather than waiting to be asked: online account access, adding a plan, the mixed-household explanation, the deadline for a 1 November start. The training called that a one-call resolution and gave the reason — it minimises the chance of the consumer calling back to ask for help with online account access after an application has already been submitted.

**And the pace.** A renewal call is allowed to be long. The training's coaching was that a long call during the renewal period is a normal case, that a supervisor may message around the 45-minute mark to check everything is all right, and that the answer to that message is that you are fine and it is a long renewal. What a supervisor does not do is tell you to wrap it up. The instruction was simple: you do not hurry the consumer, and you give them whatever help you can.

**Your notes.** The training's advice was to write the notes somewhere else first — Word or OneNote — and then copy and paste them into the record, because a crash while typing straight into the record loses the note. Sticky notes were called out as not an approved resource. OneNote was the trainer's own choice, partly because it keeps going and partly because it holds folders and notebooks.

**After-call work.** Three minutes is the target, and it has to be under four. If you are in after-call work too long, a supervisor may message asking whether everything is all right or why you are not available.

**The last thing on the call.** Give the consumer the reference number for the call. It is what takes them back to the case if the line drops, and it is what they will quote if they call back.
