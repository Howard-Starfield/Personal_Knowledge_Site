# Vision, Dental, Cost-Sharing Reductions, and Minimum Coverage

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the emphasis the training placed on them.

## What you will be able to do

- Say what is already inside a child's plan for dental and vision, and what an adult has to buy separately.
- Explain why no financial help attaches to dental or vision, whatever the household's income.
- Name the three silver levels that reduce cost sharing, say who qualifies for each, and say who decides.
- Describe the minimum coverage plan: who may buy it, what it covers, and what it does not.
- Read a drug tier and say who owns the answer when a consumer asks what their prescription will cost.

## Step 1: The three words you need to read a chart

Before any of the numbers make sense, three words. The training used a slide of cost sharing to teach them `[00:09:43]`.

| Word            | What it is                                                   | The training's example                                                   |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Copay**       | A flat amount you pay for a service                          | A plan may set a flat copay of $50 for a primary care visit `[00:10:28]` |
| **Coinsurance** | A percentage of what the service costs                       | 15% coinsurance on a $300 visit means the member pays $45 `[00:11:13]`   |
| **Deductible**  | What you pay before the plan starts paying for most services | It sits above the office-visit copay on bronze `[00:13:36]`              |

A plan design is either copay-based or coinsurance-based, and which one a consumer has shows up in the **summary of benefits and coverage** — which is where the official record tells you to look for cost sharing on covered services `[00:09:58]`.

**The tier decides the price, not the coverage.** Every Covered California health plan and every metal tier offers the same essential health benefits `[00:07:49]`. A bronze plan does not cover something a platinum plan leaves out. What changes between the tiers is the monthly premium against what you pay when you use the plan.

**One tier has a visit structure.** On bronze, a member gets three visits at a set copay before the medical deductible begins to bite `[00:12:54]`. Those three can be a visit with a primary care doctor, a visit with another practitioner, a mental health visit or a specialist visit — any mix you like, but three visits in total `[00:13:03]`. On silver, gold and platinum, office visits carry a copay and there is no deductible on them `[00:13:20]`. And on bronze or on a minimum coverage plan, the member must clear the deductible or reach the out-of-pocket limit before the plan pays for X-rays and emergency care `[00:13:36]`.

**The caution the training put on the slide itself `[00:12:14]`.** These figures move over time, and what the slide showed may not be what is actually on offer for 2026. So every figure in this lesson carries the year it belongs to, and when the training and the site disagree you are told which is which.

The fuller treatment of deductibles, copays, coinsurance and the annual out-of-pocket maximum sits in the plan-benefits lesson. Here they are only the vocabulary for the charts.

## Step 2: Children's dental and vision come with the plan

★ **Children aged 0 to 18 already have dental and vision inside the health plan.** The training made this the subject of two knowledge checks `[00:05:37]`, `[00:05:46]`.

The official record adds the detail:

- **Dental.** Children under 19 get dental at no extra cost through their health plan. Cleanings, check-ups and X-rays are free. For fillings, root canals and crowns, the member splits the cost with the dental company, up to **$350 where there is one child**, or **$700 where there are two or more**.
- **Vision.** Children's vision covers eye check-ups and one pair of glasses a year, or contact lenses instead, and no deductible applies to children's vision benefits.

**The limit that matters more than any of it: no financial help.** There is no financial assistance and no premium tax credit associated with dental or vision, whatever the household earns `[00:07:14]`. The training tested this twice, and the official record says it plainly for dental: financial help does not apply, so you pay the full monthly cost.

**If the family would rather not have the embedded cover**, they can buy their own separate dental or vision cover instead `[00:06:12]`. That is entirely allowed — it simply carries its own premium, its own deductible and its own out-of-pocket maximum, and nothing in it is subsidised.

**The shape of the answer, in one line.** Children get dental and vision inside the plan at no extra cost and with no financial help attached; adults buy their own from the open market, also with no financial help attached.

## Step 3: Adult vision and dental are bought separately

**Vision.** Covered California has brought two companies on board to make vision coverage available to members `[00:03:03]`, and the training's job description is short: you do not enrol an adult in vision, you point them at the company `[00:04:30]`. If neither of the two appeals, the consumer can go to a vision company of their own choosing `[00:04:01]`.

Four facts that change what you say to a consumer:

1. **Enrolment happens with the vision company itself**, not through Covered California `[00:03:10]`.
2. **A vision plan is not a qualified health plan** `[00:03:17]`. It is not part of the health coverage, and it does not sit alongside a premium tax credit.
3. **It is its own separate premium** `[00:04:21]`.
4. **The official record adds year-round enrolment**, so a consumer does not have to wait for the open enrolment window, and the vision company handles enrolment, billing and plan questions rather than Covered California.

A note on the name: the recording renders the first of the two companies unclearly, while the official Vision page names **EyeMed** and **VSP**. Treat the official names as the ones to give a consumer.

**Dental.** For adults, dental is a separate family dental plan, bought alongside a health plan. The official record is explicit on two points:

- **A dental plan cannot be bought on its own.** A Covered California health insurance plan is needed first, and the shopping tool asks about dental once the health plan is chosen.
- **Two rules about who goes on it.** At least one adult aged 19 or older has to enrol, though not every adult in the household does; and if one child under 19 enrols, every child under 19 in the household has to enrol.

And the two kinds, because consumers will ask: a **DHMO** covers dentists in its network only, needs a referral for a specialist, has no deductible and sets a price for each service; a **DPPO** covers dentists in and out of network, needs no referral, usually has a deductible, and you pay a proportion of the bill.

**A point worth making to a parent.** A family dental plan can sit alongside the dental already inside the child's health plan, and parents often do this because their own dentist is outside the network that arrived with the health plan. The child holds both, and the two plans settle the bill between them.

## Step 4: The silver plans that lower what a member pays

**Three of them, and the names are the actuarial values.** The training's chart set out, for 2026, what each of the three silver cost-sharing-reduction levels costs to use `[00:22:41]`, and the values are 94, 87 and 73 — the same every year `[00:22:47]`. Silver 70 is the silver plan without cost-sharing reductions, and it continues to have some deductibles `[00:22:58]`.

★ **You do not get to pick the one you like.** A consumer cannot ring up and ask for a Silver 94 because a friend has one `[00:27:00]`. It turns on an income amount and a household size `[00:27:18]`, read off the federal poverty level chart set beside the cost-sharing chart `[00:28:47]`. The shopping tool answers the same question `[00:29:07]`.

The training's own discipline on this point is worth keeping: at an income of $40,000 a consumer is probably looking at Silver 70 or Silver 73 rather than Silver 94, but you let the results be for themselves and make no assumption about their eligibility `[00:29:15]`.

**The figures the training read from the 2026 chart.**

| Figure                                  | Value   | Locator      |
| --------------------------------------- | ------- | ------------ |
| Bronze individual medical deductible    | $5,800  | `[00:25:26]` |
| Bronze family medical deductible        | $11,600 | `[00:25:26]` |
| Silver 94 individual medical deductible | $0      | `[00:26:27]` |
| Silver 87 specialist visit              | $25     | `[00:29:39]` |

**The caveat on the Silver 94 figure, which you must carry.** While the training was running, the website showed a family figure of $700 and a 2027 label, because the training course was working from the 2026 benefit design `[00:27:35]`, `[00:28:05]`. The Silver page as published on 2026-10-13 lists no medical deductible at all for Silver 94, individual or family. So the individual figure of $0 holds on both sources, and the family figure is the one to verify before you quote it.

**What the site publishes for all four silver variants**, as captured on 2026-10-13. The page does not print a plan year, so these are the values published on that date.

|                               | Silver 70      | Silver 73      | Silver 87      | Silver 94      |
| ----------------------------- | -------------- | -------------- | -------------- | -------------- |
| Individual out-of-pocket cap  | $9,800         | $8,100         | $3,350         | $1,400         |
| Family out-of-pocket cap      | $19,600        | $16,200        | $6,700         | $2,800         |
| Individual medical deductible | $5,200         | $5,200         | $1,400         | None           |
| Family medical deductible     | $10,400        | $10,400        | $2,800         | None           |
| Specialty drug, tier 4        | 20% up to $250 | 20% up to $250 | 15% up to $150 | 10% up to $150 |

**The site's two rules that make the table work.**

- **Cost-sharing help comes only with the Enhanced Silver levels** — 73, 87 and 94. Silver 70 is open to everyone; the only part that depends on income is the Enhanced level.
- **Qualifying for an Enhanced level does not oblige the consumer to take it.** They can pick any plan, and if they pick bronze, gold or platinum they keep any premium help but give up the extra savings on care costs.

**One sibling to know about.** A second family of cost-sharing plans exists for American Indian and Alaska Native members, and the training covered them in the same stretch `[00:32:02]`. Only the member who is American Indian or Alaska Native gets one, not the whole household. They have their own rules and their own lesson.

## Step 5: Minimum coverage, and who it is for

**Also called a catastrophic plan** `[00:32:18]`, and it is a different animal from the four metal tiers.

**Who may buy it.** Two groups: anyone under 30, and anyone who has been released from the individual mandate, either on general hardship grounds or because cover is unaffordable `[00:32:31]`.

**What it does.** It exists for one purpose — a medical emergency that is serious and expensive. The everyday costs of seeing a doctor or filling a prescription stay with the member until the out-of-pocket limit is reached `[00:32:36]`.

**The mechanics, in order.**

1. The first three non-preventive visits are free `[00:32:52]`: a visit with a primary care doctor, a visit with another practitioner, an urgent care visit, or a mental health or substance use visit.
2. Once those three are used up, the member is responsible for the whole bill until the out-of-pocket limit is reached `[00:33:06]`.
3. After that, everything else is handled at the in-network rate the plan has negotiated, and the deductible still has to be cleared first `[00:33:13]`.

**No financial assistance on this plan** `[00:33:20]`. That is why the consumer will not receive a 1095-A for it `[00:33:33]`. The official record says the same: the plan never qualifies for financial help, whatever the income.

**Why someone would choose it.** Two reasons, and the training gives both.

- **Protection from a very large bill** `[00:34:21]`. A procedure costing $15,000 against a $12,000 out-of-pocket maximum means the member is responsible for $12,000 and the insurer pays the remaining $3,000.
- **To satisfy the state mandate without a penalty** `[00:34:56]`.

**Minimum coverage against bronze** `[00:35:17]`, because this is the comparison consumers actually ask for:

- The minimum coverage **deductible is higher** than bronze's.
- For lab work, bronze has a **$50 copay that is not subject to the deductible**; on minimum coverage, the out-of-pocket limit has to be reached before the company makes any payment toward the service.
- And the official record adds the commercial point: a bronze plan may carry a similar monthly premium and can offer financial help if the consumer qualifies.

**Two more official-record facts that change the advice.** If the consumer is 30 or older, the exemption has to be applied for and approved first, and Covered California issues a number for it — the consumer then buys the plan straight from the health insurance company rather than by way of Covered California. Under 30, the plan is bought through Covered California, and when the consumer ages out they are moved automatically to a bronze plan.

## Step 6: Drug tiers, and who owns the answer

**The four tiers** `[00:36:11]`: generics sit on tier 1, preferred brands on tier 2, non-preferred brands on tier 3, and specialty drugs on tier 4.

**The caps the training stated** `[00:36:25]`: for a specialty drug the member's share is capped — on silver, gold and platinum the ceiling is $250 for a single 30-day fill, and on bronze the ceiling is $500 for a 30-day fill at any tier, in both cases once the pharmacy deductible has been cleared.

Those are ceilings, and the site's own tables sit under them: on the Silver page, Silver 70 and Silver 73 cap the specialty drug at $250 while Silver 87 and Silver 94 cap it at $150, and on the Bronze page the standard plan's tier 2, 3 and 4 cells are capped at $500 per prescription.

★ **The insurance company classifies the drug, not you** `[00:37:22]`. The consumer rings their carrier, finds out where their prescription sits on the tier list, rings back, and then you can read them the copay for their plan from the drug chart `[00:37:53]`, `[00:38:04]`. The training was blunt about the limit on your side: you are not expected to know the tier `[00:39:13]`.

**The formulary** `[00:38:16]`. To find out whether a prescription is covered at all, the consumer asks their health insurance company for their drug list; extra formulary information is available through the shopping tool. On the service centre's own access to the formulary, the training's reading was that it cannot be pulled up there, though it hedged that it would need to go back and check `[00:38:41]`.

**Two rules the training read off the slide** `[00:36:36]`: every plan has to run a dedicated phone line for prescription questions that anyone enrolled or about to enrol can ring, and the appeals and exceptions route has to be spelled out on the formulary, so a member can see what to do when a drug they need is not covered.

**Worked example.** A consumer asks whether a new medication is covered and what they will pay for it. You do not guess at the tier and you do not quote a copay for a drug you have not placed. You find out which plan they are on, ask them what tier their pharmacy has given, and read the copay from that plan's drug chart.
