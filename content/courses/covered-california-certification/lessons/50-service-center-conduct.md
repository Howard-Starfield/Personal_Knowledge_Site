# Service Center Conduct and Consumer Safety

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the emphasis the training itself placed on them.

## What you will be able to do

- Say which parts of a service centre training session are internal to the centre and which part a consumer is affected by.
- Describe how the centre is organised around you: the systems you work in, the internal positions, and how openings are filled.
- Give the short empathy line that is enough, and name the two responses that are not.
- Apply the definition of a threat, and recognise what is not a threat.
- Carry out the first minutes of a call from someone who has made a threat, up to the point where the supervisor takes it.
- Ask the questions that help, and point a consumer to help that exists beyond your own desk.
- Draw the line between helping a consumer who cannot afford cover and trying to keep them, and give the state requirement once, as a heads-up.
- Recognise the anxiety of a first call as normal, and treat the patience you owe yourself as part of the job.

## Step 1: What this session was

The session recorded on 21 October 2026 was a training session about the service centre. It had three strands, and they are not the same kind of thing.

1. **The working environment.** How the centre is set up, how the systems are arranged, and how the internal positions fit together and how openings are filled.
2. **The consumer-threat procedure.** A walkthrough of the centre's knowledge article on handling threats made by consumers.
3. **A practice consumer.** A worked scenario you work through, deciding how to respond at each step.

> ★ **The first strand is internal, and it stays internal.** The training used "we" throughout for the centre's own procedures — who gets promoted, how many people the client wants on chat, who pays for what. None of that is consumer-facing policy, none of it changes what you may say to a consumer, and a consumer has no business being told how the centre is staffed.

**The second strand is the part a consumer is affected by.** When someone rings the centre and says they intend to hurt themselves or somebody else, what the centre does next is not an internal matter. That is the part of this session a consumer would be affected by, and it is the part the rest of this lesson teaches.

The third strand is a rehearsal. Its value is in the decisions you make, not in the consumer it names.

## Step 2: The working environment around you

**The system you work in.** Salesforce. The training spent its first few minutes on a navigation question that a new starter asks: there is a row of drop-down tabs across the top, and it is noisy, and can it be removed. The answer was that it is not a fault but a view — the application menu, the grid of dots beside the tab list, is where you change the view to Service Console, and that is the view this work is done in. The training's honest verdict on Salesforce was that there is a great deal in it and it is easy to feel overwhelmed.

If you move to the chat team, the chat itself happens inside Salesforce as well, so the same navigation carries over with more on top of it.

**The internal positions.** The entry level is an SCR — a service centre representative. Above that sit the team lead, the supervisor, the chat specialist, the trainer and the quality assurance role. The training was clear that this is not a ladder where every rung pays more: it doubted that chat carried a pay rise, and the honest answer was that it depends and you ask.

**How positions open.** The centre hires internally for these roles. When a position opens it is posted on the internal jobs site, the supervisor sends the whole team a link, and you apply from your work computer. For an internal applicant the stated bar is good statistics, a recommendation from your supervisor, and a short interview.

**Temporary and permanent.** The supervisor positions around open enrolment are temporary: the first round went out at the end of August and the last round in November. When open enrolment ramps down, the temporary positions ramp down with their teams, and people go back to the position and the pay they had before. Permanent positions are limited by what the client needs, because a team has to exist before a permanent supervisor can sit in it.

**The client sets the numbers.** During open enrolment the client tells the provider how many people to move onto chat, for example. The training also mentioned that there are not always enough volunteers for chat, and that people have been moved into it who did not want to be.

**One thing that is about your life rather than the work.** The Covered California project requires you to live in California. Other positions on the same jobs site can be worked from any state. Moving out of state without telling your employer ended someone's employment.

> ★ **None of this is a promise you make to a consumer.** It is the shape of the place you work in. Keep it in that drawer.

## Step 3: Empathy is the floor

The client expects representatives to demonstrate empathy, and the training was specific that this is expected even on a day when you do not feel it. Call after call after call, you will not always feel it. The job still requires it.

**Empathy is not only for threats.** The training's point was that empathy applies to anything a consumer says, and it gave the ordinary cases: a consumer whose spouse has just died, or a consumer who needs insurance because they need medication and have none.

**The short line is enough.** A plain acknowledgement of what the consumer said, followed by what you are going to do about it — that you are sorry to hear it and you will do what you can — is all that is needed. A variation that acknowledges how the consumer must be feeling is equally acceptable.

**Two responses that are not.** The first over-promises, telling the consumer you wish you could do more than you can. The second claims experience you do not have, by describing the consumer's situation as one you have been through yourself. Neither belongs on the call.

**Also out: describing yourself as a therapist or a mental health professional.** You are not one, and the training was explicit about not implying that you are.

**Why it is not optional.** On a threat call, empathy will make or break the call. On an ordinary call, it is the difference between a consumer who trusts you and one who does not.

**The response to hopelessness.** The training worked through a practice consumer who said she felt hopeless and could not see things getting better, and marked this as the appropriate answer: say that it is very tough, say that you will do your best to help in any way possible, and ask whether there is anything you can do to help resolve it, or whether she can reach out to someone else.

## Step 4: What counts as a threat

The knowledge article's definition: a caller states, on a call or in a written message, that they intend to hurt any person, themselves included, or that they intend to damage or destroy property belonging to the state.

**A threat to oneself.** It is not a threat under the workplace violence programme's own definition. It is handled the same way anyway, because the procedure is the same. The training was clear on this point and it is the single most likely misreading.

**Who may be the target.** The consumer themselves; people in their immediate area; the staff taking the call; other Covered California employees; county workers; anybody associated with Covered California insurance agents, with Medi-Cal, or with the state of California; a public official or a political leader, including California's governor and the president of the United States. And it covers damage to a Covered California office building, a service centre, or another county, state or entity facility.

**Why the list is so long.** The training's observation was that most of the time this lands in the same category, in the form of a statement implying the person is thinking about harming themselves.

**What is not a threat.**

- Saying you are having a bad day.
- Saying you will file a complaint, or warning that a complaint is coming. The training's answer was that if you follow the policies and procedures and do everything correctly, a consumer is free to file as many complaints as they like.
- Saying you will write a bad review.
- Grounding a child for not doing the dishes, which the training called gentle parenting.

**The rule that governs all of it.** Any time a caller states on a call or in writing that they intend to hurt any person, themselves included, or to damage or destroy state property, get the attention of your immediate supervisor.

## Step 5: The first minutes of the call

1. **Take the threat seriously.** Do not weigh it up.
2. **Stay as calm as you can.** Listen, respond, and keep the conversation going.
3. **Reach your immediate supervisor through Teams as soon as someone makes a threat.** You can do it while you are still on the call; you do not need to end it, and you do not need to hand the call over first.
4. **Say something to hold the caller while you do it.** The training's advice was to keep the conversation going, and to have something ready to say so that the caller is not left in silence.
5. **If your supervisor has not replied within about five minutes, assume they are not available.** They may be on a break, on another call, or in a meeting. Call the supervisor cue. The backup supervisor is the supervisor cue.

> ★ **Do not put the caller on hold if you can avoid it.** The training was blunt about the reason: consumers disconnect. There is one case where it happens anyway — if you have to reach the supervisor cue, the caller is placed on hold automatically. That is the exception, not the pattern.

**And do not hang up because the caller asks whether you called the police.** You say that you did, you apologise, and you say that the situation had to be escalated, and you stay on the line. The training's reason for keeping the conversation going is the practical one: while you keep them talking, the response is on its way to the consumer's address.

## Step 6: What the supervisor does

Your part is to get the call to the supervisor. The supervisor at the cue will do one of three things: guide you through the call while they prepare to call 9-1-1; take the call over themselves, joining it and taking it from there; or let you carry on with the caller while they make the call on their side.

**What is not yours to do.** You do not call 9-1-1 yourself. You do not fill in any report yourself. You follow the knowledge article and the centre's emergency action plan.

**What you do while you wait for that.** Take as many notes as you can. If the caller has a case number, you can gather it from the case. If they have no case number and they are distraught and cannot give you one, that is fine — you cannot force it, but by the end of the call you should have some identifying information. If they mention weapons, write that down. The more you have, the better it is. And document the interaction, including the detail of the conversation if it is needed.

**If it turns out they were joking.** The training addressed this head-on, and its answer was that it is not worth the risk. People sound like they are joking, and it is not a joke. When a consumer says something that sounds like theatre, there is a line available: the system is built to pick up this kind of language, and there is a process to follow.

## Step 7: Questions worth asking

The training worked through a list of candidate questions against a practice consumer, and kept the ones that help.

**Worth asking.**

- Are any others present — is a spouse or are any children with you right now?
- Are you in a public place, or are you alone?
- Where are you?
- What has caused you to feel this way?
- How can we reach you if we lose contact?
- How old are you?

**Not worth asking.** What are you having for dinner. Did you watch the recent game. A third one was discussed and set aside: whether the caller has taken a deep breath is not really a question, and whether to say it depends on whether the caller sounds frantic.

**One that was accepted with a caveat.** Asking what caused the consumer to feel this way was judged correct, but it can feel like prying, and it can make a consumer feel you are prying. The training offered an alternative wording that ties the question to the situation you are actually there about: ask whether they are feeling this way in relation to their health insurance, and whether there is anything Covered California can do to help resolve how they are feeling.

## Step 8: Ending the call and pointing to help

**If the consumer says they feel a little better.** Do not dismiss how they are currently feeling, and do let them know that help exists if they ever feel that way again — that there are trained professionals available to them. The training was precise about the wording: do not name therapists or mental health professionals as though you were routing to them.

**And do not tell a consumer to reach out to somebody other than Covered California to help them next time.** The training marked that as not appropriate.

**The knowledge article adds one more thing.** A caller who is threatening to harm themselves is to be encouraged to speak with a crisis centre or a mental health facility, and the national suicide hotline is available around the clock.

**The distinction the article draws.** Sometimes a caller is having a mental health crisis rather than making a threat. Where that is the case, you can transfer them to the national suicide hotline — and you can make it a warm transfer, staying on the line until you have introduced them to the person who answers.

**The recap the training put on the screen.** Work out whether the person is making a threat. Show that you have heard the person's concerns. Ask them questions. Let the person know that you are there to help. Write down what happened. Where the caller shows an obvious and present danger to themselves or others, get your supervisor involved. Follow your work site's emergency action plan.

## Step 9: Helping a consumer who cannot afford to stay

This stage comes from a different session — one recorded on 6 October 2026, in the call centre, at the end of a working day. It is the consumer-facing part of that session, and it belongs here, because the boundary it draws is about conduct rather than about a system.

**Retention is not the job.** The training opened with it. The centre does not want to lose consumers, but worrying about whether a member is retained is not part of the representative's job. The job is customer service and being helpful. Whether somebody cancels is not yours to control, and the training put that decision above your pay grade (00:00:00–00:00:19).

**Do not hold the consumer hostage.** The training's example was a consumer who says the prices are ridiculous and that they cannot afford cover. The wrong response is the plea: hold the consumer hostage and ask them to stay. The right response opens with plain acknowledgement — that you completely understand, that times are tough and that things change (00:00:19–00:00:41).

**You may state the state mandate once, as a heads-up.** Having acknowledged the difficulty, the next move is to let the consumer know, in case they do not already know it, that there is a state mandate, that Californians are required to have health insurance, and that there is a penalty. The training's framing was a heads-up and not a warning shot: you are making sure the consumer has the fact in front of them, and the choice stays theirs (00:00:41–00:01:08).

**The training's own sign-off.** The modelled answer ends by regretting that the consumer is going, and by wishing them a plan B (00:00:51–00:00:55).

> ★ **You help; you do not hold.** The whole stage is one boundary. Help the consumer with the decision in front of them, give them the one fact they may not know, and let them choose. Whether they stay is not yours to decide.

**What the same session carried, and why it is not taught here.** The recording behind this stage also carried the training's own account of the 2027 penalty change. It is not taught here, because the recording does not own that rule: the training was reading it off the website while on the call, was unsure of it, and the amounts given in that part of the recording do not reconcile with one another. Read the penalty from the Franchise Tax Board and from the official Covered California pages instead. The figures this package treats as canonical are in lesson 04, 'Covered California, the Individual Mandate, and the Obamacare Call'; they are not restated here, and they should not be quoted out of a recording.

## Step 10: Your first call

The same session finished on the people starting out rather than on the policy, and it belongs in the same lesson: the boundary in Step 9 is much easier to hold if you can still pick up the phone.

**Feeling anxious is normal.** The training said the first call is frightening, and that it will be anxiety-inducing even if you have been in customer service before. That reaction is normal, and the training said so plainly (00:04:39–00:05:01).

**It takes practice, and patience with yourselves.** Knowing the procedure is not the end of the learning. The work needs plenty of practice on your own side, and the training said it would go on saying so for the next three weeks (00:05:01–00:05:06).

**The patience you owe yourself is part of the job.** The training said the patience and grace you show yourself have to match the patience and grace you are expected to show a consumer, and called it a two-way street. A call that leaves you shaken is not a sign that you are unsuited to the work; it is the first call doing what a first call does (00:05:06–00:05:30).

> ★ **Be as patient with yourself as you are with a consumer.** That is not softness. It is the standard the training set for the role, and it is the same standard, applied to you.

1. The three strands of the session, and which of them a consumer is affected by.
2. What the internal positions are, how openings are filled, and why the temporary ones ramp down.
3. The short empathy line, and the two responses that are not acceptable.
4. The definition of a threat, and the four things that are not threats.
5. The five steps in the first minutes of a threat call, and the waiting period before the supervisor cue.
6. What the supervisor does, and the two things that are not yours to do.
7. The questions worth asking, and the one that was accepted with a caveat.
8. What you say at the end of the call, and what you must not say.
9. Where the line sits between helping a consumer who cannot afford cover and trying to keep them, and the order of the modelled answer.
10. What the training said about a first call, and the patience it asks of you.

Then check the two things people miss: that the career path and the staffing arrangements are internal and never become consumer-facing policy, and that a threat to oneself is handled exactly as any other threat is handled.
