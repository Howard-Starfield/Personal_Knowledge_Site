# Tax Forms and Proof of Coverage

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Name the form that belongs to each kind of coverage, and the two you can never obtain for a consumer.
- Say what each of the three columns on Form 1095-A reports.
- Get a consumer their document through the account, a reprint, or a manual mailing, without ever emailing it.
- Draw the line between explaining a form and giving tax advice.
- Warn a consumer about the second cost of letting a premium go unpaid.

## Step 1: Which form belongs to which coverage

Start from the coverage, not from the form. Ask where the consumer's insurance came from and what kind of plan it was, and the form follows from that answer.

The official record sets the frame. Covered California sends IRS Form 1095-A once a year, by January 31. It lists the months a person carried insurance and the federal financial help they received during the year, and the IRS later compares that help against what the person actually earned. So a 1095-A does two jobs at once: it is the evidence of coverage a consumer hands to a tax professional, and it is the record of assistance that the filing then settles up.

Three groups never receive one. Someone covered by Medi-Cal, someone on a minimum coverage plan (which the official page calls a catastrophic plan), and someone whose employer bought coverage through Covered California for Small Business all sit outside this form.

| Where the coverage came from                                                                                                                                        | The form the consumer should have | Where a question about it goes |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Covered California individual market                                                                                                                                | Form 1095-A                       | Covered California             |
| Covered California for Small Business, Medicare, or Medi-Cal                                                                                                        | Form 1095-B                       | The body that issued it        |
| Employer cover where the employer has 50 or more full-time equivalent employees, including where that employer bought through Covered California for Small Business | Form 1095-C                       | The employer                   |
| Employer coverage                                                                                                                                                   | Form 1095-C                       | The employer                   |
| A minimum coverage plan, which the official page calls a catastrophic plan                                                                                          | No Covered California form        | The plan's own issuer          |

The training added the rule that decides what you do when a consumer asks about the other two. A 1095-B arrives out of Medicare, Medi-Cal or the Small Business programme. A 1095-C arrives out of the employer's payroll. Neither of them is yours to open, reprint or correct. A question about the county's 1095-B goes to the county; a question about the employer's 1095-C goes to the employer.

There is a second California form to know about: FTB Form 3895. The training stated that it existed for tax years 2020 and 2021 and not for later ones, so a consumer asking for a 3895 for a recent year is asking for something that does not exist.

> **One uncertainty to carry.** The course material and the spoken session did not agree on exactly which later years the 3895 stopped for — one of them also raised a year after 2025. Confirm the tax year against the official record before you tell a consumer that a 3895 is unavailable for it.

## Step 2: Reading the three columns of Form 1095-A

The form carries three sections, and they answer three different questions.

**Part 1** holds the recipient's personal information. **Part 2** lists everybody the policy covered, with their social security number, their date of birth, and the dates their cover began and ended. **Part 3** runs from January through December and records, month by month, what the person was enrolled in, what that cost, and how much assistance was received.

The three columns live in Part 3, and each one answers a different question:

- **Column A** — the whole price of the plan for that month, before any help is taken off it.
- **Column B** — the price of the second-cheapest silver plan on offer to the household, quoted whether or not anyone chose it.
- **Column C** — the assistance that was paid.

Take Column A away from Column C and you have the amount the household actually paid that month.

**Column B is the one that causes trouble.** A consumer looks at it, sees a silver plan they did not buy, and assumes the form is wrong. It is not a mistake and it is not the plan they picked; it is a comparison figure — the premium for the second-cheapest silver plan on offer in the household's area, and the figure the tax calculation runs on. You can define it in one sentence. The question of what it does in the return is not one you answer — that goes to a tax professional.

**Zeros need reading, not guessing.** For benefit years 2024 and earlier, a dollar amount shows in Column A only for a month in which there was paid cover, so a zero in Column A or Column B can mean the carrier ended the cover for non-payment — or it can mean a mistake. For benefit years 2025 and later, all three columns carry a dollar amount. Either way the coverage dates recorded against the person settle which it is: if the end date lands exactly where the zeros begin, the zeros are the non-payment; if the dates show unbroken cover, the zeros are an error.

## Step 3: How the form reaches the consumer

Two channels, according to the official page. A paper copy goes to the address held on file, and the document also appears in the consumer's online mailbox, with an email or a text letting them know it has arrived. A consumer may also receive more than one form.

**The email is a signpost, not the document.** Someone who asked for email contact does not get a 1095-A in their inbox. They get a notice telling them to sign in and open their mailbox. The training was blunt about the trap here: staff who email these documents to a consumer can be terminated for it. Do not email a form. Do not press a button on the expectation that it will send one. Use an approved reprint route instead.

Timing matters when a consumer rings. The official page gives January 31 as the date by which the form is sent, so a consumer who calls before that date, or who says nothing arrived, is not automatically a lost form — find out whether the form has been produced and where it is before anything else.

## Step 4: Choosing a reprint path

There are two routes, and picking the wrong one costs time or costs you.

**The CalHEERS reprint** is the ordinary one. It covers a consumer who wants another copy, a consumer who wants paper although they prefer email contact, and a consumer who simply wants the document. The training noted a "reprint via mail" option that covers the second of those, and put the usual wait at about ten business days.

**The manual Salesforce reprint** is the special-order desk. Use it when the paper has to go somewhere the normal flow cannot reach: an out-of-state address, the tax professional's own address, a preparer who is not the consumer, or a relative of a household member who has died who holds the right paperwork. The training warned that this route can take up to sixty days, that it cannot be expedited, and that neither a lead nor a supervisor can move it along.

Two rules keep you out of trouble:

1. **Do not repeat a CalHEERS reprint inside fourteen calendar days.** The system displays when a document was last reprinted. The training was explicit that this window is calendar days and not business days. A second request inside it draws a quality-assurance correction.
2. **Do not reach for the manual route when the ordinary one will do.** An unnecessary manual reprint puts the case on a list, and coaching or disciplinary action can follow.

If the only problem is that the address on file is out of date, a report of change updates the mailing address on the account. It cannot rewrite a form that has already been produced.

## Step 5: Where the tax advice stops

The boundary the training drew is simple to state and hard to hold: explain what the form says, and let a tax professional explain what it means for the tax return.

On your side of the line: which form the consumer should have, what each part and each column reports, how the document reaches them, what a zero in a column means, and whether the form is right. On the other side: whether they owe the IRS money, whether they get money back, whether a penalty applies, and what they should do about it. The official record agrees — Covered California does not provide tax advice, though it can point a consumer at the IRS and the Franchise Tax Board. The training put it more bluntly: staff do not hand out tax advice even when they are qualified to do so on the side.

Two situations where the line gets tested.

_A consumer's tax professional insists on a form the consumer does not have._ Tell the consumer which form it is, whether they should have received it, and how to get it. If the consumer has nothing and still has to file, the training was clear that they can go ahead without it. If their professional disagrees with that, the consumer takes it back to their professional — that argument belongs to them, not to you.

_The consumer asks what they will be penalised._ The training's own rough figures were up to $900 for one person and up to $2,700 for a family of four, and it said in the same breath that those numbers can vary and that knowing them is not your job. Two corrections to carry with that. First, the $2,700 figure is the December 2025 release's number for the 2026 cycle, built from $900 for each adult plus $450 for each child, so it describes a family of four made up of two adults and two children rather than a flat amount for every household. Second, the two bodies' figures differ, which is why a figure never travels without its body and its year attached.

|               | Covered California — the site's own figures                             | Franchise Tax Board — as the training quoted them     |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| tax year      | 2026 (the label attaches to the affordability test, not to the penalty) | 2025                                                  |
| penalty       | $950 for each adult and $450 for each child under 18                    | $950 for each adult and $475 for each dependent child |
| affordability | 8.05% of projected household income                                     | 7.28% of household income                             |

**One caveat on the right-hand column, because it changes what you promise.** The
left-hand column is the official record's own: coveredca.com publishes those
amounts, and it labels the 8.05% threshold for tax year 2026. The right-hand
column is **the training's account of what the FTB publishes**, and this package
holds no FTB publication of its own — the only external source it may consult is
coveredca.com, and its own verification pass could not reach an FTB or IRS
publication, so even the left-hand column's corroboration against the FTB is
recorded as outstanding. The right-hand figures are internally consistent
($950 and $475 for two adults and two children is $2,850), and they are what the
training told the service centre — but a representative's answer does not depend
on them being right. Name the body and the year, and let the consumer read the
FTB's own figure.

The line to use with a consumer: I can tell you what the form says and how to get hold of it. What it means for your tax return, and what the state may charge you, is a question for a tax professional.

## Step 6: What an unpaid premium can cost

There are two consequences to a consumer who stops paying, and the second one is the one people miss.

The first is the obvious one: cover can end. The second is tax. Assistance was already paid to the carrier on the consumer's behalf for months that were never paid for. If the consumer walks away from the premium, the carrier does not quietly absorb that money — it can come back to the consumer as a repayment to the IRS at tax time. The training's worked example put the scale plainly: a monthly premium of $100 against $2,000 of assistance. The consumer is choosing between paying $100 and running the risk of owing $2,000.
