# The Required Case Review

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Explain what a required case review is, and why the boxes on the case screen are not the review itself.
- Name the three things the review checks, and the rule that decides whether you offer a consent update.
- List the situations in which the review does not apply, and say what to do when you are unsure.
- Tell a consumer what documents are due and by when, and explain what you may and may not do for them.
- Explain why a household the review does not apply to still has to be authenticated.
- Say which of the four minimum essential coverage items the county controls, and which of them a consumer's own signed attestation can close.

## Step 1: A checklist, not a form

A required case review is a look at the case you already have open, carried out as part of a service call. It is not a form to be filed and it is not a separate piece of work that happens after the call.

The training was specific about the checkboxes in the right-hand column of the case screen. They exist so that a representative does not forget something. They are a **mental checklist**, not the review itself:

- Doing the review on the case is what counts. If you worked through it during the call and never touched the boxes, you are not penalised for that (00:03:21–00:04:23).
- Working down the list because there is something visible to tick, with no idea what any of it means, is the failure the training named. It is still a failure, because the review on the case is what the evaluation looks at (00:04:00–00:04:23).

The evaluation form carries a line for each thing a call is expected to contain, and one of those lines asks whether a required case review was done. Many of the lines on any single call are marked not applicable, because most of what the form lists does not apply to that call (00:40:47–00:41:24).

## Step 2: The three things the review checks

The training walked the review through as a sequence on the screen: authenticate, confirm the contact details that authentication did not already cover, look at the consent, look at eligibility for each person, and then view the personal verifications. Three of those are checks in their own right (00:19:17–00:20:34).

**1. Contact information that authentication did not cover.** Authentication has already taken the home address and the telephone number, so those two do not need repeating. What the review confirms is the rest: the email address on file and the preferred method of contact (00:35:03–00:35:19). The wording for that step was to read the preferred method and the email address back and ask whether they were right.

**2. Consent for verification.** Look at whether the consent is current, and whether it is about to run out. The line the training drew was two years: if the consent is due to expire within two years of now — 2027 or 2028, in the October 2026 frame the session was recorded in — you offer to update it. If the date is 2029 or later, you do not need to offer anything (00:35:19–00:35:56).

The direction of the risk is worth carrying. Offering an update that was not needed is never penalised. Not offering one when it was needed is (00:36:07–00:36:33).

**3. Eligibility for every person in the household.** Not only the primary. Read the eligibility summary for each person, because the summary is produced per person and one of them may be showing something the others are not (00:35:25–00:36:38).

The personal verifications are the fifth thing on that list, and they get their own stage below. You open them for **every member of the household**, and what you are looking for in each one is three things: any negative action, any documents, and income that is due (00:35:30–00:35:54, session 2026-10-22 07-56-48).

## Step 3: When the review does not apply

There is a set of cases where the training said the review is not required (00:30:22–00:34:38):

- **A household where every member is on Medi-Cal.** There is nothing for you to check and nothing you are able to change, so the preferred method of contact, the documents and the rest of it are left alone (00:04:29–00:04:46).
- **An inactive case, or a brand-new applicant.** There is nothing on the case to review (00:30:36–00:30:52).
- **A non-primary adult enrollee, or a divorced member.** They can authenticate the primary and you can take their request, but you are not going to read the primary's contact details back to them, because they are not the primary and you could not act on the answer (00:30:56–00:32:28).
- **Someone calling to end the whole household's Covered California cover.** If the spouse is keeping their own plan, the review still applies, because not every member of the household is leaving (00:32:35–00:33:07).
- **A household with no active enrolment in any medical cover.** There is no enrolment and no plan, so there is nothing to review (00:33:47–00:37:16).
- **A consumer with an exemption** on the case (00:33:54–00:34:03).
- **An outbound call.** The training's note was that the only outbound calls are to consumers who have been disconnected from the service centre, and in every other scenario the review is done (00:34:03–00:34:26).

**The review is not conditional on documents being due.** Asked whether the outstanding documents and the negative actions are checked only in a mixed-program family, the answer was that they are checked every time, whether the household holds a Covered California plan alone or is mixed, and that the one household which does not get the review is the one where every member is on Medi-Cal (00:41:41–00:42:07, session 2026-10-22 07-56-48).

**When you are not sure, do it.** That was the instruction for every uncertain case, and it carries no downside. Doing the review where it was not required earns credit. Not doing it where it was not required is recorded as not applicable, which is neither a penalty nor a credit. Doing it where it was not required and doing it badly is also recorded as not applicable (00:33:07–00:33:41, 00:37:31–00:37:55, 00:41:24–00:42:11).

The knowledge-base article the training cited for the exceptions is KB1417. If you keep doing the review where it was not needed, the advice was to leave a friendly note on the case rather than to raise it as an error (00:38:01–00:38:41).

## Step 4: The document step

This is the part of the review most likely to produce a task, because it produces a deadline.

**Pending means the system could not check something itself.** The application is built to verify immigration status, social security details and income electronically. Where it cannot, the record shows a pending status and the consumer is given a window of days to send documentation instead. Upload inside that window and the application is fine (00:24:16–00:25:03).

**The training's figures.** A later session in the same training splits that window: **95 days for most documents, and 155 days for proof of income** (01:36:12–01:36:31, 01:37:59–01:38:16, 02:00:09–02:00:30, session 2026-10-22 07-56-48). The same session told the team not to memorise the numbers but to read the knowledge article instead (01:36:31–01:36:39).

**The official record publishes no number of days at all.** The local copy of the official site says nothing about a period of days for the reasonable opportunity period, so any figure you quote is the training's own. Read the deadline off the record rather than from memory.

**What happens if the window closes.** The consumer stays eligible while the period runs; once it passes with nothing uploaded, the system is built to end the cover automatically (01:38:16–01:38:42, session 2026-10-22 07-56-48).

**The extension is a judgement call, not an entitlement.** Do not offer one unless the consumer asks for it or the deadline is close (01:38:42–01:38:52, session 2026-10-22 07-56-48). Once the deadline has passed there is none (01:41:09–01:41:26). The recording calls it a 60-day extension and gives the route — go to manual verifications, and give them 60 days (01:41:35–01:42:04) — while saying in as many words that there is no number anyone has to stand by (01:39:49–01:40:17). An earlier session describes two extensions of 30 days each, to be requested before the 95 days run out (session 2026-10-15 10-56-52, 02:35:00–02:35:49). Read the knowledge article; do not quote a remembered length. Lesson 53 sets out the whole rule.

**Tell the consumer what is due and by when.** That is the requirement, and the training was clear that a refusal of help is an acceptable answer: you have still told them what is due and when it is due, so you have done your job. The shape of the wording that was modelled was to say that, while going through the case, you noticed that certain documents were needed by a certain date, and then to ask whether they want help uploading them now (00:25:07–00:26:00).

**You guide; you never upload.** If a consumer asks you to email a document to you so that you can upload it for them, the answer is no. You can talk them through their own account: sign in, open the home page, find the documents and correspondence section, and add the document there (00:21:37–00:22:12).

**What the record looks like afterwards.** Once a document is uploaded, the status changes from a due date to under review, and it sits there until somebody reviews it by hand. The manual verification is not a service-centre job. The training described it as the work of the chat team, who ring the consumer back, and as something that can sit for months if nobody picks it up (00:23:03–00:24:07).

**If a document will not do.** If you can tell that what the consumer uploaded does not meet the requirement, say so straight away and ask whether they want to send something else. If you cannot tell, call the lead line: they will either tell you it is not acceptable and say what to send instead, or accept it and pass it for you. Either way the consumer waits on hold while you sort it out. You do not transfer the call away (00:26:48–00:28:04).

**The letters are on the case.** Everything written to the consumer sits under account information, documents and correspondence. When a consumer says that nobody told them, the letter and its date are what you check, and the document the consumer uploaded is in the same place (00:28:50–00:29:49).

## Step 5: What the review is not

**Authentication is separate, and it still happens.** A household where everyone is on Medi-Cal does not get a required case review, but it does get authenticated: the consumer's first and last name, their date of birth, and two further details. Authentication itself is skipped in only one place, which is where you literally cannot authenticate — a brand-new consumer with no case and a general question (00:08:15–00:09:18).

**In a mixed-program family you still do the review.** The official record calls this a mixed-program family; the training called it a mixed household. Two steps happen with the documents that Medi-Cal is waiting for, and only the first of them is yours. Helping the consumer upload them and telling them whether what they uploaded looks acceptable is yours. The eligibility redetermination that clears those documents belongs to the county, and clicking pass on your own does not do it (00:05:32–00:06:39).

That last point is the one to hold on to: the service centre can move the document, but it cannot make the determination.

## Step 6: The four minimum essential coverage items

The application asks the consumer whether they are enrolled in any other cover, and for each kind it cannot check itself the record can carry a verification item. The question behind every one of those items is how you prove that you do not have something: the documents asked for are proof that the consumer does **not** have that cover, not that they do (01:51:11–01:51:39, session 2026-10-22 07-56-48). The system is meant to check the answer against federal records, and when it can, the record comes back saying that the two agree. When it cannot confirm the answer, the item comes back **pending or not verified**: the consumer is telling the marketplace they have no cover of that kind, while the system is saying it thinks they do (01:46:11–01:49:20, session 2026-10-22 07-56-48).

So a case can carry four verification items, and this is where the review stops being one job. Three of them have a document that settles them. One of them does not, because the body that settles it is not yours.

| Verification item              | What settles it                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **MEC employer-sponsored**     | A document showing the consumer has no employer-sponsored cover and no offer of one. A self-attestation will do. |
| **MEC non-employer-sponsored** | The same, attested separately: the consumer states they have no cover that does not come from an employer.       |
| **MEC Medicare**               | The attestation form on the website: the box ticked, the information filled in, signed and dated.                |
| **MEC Medi-Cal**               | Nothing you can do. It is the county's item, and the training's own chart marks it red — do not touch.           |

**The Medi-Cal item is not yours to close.** Whatever the consumer tells you about Medi-Cal — that they do not have it, that they make too much for it — makes no difference: the item stays where it is, and so does your ability to touch it (01:52:10–01:52:49). A denial letter does not change it either: the consumer can upload that letter all they want, and the county still has to verify the information (01:52:49–01:53:13). What you do do is explain. You can say that the record shows they might have Medi-Cal, that you hear them, that you trust them and believe them, and that the county is the body that has to confirm it — and you can tell them to contact their county (01:53:07–01:53:36).

**The two employer items close on the consumer's own words, signed.** A letter from the consumer's HR department is one way to show there is no employer cover, but it does not have to be that dramatic: the consumer can attest for themselves. They can write it out by hand or type it out, put their CalHEERS case number on it, date it and sign it, and state in it that they do not have employer-sponsored cover and do not have an offer of employer-sponsored cover — and, separately, that they do not have non-employer-sponsored cover (01:53:58–01:55:26). Written on paper and photographed, or typed out, it does the same job. When such a document is uploaded, the item can be passed: by you, or by the lead until you are comfortable with manual verifications (01:55:27–01:55:55).

**Medicare has a form of its own.** The website carries an attestation for it: the consumer ticks the box saying they have never been enrolled in any Medicare plan and are not currently enrolled in one, fills in the information, signs it and dates it (01:55:55–01:56:26). The official record for proof you do not have minimum essential coverage names the same two documents — a signed self-attestation made under penalty of perjury, on the Attestation of Lack of Minimum Coverage form, and the separate Attestation of Medicare Eligibility and Enrollment Status form (coveredca.com, Proof You Don't Have Minimum Essential Coverage).

**Why the boundary matters more than the shortcut.** Suppose you pass MEC Medi-Cal by mistake, because a consumer handed you a denial letter and it seemed plain enough. The county comes to the item later and fails it, and what follows is no financial assistance and a negative action (01:56:51–01:58:46). The consumer is worse off for your helpfulness. Trusting the consumer is right; passing the item is not.

So the shape of it is this: the consumer's word plus a signature settles the two employer items, the website form settles Medicare, and the Medi-Cal item is settled by the county or not at all.
