# Documenting a Case and Adding Plans

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Set the order of work on a plan call, and say which later step depends on which earlier one.
- Correct a household before a price is quoted, and explain why the premium moves when you do.
- Add a health plan and then a dental plan, with the PIN and the e-signature entered the way they have to be.
- Help a consumer compare two plans on a live call without rebuilding the percentages from memory.
- Leave a record a reviewer can act on, and pick the case reason that matches the work you actually did.

## Stage 1: The order of work on a plan call

The training worked a scenario that is worth keeping as the shape of the whole job: a consumer calls to add health cover and dental cover for the primary, and the spouse is the one on the line (00:15:15–00:15:24).

The order that was set, and the order that works because each step depends on the one before it:

1. **Work the change on the application.** Household size, income, tax filing status, and the status of the spouse (00:02:58–00:03:41).
2. **Record the event that triggered the change** and resubmit the application (00:03:41–00:04:07).
3. **Add the health plan**, from the enrollment dashboard (00:12:53–00:13:03; 00:13:53–00:14:02).
4. **Add the dental plan**, once the health plan is finished (00:15:06–00:15:24).
5. **Document the case and close it** (00:02:30–00:02:58).

Two details about step 3. The training asked for the enrollment dashboard rather than the choose-plan option, so that people get used to the same route every time (00:12:53–00:13:03). And the prompt asking whether to choose the primary is a default — it does not need answering, because the primary is who the plan is for (00:33:15–00:34:27).

> **The dependency that catches people.** The dental option only opens once a
> medical plan is in place. The training paused the medical enrolment
> deliberately before moving on (00:15:06–00:15:24; 00:15:42–00:16:16).

## Stage 2: Get the household right before you price anything

Everything the system quotes is read out of the application. So the price is only as good as the household the application describes.

**The case that made the point.** The spouse in the scenario has employer-sponsored cover, so the spouse is not applying for cover on the marketplace application. When the application was pulled up, the spouse had been left sitting in an applicant state, which is the error the training identified and cleared (01:27:57–01:28:23). The correction the training specified: the spouse should be recorded as having not applied, or as not eligible, because they have employer cover (01:28:09–01:28:19; 01:28:49–01:28:53).

**What that did to the price.** The same household, on the same $80,000, and with no income change at all:

- With the household counted as **one person**, the price shown was around $600 and no financial assistance, because that income sat outside 400% of the federal poverty level for a household of one (01:13:32–01:14:11; 01:26:42–01:26:53).
- With the household counted as **two people**, the same $80,000 fell inside 400% of the poverty level for a household of two, and the price shown dropped to around $340, with some assistance from the programme (01:13:51–01:14:52).

Nothing about the income changed. Only the household did, and the quote followed it.

> **The habit this creates.** Fix the household, let the application
> resubmit, and only then start reading premiums to the consumer.

**And the event that allows it.** Adding a spouse is a change to the household, and outside the open enrolment window a household change needs a qualifying life event. The scenario's event was a new domestic partnership, dated 12 October 2026 (00:03:41–00:04:07).

## Stage 3: Adding the health plan

The screen is familiar if you have used the shopping comparison tool, because it is built the same way (00:14:02–00:14:12).

**Before anything is selected, ask.** Filters the consumer wants, and providers they want included — then move on (00:14:02–00:14:17).

**Then read the list correctly.** The plans that come up are the plans available to that consumer, because the list is drawn from their application and from the financial help they qualify for (00:14:17–00:14:35).

**Then the four steps that finish it** (00:14:42–00:15:06):

1. Select the plan.
2. Agree to the plan.
3. Enter the PIN.
4. Type the consumer's given name and family name.

**The PIN.** In the training environment it was 1234. In production everyone on the service centre shares one PIN, which the training gave as 3742 — and then said it would hand out again closer to live work, because nobody should be relying on a remembered number (00:16:57–00:17:44).

**The consumer's own PIN is a different PIN.** It is created with their account, it is what they use if they enrol online, and you do not know it. If they have lost it, you can help them reset it (00:17:44–00:18:09).

**The e-signature.** Type the name of the person who is actually signing the plan agreement. If the primary is signing, it is the primary's name. If the spouse is signing, it is the spouse's name (00:18:09–00:18:29). The name is not automatically the primary's just because the plan belongs to the primary.

## Stage 4: Adding the dental plan

The dental plan is a separate choice with the same shape as the health one: an HMO dental or a PPO dental, and the training expected the difference between them to be explained before a selection was made (00:16:04–00:16:16).

**The official record on what is separate.** Adult dental and vision are plans of their own, each with a bill of its own; children's dental and vision sit inside the health plan and do not produce a separate bill (coveredca.com, Guide to Your Bill and Payments). That is worth saying out loud, because a household with children hears one bill and a household of adults hears two.

**The dental-specific trap.** The network question is not the same for dental as it is for medical cover. An HMO dental is fine for ordinary treatment, but the training's example was root-canal work, and most root-canal specialists are not inside an HMO's network. That work is out of network, and the consumer pays the full price for it (01:22:58–01:23:12; 01:23:57–01:24:10).

So the questions before a dental plan are recommended are not abstract: what procedure does the consumer expect, which dentist do they want to keep, and does the work need a specialist (00:34:27–00:35:04; 01:22:47).

## Stage 5: Comparing two plans on the call

The comparison is done live, with the consumer, and the detail comes from the comparison view rather than from anyone's memory (00:32:43–00:33:15).

**The filters that are there.** Lowest premium, or the lowest estimated yearly cost (00:35:04–00:35:18). They answer different questions, and a consumer who picks the lowest monthly figure without looking at the yearly figure has not compared anything yet.

**The four questions to ask before recommending** (00:34:27–00:35:04; 00:50:01–00:50:38):

1. Do you want a smaller amount every month, or more of each bill covered when you use care?
2. How often do you expect to see a doctor?
3. What prescriptions do you take?
4. Is there a doctor you want to keep?

**The number that decides most comparisons** is the deductible — the amount you have to pay before the plan starts covering (00:47:28–00:47:48). Add it to the premiums and you have the total cost of the year if you use no care at all, which is the number the training compared (00:47:28–00:48:11).

> **The trap in this session.** The spoken percentages did not hold still. In one
> stretch the training gave 60% for Bronze, silver from 70% up to 94%, and 80%
> for Gold; then, in the same worked example, it priced a $100 doctor visit at
> $40 to the member with the insurer covering 60% — which is the Bronze
> arithmetic attached to the Gold sentence (00:32:02–00:32:36). Do not rebuild
> a percentage formula out of the recording. Read the figures off the
> comparison view, and use the tier definitions you already have.

And the frame around all of it: you describe, the consumer decides. The training's instruction was to ask which parts the consumer is interested in and give them the information (00:50:01–00:50:38).

## Stage 6: The record you leave behind

**The subject** names what the call was about, in two to three words, maybe four. For a report-of-change call the training used report a change, add a household member, or add a spouse (00:01:05–00:01:29).

**The description** is the short account of what the consumer asked for and what you did (00:01:29–00:01:42).

**The trap that loses notes.** Clicking completed request before the notes are written deletes the temporary notes on the way. The fix the training gave: copy anything you still need somewhere else, clear the temporary text, change the request type back to none, and save (00:01:42–00:02:22).

**Closing the case.** Feed, close case, the completed-request reason, and the CalHEERS case number (00:02:30–00:02:58).

**What the note has to carry** (00:02:58–00:04:07):

- That this was a primary call, and what you did on it.
- The household size you set, the income, the tax filing status, and any other relevant application information.
- Why the spouse is not asking for cover, because the cover they hold comes from an employer.
- The qualifying life event, written out in full once and then abbreviated — the training named the acronym on first use and used it afterwards — together with its date.
- The eligibility result you gave the consumer.
- What happens next: in the scenario, that the consumer would call back to add a plan.

**The document request is part of the record.** Tell the household that proof of income is being asked for, and give them the date. The training said such dates are usually around 95 days from the day of the call (00:05:24–00:06:23). A later session is firmer and splits the figure: 95 days for most documents, and 155 days for proof of income (session 2026-10-22 07-56-48). The official record publishes no number of days at all, so treat any date you quote as the training's rather than as published policy, and read the deadline off the record instead of from memory — lesson 53 sets out the whole rule. If you say it in the call and it does not reach the note, the next person has no idea the household is waiting on a document (00:06:40–00:07:04).

**The edit window is short.** A case that has just been posted stays editable for roughly 15 to 20 minutes, after which it locks on its own. After that, ordinary edits are not available and you add a later note through the post-now route instead (00:07:04–00:08:03).

**The case reason has to match the work.** For a call where a plan was added to an existing application, the training wanted the Covered California application or case picked first and plan selection picked second. In the debrief, choices such as no application enrolment and online assistance were identified as ones that could be marked wrong (01:32:14–01:32:58).

## Stage 7: The training environment is not the real thing

Everything the screen showed was the training environment's answer, not the marketplace's, and the training said so while it happened.

- **Look for the grey bar.** If a colleague is sharing a screen and you cannot see the grey bar, they are in the live system, and anything you type is a real note on a real consumer's case (00:12:00–00:12:30).
- **The eligibility result is the training's.** The worked example produced a consumer eligible for marketplace cover with no financial assistance, and the training corrected itself on the spot: the household in front of you might well be eligible for financial help (00:04:22–00:04:45).
- **The document date was missing from the screen.** The training told you to treat the spoken estimate as the date and to say the date out loud (00:05:56–00:06:23).
- **The PIN is different.** 1234 in training, a shared number in production (00:16:57–00:17:44).
- **The name search is not a shortcut.** The training warned against it: it is described as working only in production, and it brings back information that cannot be relied on. The combination search, using the details the consumer has given you, is the route (00:29:10–00:29:44).

## Review

Close the lesson and write down, from memory:

1. The five steps of a plan call, and which one the dental step waits for.
2. What a spouse with employer-sponsored cover has to be recorded as, and what happened to the quoted price when it was corrected.
3. The four steps that finish a health-plan enrolment, and whose name goes in the e-signature.
4. The difference between the service centre's PIN and the consumer's own PIN.
5. The questions you ask before recommending a plan, and the number that decides most comparisons.
6. The seven things your note has to carry.
7. How long a posted case stays editable, and what you do after that.

Then check the two things people miss: that the training's own spoken percentages were inconsistent, so the comparison view is the only source for the figures; and that the dental network question is not the same as the medical one.
