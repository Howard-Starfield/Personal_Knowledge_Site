# When Two Systems Disagree

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the emphasis the training itself placed on them.

## What you will be able to do

- Name the two systems that hold a record of the same coverage, and say what each one owns.
- Describe the worked case in this lesson and say which parts of it are the case and which are the lesson.
- Use the enrolment change log to establish what was changed, by whom and on what date.
- Choose between an 834 resend, a termination-date change and a reinstatement, and say why.
- Apply the escalation timing rule, including the waiting period when no carrier representative is on the line.
- Prepare a case for escalation in the order the training set out.

## Step 1: Two systems, two records

The Covered California enrolment system and a carrier's own system each hold a record of the same household's coverage, and they do not always agree.

| the enrolment system                                     | the carrier's system                               |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| the application and the eligibility determination        | the policy the carrier has actually issued         |
| the plan selection and the first payment taken           | what the carrier believes is active, and from when |
| what was changed, when it was changed and who changed it | what the carrier will honour a claim against       |

The two systems feed each other, and the feed is not instant. A change made in the enrolment system has to travel to the carrier before the carrier's own screen changes. In the meantime the consumer is holding two different accounts of their own coverage, and they are the one who notices.

**Why the consumer is the one who feels this.** The service centre works in the enrolment system. The carrier works in its own. Neither side is looking at the other's screen, and neither side is wrong about what it can see. The consumer calls because they have been quoted two answers by two organisations about whether they are covered.

**The consequence.** The consumer's coverage is the join of the two records. If the records disagree, the consumer cannot safely buy a new plan, cannot safely stop paying, and cannot get a refund that the carrier thinks they do not owe.

> ★ **The training's framing of the case was that one system shows something different from the other.** It did not treat either system as the reliable one by default. It treated the disagreement as a fact to be investigated, and the investigation starts in the enrolment system's own record of what was done.

## Step 2: The worked case

This is a training scenario, worked end to end in the session recorded on 20 October 2026. It ran in a practice environment: the systems on screen were sandbox copies, and the calls were role-played among trainees. The scenario is the source of the facts below; the lesson is in the method that follows.

**The scenario as it was set.**

- A carrier's representative calls, with the household's primary on the line, to confirm the household's enrolment status.
- The carrier's record shows a Blue Shield Gold ADPPO policy as active, with a start date of 1 November 2026.
- The consumer made their binder payment through the Pay Now feature in the enrolment system.
- The consumer has still not received a refund from the carrier.
- The consumer needs that money back before they can choose a new plan and put down a new binder payment.

**The disagreement.** The enrolment system shows the plan as cancelled. The carrier's system shows it as active. The consumer wants the binder payment back, and the refund has stalled because the carrier still believes there is coverage in force and money owed for it.

> ★ **Nothing in this step is a rule.** The plan, the dates, the household and the figures belong to one scenario. Another disagreement between two systems will have different facts and may need a different correction. The steps that follow are the part that carries over.

## Step 3: Read the enrolment change log

The training's instruction for this case was to research the household's enrolment history through the enrolment change log in the enrolment system, and it named that as the first thing to do before reaching out for help.

The log is the enrolment system's own record of what was done to the household's enrolment and when. In this case it showed:

- Two cancelled plans on the household.
- Both cancellations processed on 15 October 2026, by a representative with a supervisor present.
- The consumer's payment having gone through, so money was owed back.
- The information not yet having reached the carrier's site, which is why the carrier still showed the plan as active from 1 November.

**Why the log comes first.** It settles three things at once — whether the change was made at all, who made it, and when. The date is what the escalation timing turns on. The fact of the change is what identifies the correction. And "our screen and their screen disagree" is not a finding; it is the symptom that sends you to the log.

**A caution that came with the case.** The household in the scenario was a mixed-program family, and before an access code is handed over, the county note on the case is checked, because for a mixed-program family the code may not be one you are able to give. The detail of that check belongs to the case-review procedure rather than to this lesson.

## Step 4: Name the correction

The correction in this case was an **834 resend** — a resend of the enrolment transaction to the carrier. It was not a termination-date change, and it was not a reinstatement.

| the correction          | what it does                                                                                              | why it did not fit this case                                                                                                                          |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 834 resend              | re-sends the enrolment information to the carrier, so the carrier's record matches the enrolment system's | this is the one: the change was already made in the enrolment system and never arrived at the carrier, so the copy that needs fixing is the carrier's |
| termination-date change | changes the date a plan ends on the household's record                                                    | the plan had already ended on the date the change log shows, so there is no end date left to correct                                                  |
| reinstatement           | puts coverage back on for a consumer whose coverage ended                                                 | nothing was ended from this side, so nothing is being restored; the consumer's status is not changing at all                                          |

The training drew the distinction out sharply: from the service centre's side the consumer's status is not being altered by any of this. The enrolment system's record already says what it says. The record that is wrong is the carrier's.

**Why the choice matters for the consumer.** A reinstatement can be done at any time, with no deadline attached to it. An 834 resend is not in that position — it sits inside the window the household has for completing the rest of the actions needed to activate a plan. In this case the account showed 42 days. That is why the resend is worth chasing rather than waiting out.

## Step 5: When to escalate

The rule the training worked from has two branches, and which branch applies depends on who is on the line.

| the situation                                             | what you do                                                                                                       |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| a carrier representative is on the line with the consumer | try the escalation; the team lead decides whether the case is accepted                                            |
| no carrier representative is on the line                  | wait 10 business days from the date of the last change, then the consumer calls back and the escalation is raised |

**Why the two branches differ.** With a carrier representative on the line, the carrier can hear the correction being made, and the consumer's money may come back faster. Without one, the training's point was that the rule is clear and there is no room to read around it.

**In this case.** The last change was on 15 October and only five business days had passed, so the information had not yet reached the carrier. Counting ten business days forward from that date lands on about the 30th of October, and the training's suggestion was that the consumer call the carrier then to check the status. If the refund had still not arrived by then, the consumer calls back and the escalation is raised.

**The decision is not yours to make.** Where the carrier representative was on the line, the training's advice was to try the escalation and let the team lead decide whether the case is accepted. If the team does not take it, that is how it stands.

**What the consumer keeps meanwhile.** The window for completing the rest of the actions needed to activate a plan — 42 days in this case — does not stop while the two systems catch up with each other. The two clocks are different, and the shorter one is not a reason to wait.

## Step 6: Prepare the case before you reach out

The training was blunt about this, because in the session the escalation itself was practice. The point of the exercise was the habit, not the outcome. The procedure to follow when reaching out for help was set out in this order:

1. Research the household's enrolment history through the enrolment change log in the enrolment system.
2. Post your notes, using the template.
3. Give the consumer the reference number.

**The order matters.** Notes and the reference number go in before you reach out, not afterwards. A consumer waiting for a refund needs the reference number in their hand while the escalation runs, and the note is what a reviewer reads.

**The case review still has to be finished.** In this scenario the review covered all five members of the household. Several of them were showing as not verified, and proof of income was due on 18 January 2027. A representative can look at whether a document is acceptable; the lead line is where it is passed. How a required case review is run is not the subject of this lesson.

## Step 7: What this case is, and what it is not

A worked case is not a rule, and the distinction is worth keeping straight.

**Belonging to this case alone:**

- Blue Shield Gold ADPPO, a start date of 1 November 2026, a cancellation dated 15 October 2026, two cancelled plans on the household, five members, a binder payment, a 42-day window, a document date in January 2027.
- A practice environment: sandbox systems and role-played calls.

**Belonging to the general lesson:**

- Two systems hold records of the same coverage, and the consumer is the one who sees the gap between them.
- The enrolment change log is where you find out what was actually done, by whom, and when.
- The correction is chosen from what the log shows. An 834 resend is right when the change was made here and never arrived there.
- The escalation timing depends on whether a carrier representative is on the line.
- Notes, the template and the reference number go in before you reach out.

**And one boundary that is neither.** The service centre does not decide whether the money comes back. Escalating the case is what the service centre can do; the carrier then decides whether to issue the refund. The training was explicit that a consumer in this situation is supposed to get their money back — the service centre's part is making sure the carrier holds the record that lets them.
