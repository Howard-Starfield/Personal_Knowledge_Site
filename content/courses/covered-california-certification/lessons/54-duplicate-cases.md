# Duplicate Cases: Finding Them and Handing Them On

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the emphasis the training itself placed on them.

## What you will be able to do

- Describe what turns a duplicated coverage problem into an escalation.
- Work a duplicate case in the order the training set out, finding the cases before working out which coverage the consumer is keeping.
- Say who researches the health plans after the hand-over, and what your own part in the case is.
- Recognise the moment your job on a duplicate case stops.
- Route a duplicate case and a non-payment reinstatement to the right desk.
- Tell a consumer what a complaint can and cannot do once an escalation has been refused.

## Stage 1: What a duplicate case is, and the first move

A duplicate case escalation is the situation where the same consumer turns up on more than one case and more than one plan is running at the same time. The training's worked example was a consumer with three plans going at once. That is a duplicate case escalation, and the training ran it through as the other escalation scenario, beside a reinstatement after a non-payment termination.

The first move is a combo search. You search on the consumer's details and you take every case that comes back.

Here is the part that catches people out. A case that comes back marked closed and terminated is not automatically out of scope. The training's instruction was not to assume that a case is one you can ignore, because you might still need it, so the cases were worked through by process of elimination instead. In the worked example the search returned three cases for one consumer, and the first one looked closed at a glance.

So the work is this. Pull up each case in turn and go into the enrolments on it, rather than reading the header and moving on. A case can be closed and still carry a plan with coverage dates on the record. In the training's example, one of the three cases held a plan that had been cancelled but still showed the months it had covered.

Only once the cases are found does the question about coverage come up. Then you ask the consumer which plan they want to keep, and you take that as the start of the conversation.

> ★ **The order is the lesson.** The training put the case-finding first and the plan question second, and then warned straight away that the answer is not as simple as which plan the consumer wants to keep. The consumer's preference is what you record. It is not what decides the outcome, because somebody else still has to establish what was actually used before anything can be changed.

## Stage 2: Who does the work

Your part in a duplicate case ends at the hand-over, and the work that decides the case happens on the other side of it.

**What you do.** Find the duplicate cases. Post your notes. Reach out to the lead line to get approval to call the escalation queue. That is the whole of the representative's part, and the training listed it in that order: getting the case to the point where the escalation queue will take it is your concern, and what happens after that is not.

**Who does the rest.** The downstream team takes the case from the escalation queue and signs it. In the recording that team is called ERI&T; the recording never expands the letters, so treat them as a label for the team that signs the case rather than as a name you can quote.

> **One uncertainty to carry.** The recording names the downstream team but does not expand the abbreviation, and the automatically generated guide for the same session lists it among the terms to verify. Work to the process - the escalation queue takes the call over, and a downstream team does the carrier research - and not to the letters.

**What they have to establish.** Whether the consumer used services under each of the policies they held. The downstream team contacts each of the health plans involved and asks whether the member used any services in the months they were enrolled in that coverage.

**Why it is not a quick fix.** If the consumer used services under all of the policies, the fix is hard. The training's own reasoning was that the provider may have to submit the claim to the other health insurance company, and that only works when the doctor is in network with them. There are a lot of things that have to be determined, and the service centre is not the desk that determines them.

**An analogy.** It is like handing a leaking tap to the plumber. You are the person who finds the leak, writes down where it is, and gets it handed over. You are not the person who has to go into the wall and find the joint that failed.

## Stage 3: When to stop

The stop point is exact, and the training gave it in the middle of the worked example: once the escalation queue has taken the call over, you are done.

What that means in practice:

- You do not follow up with the consumer.
- You do not go back and research the case again to check whether the consumer's problem has been resolved.
- You move on to the next call.

The training put it this way: your worry is getting the case to that point - finding the duplicate cases, posting the notes, and getting the lead line's approval so that you may telephone the escalation queue. Once the queue has taken the call over and signed the case to the downstream team, that is the end of your part, and the downstream team owns both the carrier investigation and the corrections that follow from it.

The general rule is the one the escalation handling lesson teaches: the case stops being yours after the hand-off, and you keep your own case open. Nothing about a duplicate case changes it.

> ★ **Done means done.** The training said it twice in the same breath - as soon as the escalation queue has taken the call over from you, you are done - and then spelled out what that rules out. Chasing the consumer is not your duty once the case has been handed on, and neither is going back in to check whether it worked.

## Stage 4: Which desk it goes to

Two problems, two desks, two different endings. The training set this out by running the escalation scenarios side by side.

| the problem                                     | where it goes                                                                 |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| a reinstatement after a non-payment termination | straight back to the carrier                                                  |
| a duplicate case                                | the escalation queue, which takes it over and signs it to the downstream team |

**The reinstatement.** A reinstatement that follows a non-payment termination goes straight back to the carrier. It used to be that the service centre escalated it on the consumer's behalf. There is one exception: if a carrier representative is on the line and has the authority to reinstate, you escalate in the ordinary way. Most of the time that representative does not have the authority - they are ordinary people taking calls - and then the case goes straight back to the carrier and the consumer deals with them directly.

**The duplicate case.** It goes to the escalation queue. The queue takes the call over and the case is signed to the downstream team, which does the carrier research.

**Why the two are different.** A reinstatement is a decision the carrier makes about its own policy and its own account. A duplicate case is a question about what happened across more than one carrier. They are not two versions of the same problem, and they do not end in the same place.

The carrier's decision on a reinstatement is set out in the tax form dispute lesson. The general escalation routing, including the rule that the lead line comes before the escalation queue, is in the escalation handling lesson.

## Stage 5: If the escalation is refused

Sometimes the escalation comes back refused. The training's rule for that is about what the consumer can still do.

**A complaint is available when the consumer disagrees with the result or with the policy.** The training's wording was broad: any time a consumer disagrees with the policies and procedures, and any time they get a denial and are not satisfied with it, a complaint or an appeal is the route.

**But a complaint is unlikely to succeed on a complicated duplicate case once the escalation has been refused.** That is what the training said about a duplicate case that had been escalated and had come back refused. The complaint is not going to get the consumer anywhere, because the escalation has already been denied. And the refusal here was not a case turned away at the counter: the downstream team did the research and could not grant what had been asked. Where the matter is complicated - several duplicate policies across more than one health plan, with services used under more than one of them - the training's answer was that the consumer would need to file an appeal.

**What you say.** Do not promise a consumer that a complaint will reopen the escalation. Say what is available, and be straight about what a complaint is likely to achieve on this case.

> ★ **The order of the two routes matters here.** The training's general rule is generous - a disagreement with the result or with the policy is enough to open a complaint or an appeal. The duplicate case is the exception, because the escalation that would have dealt with it has already said no.

The appeals and Medi-Cal boundaries lesson sets out what a complaint is, what an appeal is, which route each kind of problem takes, and the fact that the consumer files it rather than you.

2. Why a case marked closed and terminated is still worth opening.
3. The order of the two things you do on a duplicate case, and which one is the start of the conversation.

5. What the downstream team has to establish, and why it is not quick.
