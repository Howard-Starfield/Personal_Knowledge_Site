# Inquiry-Only Calls

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Recognise a call whose purpose is inquiry or clarification rather than a change.
- Name the three actions such a call does not authorise, and say why each one is refused.
- Compare plans within the limits of a call, and correct a cost-sharing mix-up while the consumer is on the line.
- Say what has to be in place before a dental question can be answered.
- Document a call in which nothing on the case changed, and close it without implying that coverage changed.

## Step 1: When the call is inquiry-only

The session opened on a call that the training itself classified. The consumer wanted to understand the difference between health plans. Nothing about that request asked for a change, and the training's description of the call's purpose was that it was there for inquiry or clarification only.

**The rule the training applied: leave the case as it is.** Not enrol anybody, not add a dental plan, not combine or regroup the household. The reason given was practical rather than procedural. If the household is combined during an inquiry, the premium moves, and a record that says something the consumer did not ask for is harder to unpick later — the training put it as the call becoming confused.

The first caller in the session wanted Platinum and said they had no preference between carriers. That is the shape of an inquiry-only call: the consumer wants to understand a choice before they make it.

**Worked example.** A consumer calls because they want to know what Platinum costs before they commit to anything. You explain the tiers and the figures, you answer their questions about the cost-sharing terms, and you leave their enrolment, their dental cover and their household grouping exactly as they were. If they want to act afterwards, they call back and that becomes a different kind of call.

## Step 2: What you compare, and what you say

The comparison is bounded, and the training was honest about the bounds. Eight Platinum options were on the screen. The agent said they could take the consumer through three of them on the call, and offered to go into more detail on whichever carrier the consumer wanted to look at. The recording renders the other carrier names unreliably; Kaiser is the one name that comes through clearly.

**The questions the comparison starts with.** Before any tier is put in front of the consumer, the training asked what the consumer is optimising for: lower overall payments, or a higher premium in exchange for more of each visit being covered. Then how much prescription they expect to pick up and how often they expect to see a doctor. Then whether they have a doctor they want included. Then whether a zero deductible matters to them.

**The figures the training used.** The worked example was a Kaiser plan, given as a premium of $283, a $15 copay for a primary-care visit, a $0 annual deductible, and a $5,000 out-of-pocket maximum for the year, above which the plan covers the rest. These are the figures the training used for the example, not a quote for today's market.

**Two corrections the session made in the moment, both worth copying.**

The consumer asked whether the deductible is the money the company is asking them to pay. It is — what the consumer pays towards covered care until the insurer takes over paying for it. The training's agent got this wrong mid-call, spoke the $5,000 out-of-pocket figure as though it were the deductible, and then corrected it to $0 on the record.

And a $0 deductible does not mean free care. It means the insurer starts paying straight away on covered care; a copay still applies at the point of a visit. The training's example carried a $15 copay for a primary-care visit for exactly that reason.

**Handing the call back.** The consumer leaves with the figures, the answers, and the case number for the call, which is the reference they can quote if they come back. The training's close invited them to call when they were ready to do something, which is the right handoff for a call that changes nothing.

## Step 3: Different plans for different people in the same household

The consumer in the session had a reason for wanting two different answers: they had specialists and prescriptions, while a dependent rarely saw the doctor. Their question was whether they could take Platinum for themselves while the dependent took something else.

The training's answer was that each eligible member's choice can be looked at separately. The catch was the household, not the plan. The training explained that a change of this kind could not be made while the household was in the state it was in, and that grouping it so the members were eligible for Covered California was what unblocked it — and then the member could choose.

Two things to carry from that. First, a shared household does not force a shared plan, but the household has to be in order before you can do anything about it. Second, on an inquiry-only call you explain all of this and you do not make the change. The official term for a household split across the two programmes is a **mixed-program family**; the training's own phrase for it was a mixed family.

## Step 4: Dental comes after the health plan

The role-play in the session had a consumer asking what health and dental plans were available. The agent's answer had an order in it: would you like to pick a health plan as well, because in order to check on a dental plan you must have a health plan. Dental options are looked at once there is a health plan to attach them to.

On an inquiry-only call this lands twice over. Adding a dental plan is one of the three changes that call does not authorise, and the dental question itself cannot be answered properly until a health plan is in place. The figures the training named for dental were not reliably transcribed, so treat any dental price you hear in this material as unsettled.

## Step 5: What you write when nothing changed

The case has to say what the work was. On an inquiry-only call the record says the case was for inquiry and that nothing was changed — the recording renders the sub-reason as an inquiry-only reason with nothing changed on the case, though the exact screen wording was garbled.

There is a window after the call for finishing the documentation. The training named **four minutes**, and there is a template in the knowledge base to work from.

**Why detail matters here more, not less.** The quality check takes points off when details are missing, and the speakers tied a complete note to having to explain the call later if the documentation was questioned. On most calls a reviewer can read the case and see what changed. On an inquiry-only call nothing on the case changes, so the note is the only thing that shows what the consumer asked, what was explained, what was deliberately not changed, and why.

Detail means detail about the call. It does not mean copying every field from the screen into the note.

## Step 6: A closed case is not a change

The training made a point of this that is easy to miss. Closing the case records that the interaction is finished and what was done on it. It is not a record that coverage was enrolled or altered — on an inquiry-only call, it was not.

So the status at the end of a completed interaction is still closed, even though no change was made. The training's phrasing was that the case is the record of what you have done, and that from shadowing, agents closed the case whenever they had finished everything.

**Later additions.** If something needs to go on the case afterwards, it goes on as a post. The session was unsure whether the notes could be edited as well as added to, and settled on posting; the training environment may allow editing where production does not, and the speakers were clear that they were not certain about production.
