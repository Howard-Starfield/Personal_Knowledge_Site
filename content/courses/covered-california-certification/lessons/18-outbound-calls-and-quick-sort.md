# Outbound Calls, Recorded Consent, and Quick Sort

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Put an outbound call in the right order, with the consumer's consent to the recording taken before you authenticate them.
- Authenticate a caller on an outbound call against current records rather than the application file.
- Say what happens when authentication fails on a call you placed yourself.
- Use the quick sort calculator at the right moment, and explain why using it is a legal requirement rather than a courtesy.
- Hand a consumer over to a county in a way that protects them, and keep back the two things on your screen that must never be read out.
- Handle the after-hours, exception and mixed-household cases without inventing a rule.

This lesson comes from a single 90-minute recording made on **8 October 2026**, in the
last third of which the quick sort material is taught. Timestamps are given as
`[00:14:09]`.

## Stage 1: When the service centre places an outbound call

The service centre is an inbound call centre. The training is direct about it: this
team does not really place outbound calls unless a call gets disconnected (00:01:07).
Even then, the same process applies, and the consumer has to be authenticated again
rather than picked up where the earlier call left off.

The other outbound work sits with the chat team, and it is manual verification work:
the consumer has to be told that documentation they uploaded is not acceptable, or
reminded that they still have to upload documentation (00:02:38). In those calls the
consent and the authentication are repeated in that order, exactly as on a call that
began as an inbound one.

That is the whole list. The service centre does not place calls to chase cases, to
follow up on applications or to reach consumers who have not called in. If a consumer
asks for someone to call them, the answer is the one this programme uses everywhere
else: the service centre receives calls, it does not place them.

## Stage 2: Consent comes before authentication

The overview of the outbound process has three steps (00:01:24):

1. Greet the consumer and obtain their consent to the call being recorded.
2. Authenticate the consumer in CalHEERS, according to their relationship to the primary contact.
3. Determine whether the authentication has succeeded.

Consent is first, and it is not a formality to be folded into the greeting. Every
outbound call needs the consumer's agreement to the recording, because the consumer
never goes through the automated greeting that covers an inbound call (00:04:19). On
an inbound call, staying on the line is the agreement. On an outbound call there is no
automated greeting, so the representative has to say that the call is being recorded
and get an explicit yes (00:03:55).

If more people join the call later, each of them needs their own consent (00:04:04).

> **The trap.** The consent is not just for the consumer you dialled. A spouse, a
> delegate or a county worker who joins an outbound call mid-way has to agree to the
> recording in their own right, and the consent for the person you called does not
> cover them.

## Stage 3: Authenticating on an outbound call

The authentication itself is the same one used on an inbound call, with one routing
difference at the start.

When the caller is the primary contact, the steps to authenticate an inbound call in
CalHEERS are the ones you follow (00:04:35). When the caller is not the primary
contact, you may pass on the primary's first and last name together with a CalHEERS
case number, and then follow those same inbound steps (00:04:42).

From there:

- Validate the answers in the **Case Overview Hub**, not against the application PDF
  in the Application History page, which may not reflect the latest information on
  the case.
- Three correct answers from the approved list about the primary: date of birth, the
  full mailing or physical address, the phone number, the final four digits of the
  social security number, the email address, or the names of every household member
  recorded on the case (00:06:32).
- Then the extra criteria for the caller's relationship: spouse, domestic partner,
  authorised representative, delegate (00:06:40).

> **The trap.** "Three answers" is not the whole test. There is a second half to it,
> and it is the half that decides whether a spouse or a delegate is who they say they
> are.

## Stage 4: Deciding the result, and what failure means

If the caller answers three of the questions correctly **and** meets the extra
criteria, authentication has succeeded and you go on to the case review talking points
(00:09:24). If the answers are wrong, or the extra criteria are not met,
authentication has failed (00:09:43).

On a failure you release no information to the consumer, you make no account or case
changes, you provide no line information, and you reset no account password
(00:09:54).

The recovery steps are the ones from an inbound call (00:10:04):

1. Ask for the details as they appear on the caller's application or on other official documents, and check whether an official document matches what they gave.
2. If the caller can be authenticated that way, offer to correct the application so it matches the documents.
3. If it does not match, say plainly that the information they have given is not enough to authenticate them, and offer to help with general questions.
4. Point them at the website so that they can sign in and correct their own details.
5. Suggest that the primary contact calls **800-300-1506** and adds the caller as an authorised representative.
6. If the caller is a sales agent who is not on the agency roster, point them to the agent contracts address on coveredca.gov.
7. If the consumer has no existing application, start the process that helps them apply for coverage.

> **The trap.** Failing authentication does not mean the consumer gets nothing. It
> means they get no _case information_. The correction offer and the general-questions
> offer are still on the table, and they are what turns a dead end into a route
> forward.

## Stage 5: What quick sort is, and who has to be screened

Quick sort is not a service the service centre offers. The training puts it as a legal
requirement: screening for Medi-Cal eligibility is not optional (00:17:00). The law puts
the eligibility decision with the counties, so county staff have to handle the
applications of anyone who looks Medi-Cal eligible, and staff must therefore use the
quick sort calculator to screen callers before taking an application (00:20:01). The
practical consequence is stark: the service centre may not take an application at all
whenever the consumer, or anybody else in that household, looks Medi-Cal eligible
(00:18:30).

Quick sort is the feature inside the CRM that does the screening. It is used for
consumers who fall into the exchange, have no existing Medi-Cal case and no existing
Medi-Cal application, and could potentially qualify for one of the Medi-Cal programmes
the counties run (00:19:12). After a minimal amount of
entry, those consumers are transferred to a county representative, and the county
decides the eligibility (00:19:37).

The order of work matters:

| Step   | What you do                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| First  | Check whether the consumer already has a Medi-Cal case or application. If they do, refer them to contact the county directly and give the county's number from the DHCS listing (00:23:59) |
| Second | If there is no existing case or application, click the Perform Quick Sort button within the case. The quick sort has to be completed before any application is started (00:24:11)          |
| Third  | Ask the six questions the calculator lists and enter the answers, then submit (00:24:40)                                                                                                   |

**DHCS** is the California Department of Health Care Services, the body that runs
Medi-Cal rather than Covered California, and its county listing is the source of the
county numbers in this lesson.

> **The trap.** A case appearing in a search result does not take a caller out of the
> quick sort. The follow-up session in the training made this point directly: what
> matters is whether there is an active application, active coverage and active
> eligibility, not whether a case exists at all. A consumer who has merely created an
> account online is still a new consumer as far as the quick sort is concerned.

## Stage 6: The two quick sort results

Pressing Submit produces one of two results (00:25:13).

**No Transfer.** A Quicksort Complete window appears and the case remains open for
this request, so you carry on with the application: the eligibility step, plan
selection and enrolment for the appropriate family members, explaining the next
steps, and asking whether the consumer has any questions (00:25:13).

**Transfer.** The window tells you that the consumer, or another member of the
household, may potentially be eligible for Medi-Cal. Drop-downs appear for county and language. You
select the consumer's county of residence, then the language preference, and the
system shows you the result to use for the transfer (00:26:15). A wrong county
selection misroutes the call, so take care with it.

The two results pull in opposite directions on the case itself: the transfer closes
the case once the consumer has been handed over, with the resolution reason showing a
quick sort transfer to the county and the status closed (00:44:17). The no-transfer
result leaves the case open so the application can be finished.

> **The trap.** It is tempting to read "transfer" as the end of the consumer's
> journey and "no transfer" as the end of the case. Both are backwards. The transfer
> is where the consumer's application actually continues, with the county.

## Stage 7: The transfer, and the things you never say

A quick sort transfer is a coordinated, warm transfer: the transferring representative
stays on the line with the consumer, introduces the consumer to the county eligibility
worker, and only then lets the call go (00:18:39). The sequence the training gives
(00:43:31):

1. When the county representative answers, give your first name to introduce yourself.
2. Tell the county representative that the line is recorded.
3. Say that the call is a quick sort transfer coming from Covered California.
4. Let the county representative know that the consumer is coming to them.
5. Do not ask the county representative for medical information, and do not ask to remain on the call.
6. Finish the three-way conference, check that the consumer has not hung up, and then bring the consumer into the conversation.
7. Once the county representative has said they are ready to assist and the consumer is confirmed on the line, release the call.

Two things are never given to the consumer, however the call goes (00:34:42). The
county telephone number and the tracer ID appear on the transfer window, and they are
for your own use in making the transfer. The training was emphatic, and gave the
reason: a consumer who calls that number instead will be turned away by the county and
sent to the number that should have been given to them, the one on the DHCS listing
(00:36:21).

Nor may you tell the consumer that they are eligible for Medi-Cal. The quick sort
does not decide eligibility. What you may say is that the consumer might qualify for
Medi-Cal, or appears likely to, and that the county will determine it once they are
transferred (00:34:53).

> **The trap.** The eligibility wording and the telephone number are two different
> traps, and the wording one is easier to fall into. "You look eligible for Medi-Cal"
> is a promise the county has not made and you cannot keep.

## Stage 8: After hours, exceptions, and special circumstances

**After hours.** When the county office is closed, you leave a message, and you leave
it immediately, because the date of the attempt can be used to work out the month the
consumer's eligibility starts (00:53:16). Without a record of the attempt, a consumer
who does not follow up until the following month will not be backdated (00:56:54).

The message itself has to carry six things: the consumer's name, their phone number,
the county code, then the language code, and the tracer ID, along with the fact that
you are calling from Covered California and this is a quick sort transfer call (00:54:13). If
the line prompts you for specific information rather than a free message, follow the
prompts, and then take the consumer back from hold and tell them a message has been
left for them.

Two related rules. Do not hang up if a county other than the one you expected
answers, because many counties cover for each other during a quick sort transfer
(00:40:58). And if the county is busy, redial; never give the consumer the quick sort
transfer number to try themselves (01:27:14).

**The line being down.** What the training calls the IBR is the line you dial to reach
the county. If it is not working at all, rather than busy or closed, you give the
consumer the county office number from the website and tell them to submit their
application before the last working day of the month, because an application submitted the
next month postpones the effective date of their benefits (01:25:09). If it is the
last working day itself, the consumer has to be through to the county office before
that day is over. You still do not give the confidential transfer number, and you document the
line's unavailability in the instructions to the caller in the system (01:25:32).

**The exceptions.** These callers are not quick sorted:

| The caller                                                                                                                                                                   | What you do                                                                                                      |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A consumer with current Medi-Cal eligibility                                                                                                                                 | Give the county's number from the DHCS listing and send them on their way. No calculator, no transfer (01:12:43) |
| A consumer who says they have already applied with the county and is calling to follow up                                                                                    | The same: the county's number, and no quick sort (01:13:17)                                                      |
| A mixed-program family, where one or more members qualify for Medi-Cal or are already enrolled in it, and one or more qualify for Covered California or are enrolled in that | Handled by the counties, and no changes can be reported except contact information (01:20:11)                    |

The training put the mixed-household rule as state law taking effect on
**1 April 2014** (01:20:39). That date comes from the training and is not restated on
the official Covered California pages, so treat it as what the training recorded.

Two more things hold everywhere in this material. Medi-Cal accepts applications all
year round, with no open enrolment period and no qualifying life event needed
(01:07:01), which the official Covered California record confirms by describing
Medi-Cal as available to apply for at any time of year. And you never deny services
and never predetermine eligibility: you submit the application and let the system
decide (01:07:45).
