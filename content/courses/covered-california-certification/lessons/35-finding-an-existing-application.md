# Finding an Existing Application and Changing Income

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the training's own emphasis.

## What you will be able to do

- Find an application that already exists, and name the details the search needs.
- Explain why some records cannot be found, and do the thing that makes the next person able to find it.
- Report a change of household income on an application that already exists.
- Say which four facts an income change needs, and why the yearly figure is not the new amount.
- Read the final review back to the consumer and point them at the two reporting deadlines.

## Step 1: What an application record is

An **application** is the record of one household's request for coverage. One record holds the household and who is in it, the tax answers, the income on file, the plans chosen, and the notes and history that belong to it.

That record is the thing you work on. When a consumer already has one, the answer is to change that record, not to open a second application.

**Worked example.** A consumer applied earlier in the year and now rings because their pay has gone up. Their record already holds the household, the address, the tax answers and the income figure from the first application. Only one thing has changed, so only one thing gets reported — on the record that is already there.

The first time a household is set up, you create the record. Every later contact is a change to the same record. This is why the training spent a good part of its time on finding an existing record: if you cannot find it, you cannot change it.

## Step 2: Searching for an existing application

The training's tool for this was a **combo search**. It asks for four things:

1. First name.
2. Last name.
3. Date of birth.
4. The final four digits of the social security number.

Two of these come straight from the consumer. The fourth is the sensitive one, and it is also the one you cannot always get.

★ **The training's session chat window has no production equivalent.** While the search was being demonstrated, those four digits were read out of the comments pane of the session itself. The training was blunt about what that means: in production there is no way to find it there, so you ask the consumer.

**Worked example.** A consumer calls about an application they started last year. You ask for the name and the date of birth, and you ask for the final four digits of the number. If the consumer cannot produce the number, you are not finished — but you are not blocked either, because the training's point was that the date of birth is the detail you can normally get. Not every caller has a number on file, so treat the number as the uncertain one and the birthday as the reliable one.

**What a record that will not come back means.** The training expected a record that a colleague had created to appear on the same search. When it did not, the training called it a system problem rather than a consumer problem, and said that not finding the case you want is common. A search that comes back empty is not evidence that the consumer has no application.

## Step 3: When the record cannot be found

The record carries a **CalHEERS case identifier**. It sits on the case summary, at the end of the record.

★ **Without that field, the record cannot be retrieved through the search function.** The identifier is what links the case to the record you can pull back up, which is why the training kept checking it.

So the habit matters more than it looks:

- When you work on an existing application, copy the CalHEERS case identifier into a note on the case. The training was explicit about this: put everything into the case, so that the next person can find it.
- Note that there is **no view that lets you search by that number**. The identifier is something you record, not something you look up by. You find the case with the consumer's own details, and the identifier is what you write down afterwards.

The difference between _I know the number_ and _the system knows the number_ is the whole point. A number in somebody's head helps nobody on the next call; a number in a note on the case does.

## Step 4: Reporting a change on an existing application

From the record, you take the **reporting-a-change** path. The first question on that path offers two ways through:

1. Walk through every part of the application and update what has changed.
2. Skip straight to reviewing the final result.

The training walked the first one with the consumer, section by section, and the sections were:

1. Has your household changed?
2. Marital status.
3. Home address, then mailing address.
4. Tax information.
5. Income information.
6. Review and confirm.

**The tax information is four questions**, and the training read them out in order: which person files the household's tax return, do you expect to file for the year, are you expecting to be listed as a dependent on somebody else's return, and what filing status each person has.

**Worked example.** The consumer in the training had no change of household and no change of marital status. Every section was still read back and confirmed, because the point of the first route is that nothing is assumed. The consumer confirmed how the name of their town was spelled, and confirmed that the mailing address was to be the same as the home address. Reading everything back is how those details get checked at all.

## Step 5: The four inputs to an income change

The income section asks for four facts, and it will not accept the new amount without them.

| Fact                            | What you are recording                  |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| The new amount                  | The income the consumer earns now       |
| The main reason for the change  | One of four reason codes                |
| The frequency                   | Annual, monthly or weekly               |
| The date the new amount started | The date the new figure begins to apply |

**The four reason codes** are: the income has ended; the amount changed; the frequency changed; or the figure was entered incorrectly at the start.

**Worked example.** The consumer's annual income on the record was $45,000, and they now earn $60,000 a year. The reason recorded is a change of amount. The frequency is annual. The new amount started on 1 October 2026.

The training also asked why the income had changed and was told it was most likely a promotion at the same employer. That is the story behind the code, and it is worth knowing, but the record takes the code and the four facts — not the story.

## Step 6: Why the yearly figure is $48,750

After the change, the record shows two figures: a current money income of about $5,000 a month, and a yearly household income of $48,750.

The consumer asked whether the difference was tax coming out of the amount they had reported.

> ★ **It was not. The training's answer was that the figure is an average.**

The year ran at $45,000 from January to September and at $60,000 from October. Nine months at the old figure and three at the new one is $48,750 for the year. So:

- The amount you **enter** is what the consumer earns now.
- The yearly figure is **worked out for you**, from the amount and the date the amount started.

That is why the start date is not a formality. Change the start date and you change the yearly figure.

## Step 7: Reading back the final review and signing

The final review puts the whole application on one screen: the primary applicant and their age, the marital status, the home address, the income change, the calculated yearly household income, and the expected income.

**Read it back.** Do not read a screen silently to yourself and move on. The consumer needs to hear what you are about to record.

**A qualifying life event question may or may not appear.** If the flow does not put one in front of you, leave it blank. The training was asked about this and the answer was that a change made inside the special enrolment window does not need one.

**Then sign and submit.** The declaration the consumer agrees to states the two reporting deadlines:

- On **Medi-Cal**, a change must be reported within **10 days**.
- On **Covered California**, a change must be reported within **30 days**.

Both match the official record: Medi-Cal enrollees report household changes to their county within 10 days, and Covered California enrollees update their information within 30 days of an income change.

**Document as you go.** The training made a point of putting the case details into the notes while the consumer is still on the line, rather than afterwards, because the record may be hard to find later.

2. Which field on the case makes a record findable, and what you do about it every time you touch an existing application.
3. The two routes offered when you report a change, and the six sections the training walked.
4. The four facts an income change needs, and the four reason codes.
5. Why the yearly figure after the change is $48,750 and not $60,000.
6. The two reporting deadlines on the declaration, and which programme each one belongs to.
