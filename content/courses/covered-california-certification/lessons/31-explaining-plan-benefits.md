# Explaining Plan Benefits to a Consumer

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the emphasis the training placed on them.

## What you will be able to do

- Define a deductible, a copay, coinsurance and the annual out-of-pocket maximum in words a consumer can reuse.
- Work a consumer's first few visits and their first prescriptions through a plan's cost-sharing rules.
- Say which costs count toward the deductible and the out-of-pocket maximum, and which costs do not.
- Tell a consumer what a visit or a prescription will cost them, and be honest about what you cannot tell them.
- Hand the numbers to the consumer without turning the explanation into a recommendation.

## Step 1: The four words you will be asked to define

The training opened the cost-sharing module by saying that these terms will be asked of you directly. A consumer will ask what a deductible is, or what an out-of-pocket maximum is, and the expectation is that the answer comes without hesitation.

All four are cost sharing: the part of a covered bill that the consumer pays.

| Term                      | What it does                                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Deductible**            | The amount a consumer must cover on their own for covered services until the plan begins sharing. It does not attach to every service.                                    |
| **Copay**                 | A flat amount paid for a covered service, usually at the point of care or when the bill arrives.                                                                          |
| **Coinsurance**           | The consumer's share of a covered service, expressed as a percentage of the amount the plan allows.                                                                       |
| **Out-of-pocket maximum** | The ceiling on what a consumer pays for covered services in one plan year. Once it is reached, the plan pays the covered costs in full for the rest of that benefit year. |

★ **The one-line version to keep.** A copay is a fixed price for one service. Coinsurance is a slice of the bill. The deductible is the gate in front of the plan starting to pay. The out-of-pocket maximum is the ceiling for the year.

**Two sources, two jobs.** The official glossary on the Covered California site defines each of these words, so use it when a consumer asks what a term means. The plan's own medical cost-share chart carries the figures, so use that when they ask what their plan does.

## Step 2: Copay: the fixed amount

A copay is a set figure. The training's example was a Silver plan whose primary care visit was $50 flat: because the chart said $50 without qualifying it with after deductible, the consumer pays $50 for that visit whether or not a deductible is still outstanding.

**The colour code on the chart.** The copay amounts appear in blue, and the blue entries are the ones the deductible does not touch. Under Bronze, a few of those entries carry a white corner, and those are the ones that become deductible once the first three visits are used up.

**The Bronze rule, worked through.** The training used the specialist visit to show it:

- The Bronze specialist copay is $95.
- The first three visits cost $95 each.
- From the fourth visit the consumer pays the full price of the visit, often a pre-negotiated rate, until the medical deductible has been met.
- Once the deductible is met, the copay comes back.

A $300 specialist visit on the fourth trip makes the point: $300 rather than $95, and the same at every visit until the $5,800 individual medical deductible has been absorbed. After that, the next visit is $95 again.

★ **A copay is not a promise about every visit.** It is the price for the visits the chart describes. Under Bronze, the fourth visit of the same kind costs something else.

## Step 3: Coinsurance: the percentage

Coinsurance is the consumer's share of a covered cost, given as a percentage rather than a fixed amount. The training's illustration was surgery, where the plan picks up roughly 70 percent and the consumer covers roughly the other 30.

**Why the training would not give a total.** A consumer asked how much a surgery would cost. The answer was that only the carrier or the provider knows that; what can be stated is the percentage the consumer will be responsible for. The training drew the line the same way for the rest of that call: a carrier refusing a procedure is a matter for the carrier's own grievance route, and a member arguing about money they paid out of pocket and were not reimbursed is a matter between the member and the carrier.

★ **Give the percentage. Do not invent the bill.**

## Step 4: The deductible: what the consumer pays first

The deductible is what a consumer pays before the plan starts sharing. Three features of it decide how a bill actually goes.

**Which tiers carry one.** Only Bronze and the Silver 70, 73 and 87 plans have a medical or a pharmacy deductible. Gold and Platinum carry a $0 medical and pharmacy deductible, so a consumer can reach covered services straight away with nothing to pay first.

**One deductible or two.** A plan can carry a medical deductible and a pharmacy deductible side by side, each with its own figure, and the training's Bronze slide showed a $5,800 combined medical and pharmacy yearly deductible with a $450 individual pharmacy deductible alongside it. A high deductible health plan works differently: it has a single integrated deductible, which means medical and pharmacy are combined into one amount instead of sitting side by side.

**Before the deductible is met.** On a plan with a medical deductible, the consumer pays the full cost of the services the deductible applies to, and the plan has not paid any of it yet. On a high deductible health plan, everything except preventive care is charged at full cost until the deductible is met, and the training's phrase for it was that the consumer pays out of pocket 100 percent. A high deductible health plan is also the only kind of plan that works with a health savings account; money set aside there is not subject to federal income tax, and the balance rolls from one year to the next. The Covered California Bronze page puts the account more broadly, saying that as of 2026 it is offered across the Bronze range, so when a consumer is weighing Bronze options the plan page for the plan in front of them is the place to check.

★ **A ninety-day supply question belongs to the carrier.** If a consumer asks what a three-month prescription supply costs, the training's instruction was to point them at the summary of benefits and coverage, and then at their insurer.

**A note on the figures.** The training slides carried 2026 plan-year numbers: a $6,650 integrated deductible on the high deductible health plan, and $5,800 for the standard Bronze combined deductible. The Covered California Bronze page retrieved on 13 October 2026 listed that same $5,800 as a medical deductible with the $450 pharmacy deductible shown separately, and showed $7,200 for the high deductible health plan, which is also the figure the household-grouping session in the same training used. The rule is stable and the dollar figure moves with the plan year, so read it off the plan the consumer is actually looking at.

**Pharmacy deductibles.** On a plan with a pharmacy deductible, the consumer pays the whole cost of a prescription until that deductible is met, and only afterwards are the prices shared, with the amount depending on the drug's coverage tier. The training's example was a $50 individual pharmacy deductible on Silver 70. Generics are the case worth knowing: under Bronze a generic drug is a $20 copay, while tiers two, three and four run at 40 percent, up to $500 per prescription, once the pharmacy deductible has been met.

## Step 5: The annual out-of-pocket maximum

The out-of-pocket maximum is the ceiling for the year. The training put it this way: the Affordable Care Act created protections that limit what a consumer can be charged for their share, and once the individual maximum has been met, the covered services that follow are paid in full by the carrier for the remainder of the benefit year.

**What the ceiling does not reach.** It does not apply to premiums, and it does not apply to services the plan does not cover. It typically does apply to the cost sharing on covered services. On a preferred provider organisation plan there are usually separate limits for out-of-network services and providers, and the consumer's own insurer is the place to get the full detail.

**What counts toward it.** Everything a consumer pays out of pocket counts toward both the deductible and the out-of-pocket maximum, with one exception: the monthly premium. An urgent care visit, a specialist visit and a copayment for a prescription all count.

**The year, not the calendar.** The maximum applies to a plan year rather than to a calendar year that runs across two policy periods. The training's illustration was deliberately odd: an accident just after midnight on 1 January means the consumer is most likely to reach the maximum early and then has the rest of the year with the plan paying covered costs in full. And the bill still arrives, because reaching the maximum is not the same as the cost disappearing.

## Step 6: Family limits and separate groups

A family deductible is a set amount for the household, and it does not scale with the number of members. The training confirmed that directly: it is a standard figure, capped at that level, whether the household has two members or six.

**How a family limit is shared.** Every member's costs go towards the family deductible. If one member has an expensive procedure that satisfies the family deductible, the plan then shares the cost of eligible services for all the covered members until the family's combined charges reach the out-of-pocket maximum.

**The Silver 70 figures the training quoted.** Family medical deductible $10,400, family pharmacy deductible $100, family annual out-of-pocket maximum $19,600. The same three figures appear on the Covered California Silver 70 page.

**Separate groups, separate limits.** This is the part that catches people out. When household members choose different plans, they are placed in separate enrollment groups, and each group then carries its own deductible and its own out-of-pocket maximum. Two members on one application, each on a different plan, do not add up to a family deductible, and the training called that the tricky part. The worked case was a household where one member's doctors all sit in one network and another member's sit in a different one: separate them and you have two deductibles and two maximums. If everyone wants the same plan, everyone belongs in the same group, and the family deductible is shared.

How the grouping is set is covered in the household-grouping lesson. This is the money consequence of it.

## Step 7: Where your job ends

The training drew the boundary in a single sentence: explaining the numbers and what the terms mean is the whole of the job, and anything beyond that is between the consumer and the carrier.

**Inside your scope.** The four definitions. The figures on the plan's cost-share chart and in its summary of benefits and coverage. How a consumer's first few visits and their prescriptions will be charged. Where the document lives: under plan details in the shop-and-compare results, and under enrollment options in CalHEERS when a plan is being selected. And the Why This Matters column inside it, which is where a consumer can read how the household's members contribute to a family deductible.

**Outside your scope.** The price of a named drug or a named procedure. For a medication, the training's answer was to work out which of the four coverage tiers the drug sits in and give a rough idea of what the consumer will pay, and then stop, because looking up a specific drug is not the representative's job. For a procedure, give the percentage. For a rejected claim or a refused procedure, that belongs to the carrier's grievance route. Setting up a health savings account, or checking what is in one, is also outside: you cannot see the account and you cannot see the balance.

**And no recommendation.** Explaining the numbers is not choosing the plan. The training noticed that a $0 deductible on Gold or Platinum is often the thing that decides it for a consumer stuck between two tiers, which is precisely why the explanation has to be accurate: the figures do the deciding, and the consumer does the choosing. The metal-tier lesson covers how to compare without recommending; the cost-sharing version of that is the same discipline applied to the numbers on this page.
