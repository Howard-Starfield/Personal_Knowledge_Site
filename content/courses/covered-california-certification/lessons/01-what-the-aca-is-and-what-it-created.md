# What the ACA Is and What It Created

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Explain what the Affordable Care Act is, in one sentence, and name the four things it set out to do.
- List the access protections it created for people who buy insurance on their own.
- State the cost rules it placed on insurers: premium equality by age, the age-based premium ratio limit, the ban on coverage caps, the annual out-of-pocket maximum, and the minimum share of premium dollars that must go to care.
- Explain why a state can run its own marketplace and when California did.

## Step 1: The law, and the four things it was trying to fix

The Affordable Care Act is federal health-reform legislation, signed into law in March 2010. It is also commonly called Obamacare, and the marketplace California operates under it is called Covered California. In consumer conversations the three names mean the same thing, and the training's advice was to say so politely rather than to correct anyone.

The law set out to do four things: expand access to health care, bring down costs, standardise what a plan must cover, and make the system work more efficiently. Hold on to those four, because every provision in this lesson is one of them in practice.

Think of the pre-ACA world as a market with no floor under it. If you had diabetes, or cancer, or any other pre-existing condition, an insurer was entitled to say no. If you then got sick, an insurer was entitled to drop you. If you were healthy and cheap to cover, you were fine — which meant the people who most needed insurance were the people least able to get it.

## Step 2: The access protections

Five protections matter most for someone helping a consumer.

| Protection                               | What it means for the consumer                                                                                         |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Guaranteed issue**                     | An insurer may not refuse to sell coverage because of a pre-existing condition.                                        |
| **No cancellation because you got sick** | Insurers may not drop a policyholder who becomes expensive to cover.                                                   |
| **A right to appeal**                    | Insurers must have an appeal process for coverage decisions and claims.                                                |
| **Age 26**                               | A child can stay on a parent's plan until they turn 26.                                                                |
| **Exchanges**                            | Online marketplaces, run by a state or by the federal government, where individuals and small businesses buy coverage. |

There is a sixth change that belongs on the list because it decides which door a consumer goes through: the law also **expanded Medicaid eligibility to 138% of the federal poverty level**. That figure is worth memorising separately, because it is the number that moves a household between two programmes.

## Step 3: The cost rules

The access protections say a consumer can get a plan. The cost rules say what that plan may charge, and what the insurer must do with the money.

| Rule                                | The detail                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Same price for the same age**     | Premiums must be identical for everyone of a given age, whatever their health history. Premiums may vary by age, but not by condition.                                                                     |
| **The age ratio limit**             | A premium for a 64-year-old may not exceed three times the premium for a 21-year-old adult.                                                                                                                |
| **No coverage caps**                | Annual and lifetime caps on essential benefits were banned.                                                                                                                                                |
| **An annual out-of-pocket maximum** | Every health policy must cap what an individual or a family pays out of pocket in a year, premiums excluded. Once the cap is reached, the insurer pays the rest.                                           |
| **Minimum spending, and rebates**   | Insurers must spend at least 80% to 85% of premium dollars on health care. If they spend less, the excess must be returned to policyholders as rebates. Before the law, insurers were spending around 60%. |

Two details in that table are easy to get wrong, so they are worth separating.

First, the age rules are two separate rules, not one. "Same price for the same age" is about _health history_ — nobody pays more because of their diabetes. "Three times" is about _age bands_ — it limits how much more an older person can be charged. Neither rule limits the other.

Second, the 80% to 85% figure is a floor on what insurers spend, and the rebate is the mechanism. The consumer never sees the percentage; they see a rebate in their envelope when an insurer has underspent.

### The analogy that makes it stick

Imagine a restaurant. Before the law, the restaurant could keep your money and serve you badly, and you had no way to complain — you were lucky to get a table at all. The law says three things: everyone gets a table, the menu has to contain the same basics at every restaurant, and the restaurant has to spend at least 80–85% of what you paid on the food. What it does not do is tell you which restaurant to go to. That part is still yours to decide, which is the subject of the next lesson.

## Step 4: A state can run its own marketplace

Each state could choose to operate its own marketplace as a state-based exchange, or to let the federal government operate one. California chose to run its own, and it opened for business with its first open enrollment period in October 2013. The site is `coveredca.com`.

The training noted that the eligibility and enrolment rules would mostly be the same from year to year, so a consumer should not expect the ground to shift under them every January.

## Review

Close this lesson and write down, from memory:

1. The four things the ACA set out to do.
2. The five access protections, and the Medicaid figure that decides which programme a consumer lands in.
3. The five cost rules, including the 80–85% figure and what happens when an insurer falls short of it.

Then check the two areas people consistently miss: the difference between the two age rules, and the fact that the rebate — not a premium refund — is what the 80–85% rule produces.
