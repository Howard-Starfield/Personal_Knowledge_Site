# Consumer Relations and Resolution

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

The front desk takes the first call. This branch takes the ones the front desk cannot finish.

## What you will be able to do

- Say what the Consumer Relations and Resolution branch exists to do, and name the three sections inside it.
- List the kinds of dispute the appeals section takes, and say how an appeal can end in two different ways.
- Walk an escalated case through the seven stages it passes inside Escalations Resolution.
- Explain what the Special Projects Unit does about duplicate enrolment, and the three shapes its fix can take.
- Explain why a formal complaint counts as an escalation, and who has to file it.
- Say what leaves your desk when you escalate, and what stays with you.

## Step 1: What the branch is for

**Consumer Relations and Resolution** — CRNR for short — is the branch that carries work the service centre cannot close out. Its stated remit is four things:

- appeals
- formal complaints
- urgent and high-profile escalations
- special projects

Alongside the consumer work, it feeds advice into policy, technical and training processes across the organisation. It states its purpose plainly: service to the consumers with the biggest access-to-care needs, delivered at the highest level of customer service, and held to integrity and respect. Improving the consumer journey is a continuous goal rather than a finished thing.

**Who works there.** An office technician, analysts, managers, health program specialists and a branch chief.

**An analogy.** Picture a large workshop with a counter at the front. The counter fixes what can be fixed in ten minutes. Everything else goes through to the workshop floor, where the job is longer, involves a carrier or a county rather than a phone queue, and is not finished in one call. CRNR is the workshop floor.

## Step 2: Three sections, three different jobs

| Section                     | What it is there for                                                                                  |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Appeals                     | Works and settles consumer appeals, trying an informal fix before anything heavier.                   |
| Escalations Resolution (ER) | Resolves the hardest eligibility and enrolment problems, working directly with carriers and counties. |
| Special Projects Unit (SPU) | Runs the individual projects, including duplicate enrolment and financial discrepancies.              |

> ★ **The pair of names that sound alike.** **PSU** is the **priority support unit** — the queue your escalation reaches, and the middle rung of the escalation ladder; the training also calls it the priority service unit. **SPU** is the **special projects unit** — one of the three sections above. The training session blurred the two for its first minute, and then the branch video put them right. Keep them apart on paper.

## Step 3: The appeals section and the two ways an appeal ends

An appeal is the formal way a consumer asks for an official Covered California eligibility decision to be reconsidered — usually because the marketplace decided something about their premium assistance that they dispute.

**What the appeals team does with it.** It analyses the appeal and tries to settle it informally, inside every rule, regulation, policy and procedure that applies. Two ways it can end:

1. **Informal resolution.** The appeal is filed and Covered California fixes the problem without any hearing.
2. **Formal resolution.** If the issue cannot be settled informally, or the consumer asks to be heard, the California Department of Social Services holds a hearing. An administrative law judge sits with the consumer and a Covered California representative, and the judge produces the final decision. Covered California then carries out what the judge ordered.

**Why the informal route is the one they push for.** From the appellant's side it improves the consumer experience, it is faster, and it gives the appellant a fuller explanation of the regulation when the request cannot be granted. From Covered California's side, settling it in-house is simply more efficient. There is a third case that sits oddly with both: when the problem is not an eligibility question at all — a qualified health plan issue, say — the appeal is passed to the agency that owns it, such as the Department of Managed Healthcare.

**The kinds of dispute the section takes.** The training listed these:

- enrolment into a plan for health or dental cover was turned down
- the amount of premium assistance given is wrong
- the level of cost-sharing reduction is wrong
- the consumer's information was not dealt with quickly enough
- the consumer has been said not to be a US citizen, a US national, or a lawfully present individual living in the United States
- the consumer's application was called incomplete
- the consumer has other cover, such as free Medi-Cal or cover from an employer, which stops them qualifying
- the consumer has been said not to be a California resident
- premiums were not paid by the due date
- the income is too low to qualify for Covered California cover
- a refusal to grant an exemption from the penalty for being uninsured

**Worked example — an error found.** A consumer disagrees with a determination refusing advanced premium tax credits. The appeals analyst reviews the appeal, finds an error on the application, and settles it informally. No hearing, no judge.

**Worked example — a dispute that runs on.** A consumer enrols in a plan, then reads the eligibility letter and finds less premium assistance than she expected. The service centre checks her reported income, zip code, household size and ages in CalHEERS and confirms the amount is correct. She still disagrees, so she appeals. The Department of Social Services assigns an appeal number and sends it to Covered California for review. If nothing is settled inside 30 to 45 days, the hearing is scheduled. Covered California sends the consumer its position in writing — a statement of position — to the consumer and to the judge. After the hearing the judge decides, the decision is mailed to the consumer and sent electronically to Covered California, and Covered California abides by it.

## Step 4: Escalations Resolution, the last rung

**Where it sits.** The service centre is tier one. The priority support unit is tier two. Escalations Resolution is tier three, and the last stop of the ladder. ER works on highly sensitive and complex eligibility and enrolment problems, and does it by researching the file, analysing the detail, and talking directly to carriers and counties.

**What ER covers.** Highly complex eligibility and enrolment problems; politically sensitive cases; access-to-care problems at every level, emergency and urgent and non-urgent; formal complaints; programme reviews; complex escalations; and special projects.

**Where the cases come from.** Internal escalations raised by service centre representatives and by other divisions, and the service centre is the primary source. Outside, enrolment discrepancies are reported by qualified health plans, dental plans and carriers, and by consumer advocacy organisations, the Health Consumer Alliance among them, along with county Medi-Cal offices, the Department of Managed Healthcare and other state agencies. Consumers can also submit directly through the Covered California website.

Several internal departments feed ER as well. External affairs brings highly escalated cases, sometimes raised by a state senator's office, by senior leadership or by the media. The Ombuds office brings inquiries, complaints and issues that advocacy groups have escalated for a consumer or their authorised representative. Sales brings urgent access-to-care inquiries and requests submitted by insurance agents. The Privacy Office brings consumer inquiries and requests. Executive leadership brings highly escalated inquiries addressed to the executive director, the chief deputy executive director or the director of the service centre. Marketing brings the social-media inquiries. Plan management brings special requests that carriers submit to its division.

ER is also the direct point of contact for the Department of Managed Healthcare, and the liaison point for the Health Consumer Alliance, which handles all urgent and non-urgent inquiries. Part of ER's job is researching the cause of an issue and settling it in an attempt to resolve it informally, before it ever becomes an appeal.

**The seven stages.** ER's own process for working an escalated case runs in seven steps, and they are worth knowing because they tell you what happens to the case you handed over:

1. **Intake** of the escalated issue.
2. **Research** on the consumer's issue begins.
3. **Documentation review** — what is on the record in CalHEERS and in the customer relationship management system, which is Salesforce.
4. **Analysis** of the issue, and the decision about which kind of resolution is needed.
5. **Implementation** of that resolution.
6. **Confirmation** that the implementation actually happened.
7. **Closure** — the consumer has been informed, and the case can be closed.

## Step 5: The Special Projects Unit and duplicate enrolment

SPU exists to do the work too tangled for a single case: in-depth research and analytical assignments on duplicate enrolment and financial discrepancies; complex eligibility and enrolment problems that could affect a consumer's access to cover; cross-functional support for the other two sections; and projects for other parts of the organisation, among them policy, plan management and information technology.

**The periodic data matching cycle.** SPU runs a periodic data matching process, PDM for short. Three times a year, Covered California posts a 30-day notice to consumers who hold subsidised duplicate enrolment, asking them to act so the overlap is resolved. After the 30 days, the consumers who have not acted are identified, and SPU applies a separate workflow that splits into reachable and unreachable consumers.

**Reachable consumers — the overlapping enrolment review.** For the ones who answer, SPU reviews the case variables that decide which plan is real:

- different addresses, which affect plan rating and availability
- claims utilisation, which shows which plan is actually being used
- primary contact and overage dependence, which may need several consumers on the case
- household size and projected income, which drive the federal poverty level and subsidy eligibility

Where several agents are attached to the case, the delegated agents have to be worked with too. Three shapes of resolution come out of the review:

1. **Cancel the overlapping enrolment.** The duplicate case and its health plan are cancelled, the case is closed, and one plan remains.
2. **Achieve continuity of cover.** The first plan is ended before the second plan starts, so the consumer is never left uncovered.
3. **Remove the dependent.** Where the overlap exists because a dependent is still listed on a parent's case, the dependent is removed retroactively, both plans stay active, and there is no overlap.

**Unreachable consumers.** Where the consumer cannot be reached, or will not authenticate, SPU first checks that the overlapping-enrolment notices are on file and that the 30 days have passed, and then removes the advanced premium tax credits from one of the duplicate cases. Only one case keeps a subsidy. The extra research behind that decision includes when each enrolment was created and who created it — a consumer agent, automatic plan selection, or a service centre representative — which of the two enrolments is connected to a county Medi-Cal case, and the Salesforce cases and other documents that bear on the choice. The removal starts the following month: a case processed in July has its subsidy removed from August onwards.

**The cap that makes it work.** A consumer may hold more than one plan. They may not hold more than one plan with financial assistance on it. If the consumer wants a second plan, and the reason is that some of their doctors do not take the first plan, the answer is that the second plan can stay in place without a subsidy. The same holds alongside Medi-Cal, and alongside employer cover: Covered California can sit next to either of those, but unsubsidised.

## Step 6: Where your desk ends

**You never choose the rung.** When you escalate, the escalation queue takes the call over and changes ownership, and it is the queue that decides whether the case goes to the priority support unit or to Escalations Resolution. Your part is to get the call there. If you move up to a lead or supervisor role later, changing ownership becomes yours.

**The difference in weight.** Think of the priority support unit as the queue for the harder calls that are still comparatively straightforward, and of ER as the people assigned to the ones that may need a call to a county or to a carrier and that will take time to settle.

**A complaint is an escalation in another shape.** A formal complaint covers problems with the website, problems with eligibility or enrolment, and dissatisfaction with the way an issue was resolved. When the escalation queue has refused a request as invalid, the answer you can give the consumer is that a complaint is not the final word — filing one online acts as an escalation. The difference from the service centre route is that **the consumer files the complaint themselves, online, on the form on coveredca.com**; staff do not file it for them. Once it is in, it goes to the priority support unit.

**Duplicate cases are not closed at the counter.** Before a duplicate-case escalation is valid, you confirm the consumer really does hold at least two active plans across the cases. Two open cases for the same person on a first look is not proof of that — one of them may have no plan on it. And the check does not stop at the case in front of you: you go into every case the combo search returns, including the ones showing as closed and terminated, because your quality reviewer runs the same search and compares. When the consumer says they want to keep every plan, the answer is still the same — the subsidies or the extra plans have to go.

**What the branch does once it has the case.** It reaches the carriers to find out which plans the consumer actually used services under, because if the consumer has used services under all of them, cancelling the extra plans means a carrier coming back for the full price of those services. How that is resolved is not a question you answer at the counter.

> ★ **The two duplicate-case routes.** This step is the branch's own review of a duplicate enrolment. The service centre's side of it — finding the related cases, deciding which cover the consumer is keeping, and the hand-over to the escalation queue and the team that signs it — is lesson 54.
