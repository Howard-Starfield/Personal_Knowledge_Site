# Traps and Edge Cases: Where the Obvious Answer Is Wrong

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings, cross-checked against the official Covered California site. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Name the figure that is most often quoted wrongly, say what it actually refers to, and say who computes the real number.
- Recognise the four exceptions that break the general rule, so you do not apply the rule to a case it does not cover.
- Use the terms the official site uses, so a consumer who has read the site is not confused by yours.
- Separate what federal law says from what the California marketplace site publishes, and neither misattribute one to the other.
- State the 2027 changes as what they are: planned, and not yet settled.

## Why this lesson exists

Everything else in this course teaches the main facts. This lesson teaches the
places where the main facts are a trap — where a learner who has understood
the material perfectly still answers wrongly, because the obvious answer is
almost right.

Those are the points worth spending your revision time on. A fact you already
know does not need rehearsing; a fact you _almost_ know does, because
recognition will feel like knowing right up until the question is in front of
you.

**The habit to build.** For each trap, learn the **trigger** — the wording in
the question that pulls you into the wrong answer. Then rehearse the trigger
with its correction, not the fact on its own. The correction only sticks if
you can name what would have made you think otherwise.

## Stage 1: Numbers that are not what people remember

Two figures cause more wrong answers than any other part of this programme.

**The California health coverage penalty.** The figure most people quote is
\$2,700 for a family of four. Here is what that actually is: it comes from the
**December 2025** release, and it is built from **\$900 per adult plus \$450
per child**. So it is a **2026-cycle** number, not a 2024 one — and it has
already been superseded. The Covered California penalty page now publishes a
minimum of **\$950 per adult and \$450 per child under 18**, together with an
affordability threshold of **8.05%** of projected annual household income —
and that threshold is the figure the page labels **tax year 2026**. The
penalty amounts themselves carry no year on the page.

> **The trap.** The Franchise Tax Board, which is the body that actually
> computes and collects the penalty, is quoted by the training as publishing
> **\$475 per child** for tax year 2025 and a **7.28%** threshold. So the two
> official bodies are not merely differently worded — they are given
> **different numbers for different tax years**. A consumer who is sent to the
> FTB may read \$475. A consumer who stays on the marketplace site reads \$450.
>
> **And one caveat on the FTB side, because it changes what you promise.** The
> marketplace figures are the official record's own, published by coveredca.com.
> The FTB figures are **the training's account** of what the FTB publishes:
> this package holds no FTB publication of its own, the only external source it
> may consult is coveredca.com, and its own verification pass could not reach an
> FTB or IRS publication, so the corroboration is recorded as outstanding. The
> two FTB figures are internally consistent — \$950 and \$475 for two adults and
> two children is \$2,850 — but the representative's answer does not depend on
> them being right.
>
> **What to say.** Name the tax year and the body, every time. "The Franchise
> Tax Board computes it, so use the FTB's Penalty Estimator for your figure."

**Silver's share.** The second number is Silver. It is not "the 70% plan".
Silver's value depends on **household income and household size**, and it
comes as a set of levels — **70, 73, 87 and 94**. The 94% variants are only
available to households that qualify for extra help, and that is exactly why
Silver behaves differently from Bronze, Gold and Platinum.

> **The trigger.** A question that gives a single percentage for Silver is
> testing whether you know it is a range. And a question that names a
> household size but not an income is testing whether you notice that the
> household size is only half the rule.

## Stage 2: The rules that look settled and are not

The federal individual mandate is the most mis-stated rule in the programme,
and the training itself contradicts itself about it.

The accurate position: the **federal requirement** to have health insurance
was removed, and the **federal penalty** had already been reduced to zero.
What survives is **California's own requirement and California's monetary
penalty**, in force since 1 January 2020, administered by the Franchise Tax
Board.

> **The trap.** A consumer who hears "the federal mandate was repealed" and
> stops there concludes that nothing is required and nothing is owed. That is
> the wrong answer to the question consumers actually ask, which is _what
> happens to me_.
>
> **What to say.** Keep it factual and keep it out of politics: the requirement
> that applies to this consumer is California's, the penalty is the FTB's, and
> there are exemptions — including for people who cannot afford coverage, for
> religious conscience, and on appeal.

## Stage 3: Exceptions that break the general rule

Four rules have an exception, and in each case the exception is the thing a
consumer will actually hit.

| The general rule                                                | The exception                                                                                                                                  |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Outside open enrolment you need a qualifying life event         | **American Indian and Alaska Native consumers may enrol at any time** — no open enrolment, no life event needed                                |
| You have 60 days from a qualifying life event                   | Losing **Medi-Cal** coverage gives **90 days**; losing job-based coverage allows **60 days before or after** the loss                          |
| A reduced-hours change does not open a special enrolment period | It still does not — the coverage and the plan both have to change. A move _within_ California qualifies only where the available plans changed |
| Report a change within 30 days                                  | A **Medi-Cal** enrollee has **10 days**; and if the change opens a special enrolment window, a further **60 days** to pick a plan              |

> **The trigger.** Any question that says "how long do you have". The answer is
> never one number, and the number depends on which programme the consumer is
> in and which event happened.

## Stage 4: The label you use can change the answer

A consumer who has read the Covered California site will use the site's words.
Using the training's words instead is a small thing that makes a consumer think
you are talking about something else.

| Training's wording                    | The official wording                                                | Why it matters                                                              |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "No wrong door"                       | _One application covers both Covered California and Medi-Cal_       | Federal vocabulary, not the site's                                          |
| "Mixed household"                     | **Mixed-program family**                                            | The label on the form                                                       |
| "March 2010, signed by the President" | The law _passed_ in 2010; the marketplace was _established_ in 2014 | Two different dates, two different facts                                    |
| "Colonoscopies"                       | **Colorectal cancer screening**                                     | The site's word, and only a _recommended screening_ is free of cost sharing |

> **The trigger.** A question that quotes a phrase. If the phrase is not the
> site's phrase, the consumer has been reading something else and the answer
> follows their source, not yours.

## Stage 5: Federal law the California site never mentions

Four rules in this material are real federal law that **coveredca.com does not
publish**, because the site is about the California marketplace and not about
the federal law.

- the **3-to-1** limit on age-based premiums
- the **80% to 85%** floor on the share of premium dollars insurers must spend
  on care — 85% in the large-group market and 80% in the small-group market —
  and the rebates when they fall short
- a child remaining on a parent's plan **to 26**
- an insurer **not cancelling** coverage because a member became ill

> **The trap.** A representative who says "coveredca.com says that" about any
> of these is misattributing a federal rule to a state site. A consumer who
> goes looking for it on the site will not find it and will think it was
> invented.
>
> **What to say.** "That is federal law rather than something on our site."

## Stage 6: The free-service carve-out has a condition

Preventive services carry no copayment, no coinsurance and no deductible.
But the carve-out is narrower than it sounds: it applies to a preventive
service carried out **as a recommended screening** — mammograms, colorectal
cancer screening, wellness visits, and the additional preventive services and
screenings for women, including approved contraceptive methods.

If something is **found and treated** during that visit, the ordinary cost
sharing can apply. And a required benefit _category_ is not the same as free
care: the category must be covered, and the preventive services inside it are
the part that is free.

> **The trigger.** A question with the word "always" in it, or one that names a
> procedure without saying it was a recommended screening.

## Stage 7: The 2027 changes, and how firmly they are stated

The official record publishes what is planned for 2027, and it marks every item
as **contingent on court decisions**. So are they. Two different official
pages carry this, and it is worth keeping them apart.

The **Important Changes** tracker carries the dates:

- **Enhanced federal premium tax credits end on 31 December 2025.** From 1
  January 2026 monthly premiums may be higher for some enrollees.
- **From 1 January 2027** financial help for immigrants narrows to certain
  groups, such as lawful permanent residents.
- **From 1 January 2028** full income reporting and repayment apply.
  Beginning with the 2026 tax year, filed in 2027, an enrollee who received
  too much financial help may have to repay the full amount, and it is no
  longer capped based on income.

The **21 July 2026 rates-and-plans announcement** carries the money:

- California's own credit for 2027 is **\$300 million**, up from **\$190
  million** for 2026, and it reaches households at or below **200%** of the
  federal poverty level — about **\$31,920** for an individual and **\$66,000**
  for a family of four.
- The preliminary weighted average rate rise for 2027 is about **9.9%**, and
  the announcement puts the Covered California enrollment at **1,785,000** as
  of March 2026.
- More than **500,000** Californians are projected to receive the state
  subsidy, roughly **200,000** more become eligible than in 2026, and nearly
  **200,000** can choose between two Silver plans at no premium.

Separately, a **12 June 2026** ruling vacated eight provisions of the federal
marketplace rule, and California has not adopted the federal shortened
open-enrolment window — which is why the window still runs the full November
to January.

> **The trap.** These are stated firmly in the training, and one of the
> sessions hedged about them and said the rule had been read off a website
> rather than owned as policy. Present them as plans. If a consumer asks
> whether a change is final, the honest answer is that the record marks it as
> contingent.

## Review

Close the lesson and write, from memory, the answer to each of these:

1. A consumer asks for the penalty figure for a family of four. What is the
   figure you quote, and what is the mistake in quoting it?
2. What does the Franchise Tax Board publish, and how does it differ from the
   marketplace site?
3. Name the four rules that have an exception, and the exception in each case.
4. Which of the rules in this course are federal law that the California site
   does not publish?
5. What does "contingent on court decisions" change about how you present the
   2027 changes?

Then do the thing that makes this lesson work: for each trap, write the
**trigger** — the wording that would have pulled you into the wrong answer —
next to the correction. A trap you can only correct after seeing the right
answer is not yet learned; a trap you can correct because you noticed the
trigger is.
