# Quick Sort and the County: When to Refer

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the instructor's own emphasis in the training.

## What you will be able to do

- Say what a quick sort is, who gets one, what it asks, and what its result decides.
- State the one case in which the service centre contacts a county, and what happens in every other case.
- Deliver the line you use when a consumer asks you to call the county for them.
- Explain why an after-hours message has to be left immediately.
- Write a note that shows what the consumer asked, what you did, and why.

## Step 1: What a quick sort is

A **quick sort** is a short screening run for a **brand-new applicant** — a consumer with no application on file and no eligibility determined yet. Its result decides the routing of the call.

The training flagged the number of questions, and the session that walks through the tool lists them: **six**.

1. How many people are in your family?
2. How many children are under the age of 19?
3. Are any of your family members pregnant?
4. Are any of your family members 65 or older?
5. Are any of your family members disabled?
6. What is your annual income?

The result comes back as one of two: **transfer** (the consumer should be transferred to the county) or **no transfer** (a case has been created for this request). When the result is a transfer, you choose the consumer's county and language from the drop-down, and the county telephone number and a tracer identifier appear for you to use.

**What the result does not mean.** The six answers are enough to say that a consumer **might** qualify for Medi-Cal. They are never enough to say that they **are** eligible, and you do not say so. The training's example was a consumer who found an income figure of about $100,000 on their application, when they said they only made about $30,000 — and the training's point was that this is exactly the kind of thing the disclaimers and the application are there to sort out, not something a quick sort decides. The wording is that it looks like they might be eligible, or that their programme eligibility could be Medi-Cal.

**Who gets one.** The training's description of the trigger was partly garbled by the automatic transcription, so two things are safe to hold on to: it applies to a new applicant, and it applies where the consumer has no existing case and no household member already established on Medi-Cal. The training also stressed that the consumer's projected income or circumstances do not stop you running it — you can perform a quick sort regardless of those.

## Step 2: The one exception

> ★ **F9 — The service centre does not call out for the county, and the county does not call for the consumer, except in a quick-sort case.** The training stated this boundary three times, which is usually a sign that it is the one most often crossed.

So there are two directions of contact, and outside a quick sort neither of them happens:

- The service centre does not ring a county office on a consumer's behalf.
- The county does not ring the consumer on the service centre's behalf either.

Inside a quick-sort transfer, the sequence is: put the consumer on a brief hold, call the county line that matches the consumer's county and language, and warm-transfer them. On the line you say that you are calling from Covered California, that you have a consumer who might be eligible for Medi-Cal today based on a quick sort you performed, and you offer what you have — household size, how many children are under 19, pregnancy, a family member 65 or older, disability, income, plus the consumer's date of birth and best callback number if they gave one.

Two things on that screen are **not for the consumer**. The county number that appears and the tracer identifier stay with you. The training was explicit that the tracer identifier is for your eyes only. The practical reason is easy to see: if a consumer rings that number on their own, the county picks up, works out it is not a Covered California representative, and sends the consumer elsewhere — which the training called a red flag meaning one of us handed the number over by mistake.

One more boundary on the same call: the county worker may ask for the consumer's full social security number. It is not yours to fetch. You do not put the consumer back on hold to go and ask for it; you say that the consumer will provide it to the county after the transfer.

## Step 3: Everything else is a referral

For every case that is not a quick sort, the answer is the same, and it is a **referral**, not a transfer:

- Give the consumer the **county's phone number**, taken from the county listing on the Covered California site.
- If they want it, give them the **in-person address** of the county office as well.

The training's reasoning for the address was worth hearing: county offices observe holidays, close early, or close unexpectedly, and a consumer who is tired of holding will sometimes walk in rather than hold. Walking in may mean a wait, and making an appointment is entirely reasonable — but it is often faster than the queue.

**Worked example.** A consumer already has Medi-Cal, has their benefits card in hand, and wants to know who their doctor is. Eligibility is already determined, so this is not a quick sort. You look up their county on the site, give them that county's number, and offer the in-person address. You do not call the county for them.

**Worked example.** A consumer wants a three-way call with the county so they do not have to hold on their own. The training's answer was a flat no on the three-way call, and the reason given was practical rather than policy: you can hold for about four minutes at most, and the next caller is waiting. What you offer instead is the county's number and the address.

## Step 4: Saying no without turning the consumer away

> ★ **F10 — The line about outbound calls is the one the training called a white lie, and the coaching about it was about warmth.**

Consumers ask. They say they have been on hold for hours, they are crying, they want you to call the county. The training's answer has three parts, and the order matters.

1. **Apologise, and say plainly that you are not able to make that call.**
2. **Give the reason as a property of the work, not a personal choice.** The service centre's system is set up to accept incoming calls; it is not set up to place outgoing ones. Say that it is the system, and not you.
3. **Say it warmly**, so the consumer does not feel turned away.

The training named this a white lie, and the reasoning is worth carrying with you: the line is not the literal whole truth, because the system can in fact place some outgoing calls. It is the honest description of what this service does for consumers, and it is the reason that belongs in your mouth.

**Why the warmth is not a nicety.** The training was clear that consumers who feel refused stop trusting you and stop pursuing the referral. The reason you give and the way you give it decide whether the consumer makes that call to the county at all. A consumer who hears a refusal will not make the call. A consumer who hears a limitation of the service, and who has been handed the county's number and address, usually will.

**Two practical points that came up alongside it.**

_Your own name._ You do not give out your last name. You are obliged to give your first name, and if anything the first initial of your last name, and nothing beyond that. What you can give is the **reference number for the call**, which takes the consumer back to the case. If a frustrated consumer asks for your surname or your employee ID, you answer with the reference number, your first name and your last-name initial, and you do not let the conversation turn into a fight about it.

_If the consumer drops off._ If the consumer disconnects during a county transfer, you call them back once. If they do not answer, you leave a message if the system allows it, and you give them the county's number anyway, taken from the website rather than from the internal line.

## Step 5: Why the timing matters

An after-hours message left for the county has to be left **immediately**, because it affects the consumer's eligibility date.

The mechanism is the reason. The date of the call can be used to work out the month the consumer's eligibility begins. If there is no record that the consumer tried to get eligibility for that month, it is not backdated to it. The message is the footprint.

**Worked example.** A consumer calls at 7 p.m., after the county office has closed, and wants to be assessed. The transfer result comes back as a transfer. Because the office is shut, the call goes to the leave-a-message option rather than to a person. If that message is left straight away, there is a record of the attempt on the day it was made. If it is left tomorrow, there is a record of tomorrow.

The steps the training walked through were:

1. Put in the consumer's code and call the county line for the quick sort.
2. Select the option for leaving a message.
3. Leave the consumer's name, the consumer's telephone number, the county code, the language code and the tracer identifier, and say that you are calling from Covered California and that this is a quick-sort call.
4. If the system asks for specific items rather than a free-form message, follow the prompts it gives.
5. Take the consumer back off hold and tell them a message has been left.

And then hand over the county's own number as well, because a county that calls back may not do it quickly, and the consumer standing in their own queue is often the faster route.

## Step 6: Document the call

> ★ **F12 — Document everything: what the consumer asked, what you did, what you told them, and why.**

Whatever the outcome, it gets written down. The four parts are what the training named, and each one covers a different thing going wrong later: the request, the action, the explanation given, and the reasoning behind the action.

The quick-sort case is the trap. The case **closes itself automatically** when the transfer is set up — status closed, case reason met account, sub reason quick sort, resolution transfer to county. That can look like a reason not to write anything, because the record seems to be already there. It is not. You still post your notes.

The training modelled an acceptable note for a quick-sort call, and it contained roughly this:

- The consumer called to begin a new application and to find out what they qualify for.
- They were confirmed to be a brand-new applicant.
- The quick sort was performed, along with the answers to the six questions.
- A transfer result was received.
- The county was reached, and the county eligibility worker took over the call.

If you are asked about the call afterwards, the reference number and the quick-sort record already hold most of the detail. So the note does not have to repeat every field on the screen. What it has to do is show what you did and why.
