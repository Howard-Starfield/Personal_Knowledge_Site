# Escalation Handling

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the instructor's own emphasis in the training.

## What you will be able to do

- Say what an escalation actually is, and name the two desks an escalated case passes through.
- Run the sequence in order: notes, reference number, lead line, escalation queue.
- Ask for the hold the way the training wanted it, and give the consumer the reference number before the second hold.
- Fill in the escalation template, and say where it goes and which fields you can still change.
- Name the three things you must not write down, and why.
- Say where your job stops when the case is handed over.
- Decide whether a change is an escalation, a carrier matter or a county matter.
- Tell a consumer what to do about a reinstatement after a non-payment termination, and what to do about a carrier whose system disagrees with ours.

## Step 1: An escalation is a hand-off, not a bigger queue

An earlier lesson teaches when to escalate. This one is about what happens once you have escalated, and that part is a set of steps in an order, not a judgement call.

There are two desks, and they are not the same desk.

- **The lead line.** Your team's lead. The lead does their own research and decides whether the case is an escalation at all.
- **The escalation queue.** The team that takes the case once it has been approved, and sends it up to the Provider Services Unit.

> ★ **L46-1 — The lead line comes first, every time.** The training stated the rule as a standing order for the T-Tech role: the escalation queue is never the first call you make, whatever the circumstances, and the lead line always comes before it. It is a rule about the order of the two contacts, and it does not bend for an urgent-looking case.

## Step 2: The sequence, in order

1. **Post your notes** using the standard template. This is required, not optional.
2. **Give the consumer the reference number** for the call.
3. **Reach the lead line.** The lead posts their own notes and does their own research, because they have to, to be sure the case is going to the escalation queue appropriately. Then the lead says one of two things: the case is rejected, with the reason named; or the escalation is approved, because everything you have said adds up and it holds up against the knowledge base, and you may now telephone the escalation queue.
4. **Call the escalation queue.** Escalation takes it over. It goes up to PSU, and PSU is assigned the case.

Note what step 3 is. The lead does not rubber-stamp you. The lead can send you back, and if the lead says the escalation is not valid, it is not valid and you go back to the consumer with what you have.

## Step 3: The hold, and the reference number before it

The training's best practice for the holds around an escalation is worth following exactly, because the quality check reads it.

**The first hold.** Tell the consumer why you need to put the call on hold, and wait until they have said yes before you do. Then place them on hold. You have up to four minutes at a time, and you use that time to do your notes and post your template and make sure you are good on it.

**Coming back.** Thank them for holding, and say that you need to place them on one more hold, because you need to reach your next level of support.

**The reference number, before the second hold.** Before you put the consumer on that second hold, ask whether it is okay to give them a reference number for today's call. Give them the number, then place them on the hold, and go.

> ★ **L46-2 — The reference number comes before the second hold, and missing it is expensive.** The training was blunt about the consequence: if you do everything else perfectly — the notes on point, the process followed, the lead line reached, the escalation queue reached — but you do not give out the reference number, you are docked for the entire escalation part of your quality assessment. It is a small thing to do and a large thing to leave out.

## Step 4: The escalation template

**How often.** The standard template is posted every time you reach out to the lead line or to any next level of support, for anything at all — a general question or an escalation.

**Where it goes.** In Post, which is the Feed. It does not go in the notes. The training was asked this directly and gave a direct answer: under Post.

**What is in it.**

- The primary contact's name and phone number. These are already on the case, so copy and paste them from the CalHEERS case. If the consumer prefers to be contacted on a different phone or an email, put that instead.
- The other cases you researched. If you researched none, put N/A. If you did research some, list the ones that were helpful and relevant to what you are posting — a call record you read because it mattered to this case. No cases that were not relevant.
- The CalHEERS case number.
- Whether it is a mixed household. If the consumer is on Medi-Cal or is a mixed household, the answer is no.
- Why you are contacting them. This is the substance of the contact.
- The actions you are requesting.

**What you can still change.** You can update the subject, and you can add the carrier and the escalation reason. The description is the one field you cannot edit, so anything you need another desk to read has to be in it at the first attempt.

**The steps to resolve.** You can go back and edit your steps to resolve for roughly the first hour after you close the case. It can lock at any time inside that window, and once it is locked you cannot edit it. The way round that is to put anything extra in Post.

**The order of operations.** Complete and save your documentation before you reach out to the escalation queue. The training's guidance was to be detailed, specific, clear and concise in the notes.

## Step 5: What you do not write down

There are three things the training asked you to leave out of your documentation, and it asked firmly.

1. Do not document that you **authenticated** the consumer.
2. Do not document that you **combo searched**.
3. Do not document that you **completed the required case review**.

The reason is a trap of its own. If you document that you did one of these and you did not in fact do it, or did not successfully do it, you will be docked for your documentation. The training's instruction was to leave all three out of the record: do not write that you checked the caller's identity, do not write that you searched the system for matching records, and do not write that you finished the case review.

## Step 6: Where your job stops

This is the part that goes wrong most often, and it goes wrong in both directions.

**The hand-off.** When the escalation is valid, the escalation queue takes over the call. It goes up to PSU, and PSU is assigned the case. From that moment your job is to stop. Chasing the consumer is no longer your duty: you do not follow up with them, and you do not telephone them with updates. That is PSU's job, and PSU is the team responsible for closing out the case.

**Do not close your case.** When you pass the call over to the lead line or the escalation queue, you keep your case open. You do not close it. The training flagged this as a mistake new representatives make, because the temptation is to click close case as soon as the call has gone. Please do not.

**Afterwards the case is theirs.** Its status moves through the PSU statuses — new, in progress, pending carrier, pending customer, pending help desk ticket, closed — and that is the other team's to manage, not yours. The timeframes they work to are one to ten business days for the initial contact once the escalation has been transferred to the PSU queue, and one to thirty business days for the resolution.

## Step 7: The date changes, and when they are escalated

The service centre does not change a consumer's start date, and it does not change the consumer's end date either. Both are escalations, and both have a short, closed list of grounds.

**Termination date change.** You do not change anybody's end date unless the consumer has died, or unless there is a provable error at our end that the consumer can prove. If someone asks for a 9/30 end date because they cannot afford October and have employer coverage, that is not escalated. The earliest end date the service centre offers is the end of the current month.

**Mid-month termination is a different thing.** It requires carrier approval, and even then the carrier is not obliged to give the money back.

**Effective date change.** This is the mirror image of the termination change, and the discipline is the same: you do not change the start date yourself. You escalate it when the consumer has given birth, adopted or fostered a child, and when there is a provable error by Covered California.

**Worked example.** Someone gives birth today and calls in five days. The start date can be 10/20. They do not have to wait until 1 November. The baby is covered under the mother for the first 31 days, and if the mother's plan does not cover the baby adequately, the parents can choose a separate policy for the baby starting from the date of birth, adoption or fostering.

## Step 8: The cases that belong to the carrier

**Reinstatement after a non-payment termination.** The training flagged this as a big change that was only a couple of weeks old at the time, so it was new to everybody on the floor. Any reinstatement due to non-payment goes **directly to the carrier**. It used to be that you escalated on the consumer's behalf.

What you do now is short and it is the same every time:

1. Confirm that the consumer was terminated for non-payment by their carrier.
2. Tell the consumer to call their carrier and make their past-due premiums to them.
3. Tell them the carrier will then contact Covered California to get them reinstated.

**The one exception.** If the carrier is on the line with the consumer, and that carrier representative is authorised to approve a reinstatement, then you escalate. There are two tests and both have to pass: is the carrier on the line, and are they authorised to approve reinstatements due to non-payment. Having somebody from the carrier on the line is not enough on its own. If the carrier representative says they are not authorised, then it is done — you are not escalating, you continue the conversation with the carrier.

**A reinstatement that is not a non-payment issue** is escalated, and that is a different route.

## Step 9: The cases that belong to the county

**The did-not-apply case.** A consumer in a mixed household who is listed as did not apply. You cannot report a change on a mixed household, so the consumer has to contact the county, ask them to correct the did-not-apply entry, and then call you back so that you can escalate for reinstatement.

Why the county has to go first: the county, in the middle of redetermining the consumer's eligibility, marked them as not applying for coverage, and that automatically terminated their coverage. Before you can reinstate anybody, their application has to be updated, and the application in a mixed household is the county's to update.

The training was honest about how it feels: the consumer is asked to be patient with something that is not their fault, and the training's answer is to apologise, say you understand how frustrating it is, and give them the steps. It also said what it hoped for — the day when Covered California is no longer associated with Medi-Cal. For now, we are partners, and we have to cooperate with each other as best we can.

## Step 10: The 834 resend, and its waiting period

An **834 resend** is the training's name for the case where the carrier is missing information about the consumer's enrolment, and the escalation asks for that information to be sent to the carrier again.

**The trigger is a disagreement between two systems.** The carrier's system shows one thing, our system shows another, and somebody has to get everybody on the same page. The training's examples:

- The carrier shows a different date of birth.
- The carrier shows a different first and last name.
- The carrier shows a different address.
- The carrier does not show the consumer as active at all, and so will not let them pay their premium.

**The waiting period.** It has to have been more than 10 business days since the last transaction feed. If it has been fewer than 10 business days, the consumer has to wait until the 10 business days have run out, and if the issue is still happening then, you can escalate for the 834 resend.

**Worked example.** A consumer's plan is shown as active in the carrier's system while our system shows it cancelled. The consumer cancelled as never effective, made a binder payment of $991.81, and the carrier will not give the money back. The carrier keeps sending premium bills for a plan the consumer believes they hold, and has told the consumer to call us so that we can send some information back to them. That is an 834 resend.
