# The Four Metal Tiers: Comparing Without Recommending

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Name the four standardised plan categories and the average share of health-care costs each one is expected to pay.
- Explain what the percentage actually measures, and what it does not promise.
- Explain why Silver has to be given as a range rather than a flat figure.
- Help a consumer compare the categories by asking probing questions, without recommending one.
- Correct the belief that Platinum is automatically the best plan.

## Step 1: Four categories, one set of benefits

Covered California standardises plans into four metal categories: Bronze, Silver, Gold and Platinum. The name tells you nothing about the price of the plan. It tells you how the plan and the member intend to split the cost of care.

| Category     | Average share of health-care costs the plan is expected to pay | Monthly premium | What the member pays when care is used |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------: | --------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| **Bronze**   |                                                            60% | Lowest          | Highest                                |
| **Silver**   |          70%, rising as high as 94% depending on the household | Middle          | Middle                                 |
| **Gold**     |                                                            80% | Middle          | Middle                                 |
| **Platinum** |                                                            90% | Highest         | Lowest                                 |

Every one of the four offers the same essential health benefits. That is the entire point of the standardisation: a member is not buying less protection by choosing Bronze. They are buying a different shape of payment. What changes is the balance between the amount paid every month and the amount paid at the point of care.

Two related facts that keep surprise off a call. Preventive care is free in every category, not only in the cheapest one. And every plan sets a yearly limit on what a member pays out of pocket, so even on Bronze a member is never responsible for the whole cost of their care.

One thing to know so you are not caught out by a consumer's question: the official site also lists a minimum coverage plan. It is not a fifth metal category. It is available only to people under 30, or to people who qualify for a hardship or affordability exemption.

## Step 2: What the percentage actually means

The figure is the average percentage of health-care costs the plan is expected to cover, as opposed to what the member pays out of pocket. So on a Bronze plan the plan picks up roughly 60 of every 100 dollars of covered care and the member picks up about 40. On a Platinum plan the split is nearer 90 and 10.

Read the number as an average across everyone in that category, not as a promise about a single bill. What a member actually pays depends on the care they use, the plan's deductible, and whether the provider is in network. The official site is blunt about the larger point: there is no single right choice, and the best level depends on the person's budget and how much care they expect to use.

Here is the whole trade in one line. A Bronze member pays the least each month and the most every time they pick up a prescription, see their primary care doctor or use the emergency room. A Platinum member pays the most each month and the least per service. Gold and Silver sit between them.

**The analogy.** Imagine four ways of paying for the same year of meals. One guest pays a small amount every month and then a large amount at every sitting. Another pays a large amount every month and then almost nothing at the meal. The food is identical. Only the shape of the payment changes, and the right shape depends entirely on how often the guest eats.

## Step 3: Silver is the category that moves

Three of the four categories carry a single number: 60, 80 and 90. Silver does not, and this is the point most people get wrong.

The training gave Silver as a flat 70%. The official record publishes Silver as a range of 70% to 94%, and the range depends on the household's income and size. The reason is that enhanced Silver plans exist: the official site lists Silver 73, Silver 87 and Silver 94 alongside Silver 70, and each one lowers cost sharing for the households that qualify for it. The more help a household qualifies for, the more of the cost the plan carries.

Two consequences follow, and both matter on a call.

1. **Never describe Silver as the 70% plan.** That is what Silver 70 covers. The accurate description of the category is a range from 70% to 94%.
2. **Where a household lands inside that range is not yours to decide.** It is worked out from income and household size when the application is processed. Your job is to describe the range and let the application settle it.

The official site also makes clear that anyone may choose a Silver 70 plan, and that the extra savings on care costs come only with the enhanced variants. A household that qualifies for an enhanced Silver plan and chooses Bronze instead still keeps any premium help it qualifies for, but gives up the extra savings on the cost of care. That is a trade the consumer gets to make, not one you make for them.

## Step 4: Ask, do not recommend

This is the conduct rule that gets broken most often in practice, and the training flagged it repeatedly.

A representative does not pick a category for a consumer. Recommending a plan, or steering a consumer towards a particular category, is steering, and it is outside what the role is for. The rule is not to be careful with your wording. It is to let the consumer's own answers decide.

So what do you do instead? You ask probing questions, and you let the answers point at the category:

- How often do you go to the doctor?
- How many specialists do you see?
- How many prescriptions do you pick up regularly?
- Or do you go once or twice a year for a check-up, with no prescriptions and not many specialists?

Someone who rarely uses care will land on a lower-premium category. Someone who sees several specialists and fills regular prescriptions will land on a higher one. You have not steered them. You have given them the information they need in order to see which shape fits them.

This is not a private habit of the training. The official site's own coverage-level selector asks the consumer two questions along the same lines: how much they expect to use their coverage in a typical year, and whether they would rather pay less each month or less when they get care. The answers produce a suggestion worth exploring, and the consumer still decides.

## Step 5: The question you will be asked, and the reply

You will hear this constantly. A consumer will say some version of: what do you think is best? Or: what do most people get? Or: based on your experience, which one should I pick? Or: I will just go with the one you suggest.

The reply is short and it is scripted. You are going to ask a few questions first.

Do not answer the question they asked. Answer the question they need answered, which is which category fits the way they use care. And keep it professional. The training's line is that the goal is simply to make health insurance affordable for the consumer; everything else is detail.

**The misconception to kill.** Platinum is not automatically the best plan. It is the best fit for a household that expects to use a lot of care and can carry the higher monthly premium. For someone who sees a doctor once or twice a year and takes no prescriptions, a lower-premium category can leave them better off overall. The category that covers the most is not automatically the category that suits the person.
