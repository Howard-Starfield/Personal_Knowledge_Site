# Income Documents and Payment Timing

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the emphasis the training placed on them.

## What you will be able to do

- List the income documents to ask a consumer for, and say when the attestation form is the right answer.
- Explain who has to upload those documents and how long the consumer has.
- Name the household questions that have to be answered before the money can be worked out.
- Say what the income figure the consumer enters does and does not decide.
- Tell a consumer when their plan starts, and what happens if the first payment never arrives.

## Step 1: What to have ready before you start

The training read out a short list of what a consumer should have to hand before the application:

- the social security number of everybody who has one
- the most recent federal income tax form, for anyone who files a tax; or an immigration document for somebody who is not in the United States
- employer and income information, such as pay stubs or self-employment records
- a home and mailing address, and a telephone number

The official record carries the same four items in a table: social security numbers for applicants who have them, federal tax information for applicants who file taxes, immigration documents for non-citizens, and employer and income information for applicants who earn money.

★ **Nothing is handed in at the start.** The official enrolment guidance is that you do not need to submit documents up front, because what you have told the application is checked against government records. If something further is needed, Covered California comes back to you with the specific document it wants. The training made the same point from the other side: a new customer still has to upload income evidence, but the upload happens later, and the consumer is the one who makes it.

## Step 2: Which income documents to ask for

The training's list was short: a W-2, a pay stub, a 1099, and the rest of the income information.

The official proof-of-income page sets out the same documents for employer wages, and says that in most cases one document is enough:

| Document                     | What it has to show                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pay stub                     | Your name or other identifying details, the income amount, the pay period or its frequency, and the date of payment                                                                      |
| Federal Income Tax Form 1040 | Your most recently filed Form 1040 with any appropriate schedules, and the tax year                                                                                                      |
| Wage or income tax statement | The W-2 and the 1099 family — 1099-MISC, 1099-G, 1099-R, 1099-SSA, 1099-DIV, 1099-INT and 1099-NEC — carrying your name, the income amount, the year and the employer where there is one |
| Employer statement           | Used when no other formal document is available; it has to be on company letterhead, signed by the employer, and dated within 45 days of submission                                      |

★ **The attestation form is the last resort.** The training's rule was that the attestation form is for people who cannot provide other documents. The official record says what that form is: the Attestation of Income, No Documentation Available, which is accepted case by case, signed under a penalty of perjury, valid only for the benefit year it was asked for, and renewed every benefit year.

## Step 3: The consumer uploads, and the clock is running

The training's line was flat: you cannot upload on the consumer's behalf. It is their own responsibility to do it.

★ **You cannot upload the documents for the consumer.** What you can do is show them where the account says something is still outstanding, and tell them how long they have.

The consumer signs in to their own account and can upload whenever they want to. The account also tells them what is still missing. And that is why a case shows the income as not verified: where the system could not check the figure against its records by itself, something has to be provided.

**How long.** The training's figure was 30 days from the application date, and it hedged the number when it first raised it, so carry it as the training's figure rather than as a rule you can quote blind. The same window sits around the payment: the training described the first payment as due straight after the application, or within the next 30 days.

## Step 4: The household questions the money depends on

The training ran the screening questions in front of the class: the size of the household; how many of the children are under 19; whether anybody in the family is 65 or older; whether anybody in the family is disabled; and the household's annual income.

It then read out the household that the application wants:

- a spouse or registered domestic partner, and anyone else living in the home
- children under 21 living with you, including stepchildren
- parents or stepparents who live in the home with a child under 21
- anybody named on the family's income tax return, if you file one
- the other members of the tax filing household
- any family member living with you whom you claim as a dependent on somebody else's tax return

Alongside it sit the filing-status question — single, or married filing separately — and the question of whether anybody claims the consumer as a dependent on their tax return. How the household is built for eligibility is covered in the application lesson; what belongs here is what the answers are for.

★ **A person who does not want coverage still goes on the application.** The training was explicit about it: the people go on the application even when they are not applying for coverage themselves. The reason is the money. The household income figure decides which programme the household qualifies for and how much financial help it gets, and a household with a person left off it produces the wrong figure and therefore the wrong help.

**The tax duty that comes with the money.** A consumer who has been given advance premium tax credits has to file a federal income tax return, and a consumer who has been given the California Premium Subsidy has to file a state one. The official financial-help page puts the same condition on the state subsidy, which is available only to households that qualify on income and agree to file taxes. And there is a consequence at tax time: the advance premium tax credit on the 1095-A is weighed against the income actually earned, so a lower income can bring a credit or a refund and a higher one can mean repaying some of the help.

## Step 5: What the income figure decides, and what it does not

★ **The number the consumer enters decides the preliminary answer; the documents decide the final one.**

The training's framing was that on the strength of the figure the consumer gave, they are qualified for Covered California or Medi-Cal, but it is up to the IRS and the county to verify and check whether they really are. The official record says the same thing from the other end: the reported income is compared with IRS and Social Security Administration records, and only where the two do not match is a document asked for.

So the document does not set the income. It confirms the number that was already entered. That is why the household answers and the income figure have to be right before you ever reach the document step.

## Step 6: When the first payment lands, and what happens if it does not

The training's sequence: once the application is submitted, the first payment follows straight after, or the consumer has the next 30 days to make it. Making the payment takes a little while, and coverage then takes effect from the start of the following month. The training's illustration was a plan taking effect on November 1st.

★ **If the first payment does not arrive, the plan does not start.** The training's wording was that the plan will only start the following month, once the payment has been made. The official billing guidance is blunter still: coverage does not begin without the first payment, so anything the consumer received before the plan started is not covered and comes out of their own pocket.

**Who gets the money.** The bill comes from the insurance company, not from Covered California. The first payment can be made from the account with Pay Now, and that option is only available for the first payment; otherwise the insurer mails a bill and the consumer pays the insurer by the due date printed on it, which usually takes about two weeks for the bill to arrive. Questions about the premium, the due date and what a visit costs belong to the carrier as well.

**And if the payment never comes at all.** Outside open enrolment, a consumer who wants coverage again needs open enrolment or a qualifying life event to apply. Deliberately not paying is not one of them. The training's reasoning was that losing coverage by not paying is not the same as losing it by coming off Medi-Cal, which does count. The official record agrees: a missed payment can lead to cancellation, and to get insurance back you may have to wait for open enrolment or for a qualifying life event.
