# Who Qualifies, and Why You Never Predetermine

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the instructor's own emphasis in the training.

## What you will be able to do

- Say what has to be true of a plan before Covered California will offer it, and what every such plan provides.
- List the three criteria a consumer has to meet, and say which one allows for a future intention.
- Explain why financial help is a discount rather than a ticket to the door.
- Apply the rule that you never predetermine eligibility, including the scripted way to say it.

## Step 1: What a qualified health plan is

A **qualified health plan** is an insurance plan that is approved by Covered California, provides the essential health benefits, follows the established limits on cost sharing — deductibles, copayments and out-of-pocket maximum amounts — and meets the other requirements of the Affordable Care Act.

And on top of those four conditions: every qualified health plan meets the law's requirement for having health coverage at all. That requirement has a name, **minimum essential coverage**, and the official glossary defines it as a health insurance plan that meets the Affordable Care Act's requirement for having health insurance.

Think of a qualified health plan as a building with a sign on the door. The sign stands for four things at once: the right body has approved it, it carries the standard set of services, its prices are capped in the standard way, and it obeys the rest of the building code. A consumer does not check the four things one at a time. The qualification is the seal.

**Worked example.** A consumer asks whether a plan they saw advertised will work for them. The answer is that you do not vet the plan service by service against the rules yourself — you find out whether it is a qualified health plan. If it is, the essential health benefits, the cost-sharing limits and the rest of the requirements have already been dealt with.

## Step 2: The three eligibility criteria

A consumer qualifies for a qualified health plan through Covered California when all three of these are true.

1. They are a **citizen or national of the United States**, or a **non-citizen who is lawfully present** in the United States.
2. They are a **resident of California**, or **intend to become one**.
3. They are **not incarcerated**, other than incarceration **pending the disposition or judgment of charges**.

Three features of that list are worth noticing.

The first criterion has two halves that are alternatives, not a sequence: you either are a citizen or a national, or you are a non-citizen who is lawfully present. Applying it is not a matter of finding the strongest version of the claim.

The second criterion looks backwards and is not. _Intend to become a California resident_ is enough. Someone who has moved here and is settling in, or who is about to move here, is not excluded for having not yet completed the move.

The third criterion has one narrow exception, and the exception is worth its width: a person who is incarcerated **pending the disposition or judgment of charges** is not barred. That covers someone who has been arrested and is awaiting their case, which is a very different situation from someone serving a sentence.

## Step 3: Financial help is not a condition of enrolling

Consumers do not have to be eligible for financial assistance in order to enrol in a qualified health plan. The two are separate.

The official record puts the same point from the other side: having other health insurance does not disqualify you from applying for Covered California, though it may affect whether you qualify for financial help. So the two questions — may I buy a plan, and may I get help with the price — are asked and answered separately.

Think of the shop being open to everyone and the subsidy being a discount on the way in. You do not need a discount to come through the door, and you do not need to be sure you qualify for one before you walk in.

**Worked example.** A consumer says they are embarrassed to apply because their income is low and they think they will not qualify for help. The answer is that help is a discount, not a condition. They can buy a qualified health plan without it, and applying is how the question gets answered either way.

## Step 4: Never predetermine the outcome

> ★ **F4 — Never predetermine the outcome.** You do not decide eligibility on the call. You submit the application and let the application decide.

Even at 99.9% certainty — and that figure is the training's own, not an exaggeration to make the point — you do not tell a consumer they cannot apply. You submit the application.

The training scripted the phrasing, and the shape of it is worth keeping: **state the requirement plainly, then say you can still submit the application and let it show the consumer what they qualify for.** The point of stating the requirement is to be honest about what the rules are. It is not a reason to end the call.

So the sentence has two halves and both are needed. Drop the first half and you are withholding information. Drop the second half and you have refused the consumer.

Two refinements the training added, and they are the ones that get forgotten:

- **You may note informally that eligibility looks unlikely.** You can tell a colleague, in the course of the call, that based on the conversation this consumer is unlikely to qualify. That is a heads-up, not a verdict, and it is never the answer you give the consumer.
- **If the consumer would rather not spend the time, that is fine.** Offering to call back later is reasonable. But if they say go ahead, you submit the application anyway.

The rule is not "be right about eligibility". It is "let the application be the thing that decides".

**Worked example.** A consumer tells you they have been in the country for eight months on a work permit and have not yet heard back. You know how the citizenship or lawful-presence criterion works, and your honest read is that they will not qualify today. What you do is say the requirement plainly — that a consumer has to be a citizen or lawfully present in the United States to qualify for Covered California — and then say that you can still assist them with submitting the application to see what they qualify for. If they would rather not, you offer to call back later. If they say go ahead, you submit it.

## Step 5: What it looks like in practice

A few operational consequences of the rule, from the training:

- **The disclaimers have to be agreed to before an application can be submitted**, whether the consumer is on the phone or doing it themselves on the website. The only thing to establish with a consumer on the phone is whether they agree — yes or no. You are not collecting answers on this call; the application collects the answers.
- **Human error is expected.** People get their income wrong. People forget to include a household member. The training said so plainly: people make mistakes, and that is part of why the documentation habit matters.
- **The shopping tool is an estimate; the application is the decision.** The tool on the site produces an estimate built on what the consumer typed in. The application is what produces the result. They are not the same instrument, and telling a consumer that a shopping-tool estimate is their answer would be the same mistake as predetermining, only tidier.
