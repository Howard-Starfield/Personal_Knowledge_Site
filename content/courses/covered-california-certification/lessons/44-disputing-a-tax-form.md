# Disputing a Tax Form

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Work out whether a consumer should have received a form at all, and route them to the right body when they should not.
- Separate a reprint from a dispute, and say why filing a dispute for a reprint is a mistake.
- List the errors on a form that are worth disputing, and check one against the enrollment history before filing.
- Tell a consumer how long a dispute takes and what the review can and cannot change.
- Write a dispute that carries what it has to carry when reinstatement is being asked for.

## Step 1: Reprint, dispute, or no form at all

There are three routes, and the order in which you ask the questions decides which one you pick.

The first question is not what the consumer wants. It is whether this consumer should have received a form at all.

If the answer is no, the job is to explain which rule stops it and to point the consumer at the body that did issue their cover. If the answer is yes, the next question is whether the document already exists in their online account — if it does, the fastest remedy is access rather than another document. After that, the question is whether the document is right. A correct document that the consumer wants again is a reprint. An incorrect document is a dispute.

The training was blunt about the classic mistake: filing a dispute when a reprint would do. It puts the case onto a list that staff would rather keep clear of, and it leaves the consumer waiting for a review that was never needed.

The official record offers a useful first check of its own, because a form that looks wrong is sometimes right. Help of zero for some months can mean a missed premium ended the cover for those months, while help of zero across the whole year can mean the consumer paid the full price, in which case the form is accurate as it stands.

Nobody is owed a Covered California 1095-A or 3895 when:

- the cover never came through Covered California at all
- the plan is a minimum coverage plan, which the official page calls a catastrophic plan
- the cover came through the Covered California for Small Business programme, or through Medicare or Medi-Cal
- the employer's plan covers 50 or more employees
- the plan was bought straight from a carrier outside the marketplace

In each of those cases the consumer's question goes elsewhere: to the county, to the employer, or to the carrier. A consumer on a catastrophic plan may get a 1095-B from the plan's issuer, and the training noted that the issuer is not obliged to send one, and that the consumer can still file their return without it.

Form 3895 belongs to tax years 2020 and 2021 only. Do not open a 3895 dispute for a later year.

**And one case where a dispute is exactly right.** A consumer on a plan with a zero-dollar premium can find their enrollment status stuck at pending for the whole year even though the carrier accepted the plan selection weeks earlier. Nothing was paid, so nothing triggered the status to move, and no form is generated at all. The training's worked example had a reviewer approve the dispute, change the status from pending to enrolled, and the consumer get a new 1095-A. That is a fault in the system, not something to wait out — do not tell the consumer to give it time.

## Step 2: What makes a dispute worth filing

A dispute is the route for a genuine mistake on the form. The training's list of reasons worth filing on:

- the assistance amount is wrong
- a coverage start or end date is wrong
- a household member who should be on the policy is missing
- information in Column A or Column B is missing
- the demographic details on the form are wrong
- the form was never received

What is not a reason: disliking the tax result the form will produce, having cover that overlaps, or wanting the document sent somewhere else. A consumer angry about a penalty needs the right process, and for a penalty that process is a tax professional rather than a dispute.

**Verify before you file.** Compare what is printed on the form against the enrollment history held in the system. The consumer's own account of what happened is not enough on its own to establish that a form is wrong.

## Step 3: The window, and what the consumer should expect

- A dispute can reach back seven tax years. The earlier session on these forms spelled that out as the previous seven tax coverage years, with 2018 already outside the window as of January 2026.
- A dispute can take up to sixty days to resolve.
- It cannot be sped up — not by a lead, not by a supervisor.
- Disputes are worked through in the order they arrive.

Say the sixty days up front. A consumer who learns about the wait on day forty has been given a deadline they cannot meet, and the earlier session on these forms had a word for what a complaint against a named enroller brings with it.

The official record describes the consumer's own route. The dispute is submitted on the online form, which needs the case number, the health plan name and the primary member's details. A confirmation follows once the request has been received, and if the review finds a mistake, corrected tax forms are sent for the filing. If no confirmation arrives, the official page gives the service centre number, (800) 300-1506.

> **One uncertainty to carry.** The recording tied the opening of a dispute to the January 31 issue date, but it referred to January 31 in two different years. Check the benefit year against the official record before you tell a consumer when they are allowed to dispute.

## Step 4: Changing the record, and what a change cannot undo

Two processes do different work, and the data decides which one you use.

A **report of change** handles the demographic data: name, social security number, date of birth, and the contact details on the account. A **1095-A dispute** handles the things that change the numbers on the form: coverage dates, monthly premiums, and the financial assistance totals.

There is no button that edits a form that has already been produced. When the training said a field may be able to change, it meant one of these two processes rather than an edit of the document itself. A home-address change needs a full report of change where a mailing address, phone number or email address can be handled with the shorter version, and in a mixed-program family only the name change for a Covered California enrollee can be completed that way, with the other demographic corrections going to the local county.

The limits are worth stating plainly:

- A demographic correction updates the current year, and once the change is completed a corrected 1095-A comes out for the benefit year before it.
- A correction to the income or household size reported for a year that has already closed does not change the tax credit the consumer has already received for it. The application can be corrected; the tax result cannot.
- Income and tax filing status cannot be changed retroactively for the same reason, and a consumer cannot ring up and ask for that in order to escape a penalty.

So a consumer who wants to update their income so that a prior year's penalty disappears cannot be helped that way. Say so plainly, and point them to a tax professional.

## Step 5: Nonpayment, reinstatement and the carrier's decision

Termination for non-payment ends the plan. Reinstating a plan means asking for the cover to be put back in place, and it is a separate decision from the dispute about the form.

The sequence the training walked through:

1. The consumer falls behind on premiums.
2. The carrier waits until its grace period has ended before terminating, and then reports the termination.
3. A reinstatement request requires the consumer to pay back all the past-due premiums and bring the account up to date.
4. The carrier decides. It can refuse, even when the consumer has the money ready.

The training's example put the figures on screen: premiums for September through December, put at $1,608 in the session, and a carrier that refused reinstatement even though the consumer was willing to pay. The consumer was left carrying the $784.94 of assistance shown on the form.

**A dispute about the form does not restore cover.** A reviewer may ask the carrier whether it will reinstate, but the carrier's answer is the answer. Where reinstatement is refused, the consumer uses the form as it stands and settles the assistance at tax time. Where the carrier does reinstate, it gives the consumer thirty days to pay the balance, and the reviewer then approves the dispute and sends a corrected form.

**What the dispute has to contain.** The training's example of a weak dispute said only that the columns did not accept the proposed values and asked for columns A and B to be changed. It was denied, because nobody reviewing the file can read a reinstatement request into a request to change two columns. A dispute that asks for reinstatement has to say so, and has to record the consumer's agreement to pay the listed past-due premiums.

**Worked example.** A consumer's cover was terminated on September 30, and the carrier did not act until December. The dispute said the columns were wrong and asked for them to be changed. It was denied, because it never asked for reinstatement and never recorded that the consumer was willing to pay the four months. The gap was in the comment, not in the consumer's situation.

> **One uncertainty to carry.** The recording names the team that works these cases as the priority support unit in one session and as an escalation queue in another, without expanding the abbreviation. Treat the internal label as unverified and work to the process rather than to the name.

## Step 6: Requests that are not disputes

The training listed these as requests that are not 1095-A disputes:

- a reprint
- an emailed copy
- help finding the form online
- Franchise Tax Board proof of coverage
- a challenge to the pre-populated Column B amount
- a change of tax filing status
- a request that the consumer's own tax return be reviewed for errors, which
  sits with their tax professional rather than with this service

Each one has its own route, and the point of listing them is that choosing the wrong one wastes the consumer's time and does not answer what they actually need.

Column B deserves a note of its own. The programme fills it in with the second-cheapest silver plan on offer to the household, and it is not a field the consumer can dispute. What can be disputed is a column with information missing from it, not a value the programme filled in.

There is one more case that catches people out: a consumer ringing about a Franchise Tax Board penalty notice for tax year 2022 or later. Do not submit a 3895 dispute for it, and do not send them to the online 3895 dispute form. The proof of coverage they need comes from the Franchise Tax Board itself.

**When the consumer asks about a penalty.** The training's rough figures were up to $900 for one person and up to $2,700 for a family of four, and the training said in the same breath that the numbers can vary and that knowing them is not your job. The amount in front of the consumer comes from the Franchise Tax Board and is dated to a tax year, and the two official bodies publish different amounts for different tax years, so a figure never travels without its body and its year attached. And a consumer who went without cover can be charged twice over: the assistance on the form is settled at tax time, and separately the training put the allowance at ninety days, so four months without cover is past it and a penalty for having no cover can follow. The line to use: I can tell you what the form says and how to get it corrected. What it means for your tax return is a question for a tax professional.

## Review

Close the lesson and write down, from memory:

1. The three routes, and the question you ask before choosing between them.
2. The five situations in which nobody is owed a Covered California form.
3. The errors on a form that are worth disputing, and the one you check first before filing.
4. The window for a dispute, how long it takes, and why that cannot be shortened.
5. Which data goes through a report of change and which goes through a dispute.
6. The thing a correction to a previous year's income cannot do.
7. Why a dispute does not restore cover, and what the dispute has to say when reinstatement is wanted.
8. The requests that are not disputes at all.

Then check the two things people miss: that a dispute is not the answer to a missing copy, and that a dispute may be denied even when the consumer is right about what happened.
