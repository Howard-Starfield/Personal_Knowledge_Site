# Employer Coverage and Covered California for Small Business

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- State the employer shared responsibility rule: who it applies to, what the employer has to offer, and what happens if it does not.
- Say who Covered California for Small Business serves, in terms of headcount and location.
- Explain the fifty-to-one-hundred employee band and why a business can sit inside both rules at once.
- Route a caller who says their human resources department sent them, and say what you hand over.

## Step 1: The employer mandate

Two different things hide under the phrase "employer coverage", and a call about one of them is not a call about the other.

The **employer shared responsibility rule** is part of the Affordable Care Act. A business with **50 or more full-time equivalent employees** has to offer health coverage that is both **affordable** and provides **minimum value** to its full-time employees and to their dependent children under 26. A business that does not do this pays the **employer shared responsibility penalty** instead.

Think of it as a speed limit with no third option. A business under 50 full-time equivalents is not subject to the rule. A business at 50 or above is: either put a qualifying offer in front of your people, or pay the penalty for not doing so. You cannot simply ignore it.

Two details are worth carrying. First, a full-time equivalent is a calculation, not a headcount — part-time hours are folded in, so two businesses with the same number of people can sit on opposite sides of the line. Second, the training did not go into the numbers. The official record fills them in: a qualifying offer has to reach at least 95% of full-time employees, minimum value means the plan is expected to pay for at least 60% of the total average cost of covered services, and affordability means the employee's premium does not exceed 9.96% of household income. The same official page makes the point that the IRS, not Covered California, decides whether a penalty is owed.

## Step 2: Covered California for Small Business

**Covered California for Small Business (CCSB)** is the small-business side of the marketplace. It offers health insurance plans to businesses **domiciled in California** with **up to 100 full-time equivalent employees**.

Imagine the marketplace as two counters in one building. The individual counter sells to people and households. The small-business counter sells to companies. Same building, same organisation, different counter — and a caller who arrives at the wrong counter cannot be dealt with at the other one. That is the whole of the next section.

The official record adds detail the training did not cover. A business qualifies on four grounds: size (100 or fewer full-time equivalents), having at least one employee who receives a W-2 — not the owner and not the owner's spouse, the majority of its eligible employees living or working in California, and offering coverage to all eligible employees. Employee eligibility then runs on hours: 30 or more hours a week is eligible, 20 to 29 hours is the employer's discretion, and fewer than 20 hours is not eligible through CCSB.

The referral number the training talked about is on the official record as well. **Covered California for Small Business: (844) 332-8384** for new customers, Monday to Friday, 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. There is a separate line, (855) 777-6782, for existing customers working on renewals and their accounts.

## Step 3: The fifty-to-one-hundred band

This is where the confusion lives, and it is worth drawing the picture before talking about routing.

The employer shared responsibility rule starts at **50** full-time equivalents. Covered California for Small Business serves businesses with **up to 100**. So a business with, say, **60 employees** sits inside both bands at the same time: it may have an employer obligation to meet, and it also has a small-business marketplace it can buy in.

| Headcount                           | Employer shared responsibility rule | Small-business marketplace                  |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Fewer than 50 full-time equivalents | Generally not subject               | Generally eligible                          |
| 50 to 100 full-time equivalents     | Generally subject                   | Generally eligible                          |
| More than 100 full-time equivalents | Generally subject                   | Not eligible — a large-group market instead |

The word _generally_ is doing real work in that table and should not be dropped. Whether the employer rule applies turns on a full-time-equivalent calculation and on the circumstances of the business, not on a number quoted down a phone line. And whether a business qualifies for the small-business marketplace turns on all four of its own requirements, not on the headcount alone. The official record also makes the penalty decision the IRS's, not Covered California's.

So the honest summary is: which of the two doors applies is a question to ask, not a question to guess at.

## Step 4: Routing a small-business caller

**The routing rule.** The service centre does not assist with Covered California for Small Business. A caller who says that their human resources department told them to call, or who talks about coverage through their employer, is almost always a small-business caller — and the answer is to give them the small-business number rather than to guess.

> ⚑ **This is a judgement call the training gave, not a rule written in policy.** The training described it as the way calls actually arrive: the phrasing to listen for, the number to hand over, the habit to form on the phone. Treat it as a practical habit rather than as a quoted regulation.

In order, that looks like this:

1. **Listen for the signal.** Coverage through their employer. Their human resources department sent them. They are asking about a plan for the company rather than for themselves.
2. **Check the case system.** Search the caller by first name, last name and date of birth. A small-business caller will not have an application on file, which confirms it.
3. **Ask one question if the picture is still unclear.** Whether the business is at or under 100 full-time equivalents. Then give the small-business number either way rather than guess.
4. **Hand over the number and the hours.** (844) 332-8384, Monday to Friday, 8 a.m. to 5 p.m.

**Worked example.** A caller says their human resources department told them to call to talk about health insurance for the company. You search them in the case system and there is no application on file. You give the small-business number and the opening hours, and you explain that a business plan is set up through the small-business programme rather than through the service centre. You do not try to run a group's enrolment through the individual application.

One thing the training did not spell out: the variant where the caller is an employee asking about their **own** coverage rather than about the company's plan. That is not the small-business route, and the training did not walk through it, so treat it as a case to check against the knowledge base rather than one to settle on the call.

Close the lesson and write down, from memory:

1. The employer shared responsibility rule: the headcount threshold, the two conditions the coverage has to meet, who it has to be offered to, and the alternative if the employer does none of that.
2. The size limit for Covered California for Small Business, and the other three requirements the official record attaches to it.
3. The band where the employer rule and the small-business marketplace overlap, and why a business inside it is not a simple case.
