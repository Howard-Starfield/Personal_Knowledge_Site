# Renewal, Consent, Binder Payments, and Grace Periods

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Explain what the annual redetermination looks at, and why it cannot run without the consumer's permission.
- Say how long a consumer can authorise electronic verification for, and what a consumer who did not authorise far enough ahead has to do.
- Tell passive renewal apart from choosing a different plan, and say what a consumer has to do to keep the plan they already hold.
- Work out whether a renewing consumer owes a first payment for the new year.
- Say how long the protection runs when a premium is late, and what happens when it runs out.

## Stage 1: The yearly check and the permission behind it

Once a year the marketplace runs an **annual redetermination**. It looks at whether a consumer still qualifies for the whole range of premium assistance and for the Medi-Cal programmes, and it does the looking electronically: the household's income and tax information are checked against federal records rather than sent in on paper (00:02:03–00:02:17).

That check does not happen on the marketplace's own authority. Covered California has to hold the consumer's **authorisation, or consent**, before it may verify income and tax information for the purpose of a redetermination (00:02:17–00:02:29).

Where the permission comes from is worth knowing, because a consumer who cannot find it will assume the renewal takes care of itself. It sits in the signature section of the application, where the applicant is asked whether the marketplace may carry out electronic verifications for a period of up to five years (00:02:29–00:02:43).

> **The words the training used.** The permission is called **consent**, and more
> precisely consent _for verification_. It is what allows the marketplace to look
> up whether the household qualifies for financial assistance or for Medi-Cal
> using federal sources such as the Social Security Administration's records and
> the Department of Homeland Security's records (00:03:06–00:03:42).

## Stage 2: How far ahead the consent reaches

The length of the authorisation is the consumer's choice. It can be anywhere between zero and five years, and it can be changed at any time, in either direction (00:04:19–00:04:29).

The training's advice turned on how long the consumer expects to stay:

- **Expecting to be here a while?** Push it out to five years, so the household is not revisiting the permission every single year (00:02:56–00:03:06; 00:03:42–00:03:59).
- **Only here temporarily** — waiting for an employer to offer cover, or waiting to start a job — then a shorter period is reasonable. The training still pushed five years, but accepted the consumer's judgement on this (00:04:04–00:04:25).
- **Either way**, if the household wants financial assistance or Medi-Cal and the consent has run out, permission has to be given again at some point (00:03:53–00:04:04).

**The trap that catches people out.** Consent given for one year or less does not carry a consumer through the following renewal. A consumer who authorised only a year has to go back online and give the marketplace permission again before the annual renewal process can be completed (00:02:43–00:02:56).

## Stage 3: Passive renewal, and choosing something different

Renewal happens in the open enrolment period in October (coveredca.com, How to Renew Your Plan). If a consumer does nothing at all, the official record says they are put back into the plan they already hold, no later than 30 days after the renewal deadline, on the strength of the household and income details the file already holds (coveredca.com, How to Renew Your Plan).

The training called that **passive renewal**: the consumer is put back into the same plan unless they cancel, they pick a different plan, or the plan they hold has gone off the market and they take no action to choose a replacement (00:09:18–00:09:38).

Everything about it is automatic — the application and the plan renew themselves for the coming year (00:09:51–00:10:16).

Two things a renewing consumer can still do in the window:

- Shop the plan catalogue on the marketplace site and switch if something suits them better (00:09:38–00:09:51).
- Find out whether they have become eligible for premium assistance for the first time; the renewal is where that new eligibility shows up (00:09:43–00:09:51).

**The official record adds a caution.** Because re-enrolment uses the household and income information already on file, a household whose income has moved and has not reported it inherits last year's figures into the new year (coveredca.com, How to Renew Your Plan).

## Stage 4: The first payment for the new year

A **binder payment** is the first payment a consumer makes when they take a plan, and for a brand-new enrolment it comes with a clock attached: if it is not made within 30 days, the enrolment can be cancelled (00:08:25–00:08:42). The official record makes the same point from the other side. The first payment can be made through the Pay Now option in the consumer's account, that option is only available for the first payment, and the plan will not start without it (coveredca.com, Guide to Your Bill and Payments).

**For a renewal the rule is different.** A binder payment is not required where a subsidised enrollee is re-enrolling, whether they go into the identical plan or into a different plan of the same product — such as moving from a bronze or silver HMO to a gold HMO with the carrier they already have (00:04:29–00:04:52).

What a renewing consumer does instead:

- The plan carries over into the new year; there is no new binder payment to make (00:07:23–00:07:43).
- The Pay Now button is not there for January, precisely because the plan is rolling over rather than starting (00:08:42–00:08:59).
- The premium itself may have moved, because the carrier may have raised or reduced prices, but the plan stays the same (00:07:52–00:08:15).
- The January premium still has to be paid by its due date, or the coverage is not effectuated (00:05:12–00:05:30; 00:06:16–00:06:22).

The training's worked example states it most plainly: an existing household that receives premium assistance and pays regularly in 2025, renewing with the carrier it already holds, on that same product, for 2026, owes no new binder payment in 2026 in order to keep the grace period (00:04:57–00:05:19).

> **Nothing is collected by you.** A binder payment is the consumer's own money
> going to the carrier. You point at the link; you never take the payment
> yourself.

## Stage 5: When the premium is late

Here the training's numbers and the official page do not say the same thing, so keep them apart.

**The training's figures.**

- A consumer **receiving premium assistance** gets the full three-month grace period, described in the recording as 90 days (00:04:52–00:05:19; 00:07:37–00:07:52; 00:08:59–00:09:18).
- A consumer **not receiving premium assistance** gets 30 days, and that clock only starts once the enrollee has been duly notified and billed for non-payment. Pay the outstanding premium by the end of that 30 days and coverage continues; do not, and the coverage is ended (00:06:35–00:06:54).
- Either way, before a carrier may end a renewed policy for non-payment, it must give the consumer the grace period and all the required notices (00:06:16–00:06:35).

**The official record.** The marketplace's own billing page says only that a payment which is not made can result in the plan being cancelled, and that getting cover again might not be possible until the following open enrolment period arrives, or until a qualifying life event occurs (coveredca.com, Guide to Your Bill and Payments). It publishes no number of days at all.

So: the 90-day and 30-day figures are the training's, and they are worth giving a consumer because they were taught as policy — but the carrier's own notice is what binds, and the official page does not state a period.

## Stage 6: Leaving on purpose

A consumer may pull out of a Covered California plan for any reason, including because they have picked up employer-sponsored cover or some other government-sponsored cover (00:10:16–00:10:34). Moving between Covered California plans is a different matter: outside the open enrolment window a consumer cannot change plans unless a qualifying life event applies (00:10:34–00:10:40).

**The mechanics.**

- Give the marketplace or the carrier at least 14 days' notice of the date the consumer wants to end (00:10:47–00:10:53).
- The end date offered is the **last day of the month** the request is made, whatever day of the month the consumer calls (00:10:54–00:11:15). A consumer calling on 31 October is entitled to an end date of 31 October (00:11:35–00:11:48).
- The 14 days is advice rather than a rule, and it exists for the consumer's benefit: it lets the carrier stop billing for the following month. If a carrier does bill for the next month, the refund comes from the carrier afterwards, and it takes a little time (00:11:41–00:12:45).

**The official record agrees on the shape of it.** A consumer has to give at least 14 days' advance notice, the cover stops on the final day of that month, a request made with less notice is handled case by case, health and dental are separate plans and are cancelled separately, there is no cancellation fee, and a cancellation generally cannot be backdated for months that have already passed (coveredca.com, Cancel Your Plan).

> **The mid-month trap.** A mid-month termination does exist. The training's
> instruction was to treat it as something that is not advertised and not
> encouraged, and to give the consumer the end-of-month date (00:22:24–00:22:47;
> 00:13:20–00:13:48).

## Stage 7: When the cover ends without the consumer asking

**Involuntary termination** is when Covered California or the health plan ends the coverage rather than the consumer (00:30:07–00:30:23). The training listed the reasons:

- **No longer eligible.** The consumer has moved out of state, is in custody, has died, or has become eligible for Medi-Cal instead (00:30:23–00:30:49; 00:31:15–00:31:28).
- **Documents never supplied.** A household that does not send in what was asked for can have its coverage cancelled for want of documentation (00:31:15–00:31:28).
- **Premium not paid.** A consumer who does not pay and uses up the grace period has the coverage ended by the carrier (00:30:23–00:30:49; 00:31:28–00:31:38).
- **Fraud.** Here the coverage is _rescinded_ by the health insurance company (00:30:23–00:30:49).

**What the consumer receives.** A notice of termination that says why the coverage was ended, the date it ended, and how to appeal (00:30:49–00:31:01; 00:32:36–00:32:48).

**What you do about a fraud referral.** The carrier has a dedicated line for it, and they will not ring the service centre directly. The carrier reaches the marketplace on that internal line and asks for the coverage to be ended; the request shows up on the case record as a call from the carrier. The service centre's part is to point the consumer at their health insurance company and let that company supply the detail — not to end the coverage, and not to investigate. The training put it plainly: this is rare, and the service centre does not handle this kind of call (00:31:38–00:32:36).
