# Call Flow, Your Voice, and Staying Calm

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Run a call through the same sequence of moments: greeting, hold, transfer, callback, solution, closing
- Put a caller on hold in a way that keeps them on the line, and choose between a warm and a cold transfer
- Work out which voice quality is letting you down, and fix it
- Explain what the research says your voice is actually doing on a call
- Recognise the three reasons a consumer asks for a supervisor, and the wordings that hand them the argument

Every contact runs through the same sequence of moments: the greeting, the hold,
the transfer, the call back after a dropped call, the point where you present the
solution, and the closing. The training treats each of those as a skill of its
own. Two of them carry more weight than the rest — the greeting, because it is
the first thing the caller hears, and the moment you present the solution, because
that is where the call turns from gathering information into giving an answer.

## Stage 1: The greeting is the first thirty seconds of the call

A good greeting does two things, and it is worth being clear about both, because
they are not the same thing. It creates a positive first impression — the caller
decides whether you are trustworthy — and it gives the caller a moment to refocus
while you explain what you are going to do. The caller has usually just been
navigating a phone menu, and the greeting is where they stop doing that.

**Do it within five seconds of the call starting.** The standard greeting has
three parts: thank the caller for calling Covered California, give your own first
name or a nickname, and then ask for the caller's name. Say all of it in a clear,
confident tone.

Two details that are easy to miss. The name you give has to be the same one you
use on every inbound and outbound call, because a caller may well meet you twice
and a different name each time breaks the impression you just made. And the tone
is part of the greeting, not decoration around it: a caller who is still getting
their bearings at the end of your opening has already formed an impression of
you, and it will not be a good one.

> **Why five seconds.** Researchers put the time it takes a person to form an
> impression at somewhere between 4 and 15 seconds. The training works with a
> figure of up to 30 seconds for making that first impression with a caller, and
> five seconds is how long you have to start it. A greeting that arrives late is
> a greeting that arrives after the caller has already decided something about
> you.

## Stage 2: Holding, transferring and calling back

### The hold

People dislike being put on hold, and the training is clear that it is not
vanity. A caller may already have waited in the queue before they reached you.
While they are on hold they have no idea what is happening, and they begin to
feel forgotten, or taken advantage of. Some of them hang up rather than wait,
and then have to start again.

Hold a caller when the call needs to be transferred, when you need to consult a
lead, when there is a temporary system issue to resolve, or when you need to
document the call in the case system.

The procedure has four steps:

1. Ask the caller's permission to put them on hold, and give them the reason.
2. Wait for their reply.
3. If they agree to the hold, say thank you.
4. Keep the hold to four minutes or less, and check back in with them every four
   minutes at most.

The first three are about consent and courtesy. The fourth is about the call
surviving.

### Warm and cold transfers

A **warm transfer** is one where the person taking the call over is on the line
with you and has been briefed on the situation. The briefing can happen with the
consumer waiting, or you can bring the consumer, yourself and the other person
together on one line. What a warm transfer buys you is the ability to stay on the
call with the consumer until you know they have reached an actual person.

The technique runs along the same lines as the hold technique: give the reason
you are passing the call on, which is usually that you have a team or a
specialist who handles it; ask whether that is acceptable and wait for the
reply; say that you will stay on the line until they are through; if you put them
on hold, let them know that is what you are doing; then dial and brief the other
person with the caller's name, the identifiers you have such as an account
number, and what the call is about; and finally complete the transfer, or bring
the consumer into the conversation and introduce them, before you disconnect.

If you are the one receiving a warm transfer, let the consumer know you can help
them and carry on quickly from where the previous person stopped. If they were
not present for the briefing, summarise it briefly and confirm what they need,
so that they do not have to say everything again.

A **cold transfer** is one where the customer is told that the call is passing to
somebody else, the number is dialled, and the representative leaves the line. The
new representative answers a call and has no idea that the customer has already
spoken to you. Covered California does this rarely; the usual alternative is to
give the caller a number they can call. Some assignments require a cold transfer,
and the most common request for one is a caller asking for a supervisor or for a
way to pass on praise, which tends to produce disconnected calls.

### Calling back after a dropped call

Do not call back if the caller hung up in anger, if the consumer actively
disconnected the call, or if no one was on the line when the call was answered.
In those three cases the consumer has ended the contact, and calling back is
going to be unwelcome.

When a callback is right, use the phone number on the case unless the consumer
gave you an alternate number during the call. If the case holds no number at all,
use the one from the Finesse IVR pop-up.

The order of work matters, and it is the reverse of what most people would
guess:

1. Update the status in Finesse to 14, which means customer call back.
2. Send a Teams message to your supervisor, or the backup supervisor, telling
   them you are making an outbound call. Do not wait for approval or for a reply.
3. Update the case record in Salesforce with what happened and what you did.
4. Call the consumer.

Then follow the standards that apply to outbound calls: authentication, call
recording and leaving a message.

## Stage 3: Presenting the solution and closing the call

At some point the representative has to stop asking questions and start giving
answers. Getting enough information first is the critical opening step, but the
moment you present the solution is the vital moment of the call.

The solution has to be presented in clear, positive language the caller can
understand. Four things make it land:

- **Explain why this is the right solution, and why you chose it.** This matters
  most when the solution is less than desirable or inconvenient, because that is
  when the caller is most inclined to argue with it.
- **Break the solution into steps and responsibilities**, so the caller knows how
  it will actually happen. The training's example: when you explain that a
  doctor's visits have to be approved in advance by the insurance company, the
  caller needs to know whether to ring before the appointment, once it is
  booked, or at some other point.
- **Set clear expectations**: the deadlines and time frames involved, the
  correspondence and callbacks to expect, and who is responsible for the next
  step.
- **Explain how the caller will know it worked.** This is the one that stops
  repeat calls.

The **closing** has three ingredients: check whether the consumer needs anything
else, thank them for calling, and wish them the best. Not all three are
necessary. If the consumer is angry and you were not able to do what they wanted,
a cheerful close would only grind the wound further, but thanking them for
contacting the service centre and wishing them a good or better rest of the day
can still be the right thing to do.

## Stage 4: The five voice qualities you can control

Start with what your voice is actually worth on a call. In face-to-face
communication, the split the training quotes is 55 percent body language, 38
percent tone of voice and 7 percent words. Over the telephone it becomes 5
percent body language, 81 percent tone of voice and 14 percent words.

Body language still counts when you cannot be seen, because your body position
affects your tone. You can hear a smile. Slouching restricts your voice.

There are five voice qualities to work on, and the training names them as
**volume, pitch, inflection, tone and pace**.

**Volume.** When you are in doubt, speak a little louder rather than too
softly. Project deliberately into the mouthpiece. A slightly louder voice
conveys more confidence and authority than a quiet one. It starts with posture:
sit erect, because you can breathe more deeply when you do, and breathe deeply.

**Pitch.** This is how high or low you speak. A monotone is a single tone, and
monotony is exhausting to listen to; what cures it is vocal variety. A lower
voice generally travels further, arrives more clearly and is easier to listen
to, and medical professionals tend to speak low because it sounds authoritative
and controlled, which reassures the people they are speaking to. Nervous
speakers pitch higher, and a high pitch reads as a lack of confidence. To bring
yours down: take deeper breaths, leave a pause between sentences, focus on the
lowering itself, and speak concisely and deliberately. Do not take it to the
extreme, because noisy breathing, long pauses and talking too slowly are all
their own problems.

**Inflection.** This is where you put the emphasis, and it changes the meaning.
The most common fault is uptalk: the pitch lifting at the close of a phrase or a
sentence, which makes a statement sound tentative and makes you sound unsure. The
rule is simple. At the end of a declarative sentence, the inflection stays level
or drops slightly. When you ask a question, it rises near the end. To improve it,
underline the keywords you want to stress, practise the same sentence with
different inflections, and listen to how other people do it.

**Pace.** Vary it, and mirror the other person's pace. Rushing is the fault that
comes up most often, and the impressions it creates are a con artist, a carnival
barker, a used car salesman, someone self-absorbed, or a liar. Watch the
greeting and the first thirty seconds especially. There is an enthusiasm trap
here: the coaching to sound enthusiastic turns into fast talking, so express it
through projection and vocal variety instead.

| You are speaking too fast when                                 | You are speaking too slowly when     |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| The caller asks you to say that again                          | The caller sounds irritated          |
| You run out of breath before you reach the end of the sentence | The caller frequently interrupts you |
| The caller is confused by what you have told them              |                                      |

**Tone.** Tone combines all the other four. A tone can make a person sound
insistent, or whining, or pleading, or nervous, or irritated, or authoritative,
or enthusiastic, or confident, or warm, or thoughtful, or reassuring. The tones
to aim for are happy, warm, confident and clear.

How you improve tone: breathe from the diaphragm; drink plenty of water, because
that keeps the vocal cords lubricated; avoid too much caffeine, which is a
diuretic; sit up straight; use gestures; smile, because it warms the tone
automatically; extend your range by singing; record yourself and play it back;
ask yourself whether it is a voice that says you care; practise at a slightly
lower octave, the way radio presenters do; and get feedback from someone you
trust or from a supervisor.

## Stage 5: Why consumers escalate, and the words that start it

Consumers ask to be put through to a supervisor, or to someone at the next level
of support, for three reasons, and the training groups them into three buckets:

1. The representative's own behaviour on the call triggered the escalation.
2. The consumer does not like the answer the representative gave.
3. The consumer does not want to wait for an answer.

Knowing which bucket a consumer is in tells you what to change, so it is worth
naming it out loud in your own head before you respond.

### The wording that makes things worse

- **Just saying no** to a consumer who is already frustrated. Use help instead,
  and give a rational explanation or offer alternatives.
- **"I do not have the authority to do that."** The caller answers that they
  would like to speak to someone who does.
- **"There is nothing further I can do for you."** It hands the call back to the
  consumer instead of owning it, and it frustrates them.
- **"Your supervisor will only say what I have already said."** It sounds
  defensive and aggravating, and it does not stop the escalation.
- **Blaming the consumer.** "you did not pay on time", "you did not submit your
  request in time", "you did not complete it correctly", "you did not sign the
  form" — each one makes the consumer defensive and keen to push back. "We did
  not receive it on time" and "what we received was incomplete" say the same
  thing without the accusation.
- **"That is our agency policy."** It explains nothing and makes you sound like
  a roadblock.
- **"I would like to, but my boss will not let me."** The caller will ask to
  speak to your boss.

The difference underneath all of these is between an excuse and an explanation.
Excuses are defensive and deny responsibility; explanations clarify. "Our systems
are really slow today" is an excuse. "The system needs a moment to bring up the
record" is an explanation. If you are blaming something, you are probably giving
an excuse.

### The technique for saying no

The training gives a short technique for the moment you have to say no. It
arrives as four parts, and they are worth learning by what they do:

- **Show the human touch.** Let the consumer know you get it, that you understand
  their viewpoint and their situation.
- **Say the no with we rather than I.** If you use I, the caller may think this
  is only you, and that a supervisor would treat them differently.
- **Let the consumer know why.** Give a rational explanation rather than an
  excuse, because adults want to know the reason for things. Saying that it is
  company policy does not really explain why.
- **Provide a recommendation where it is appropriate.** For example, offering to
  help them log in to their online account so that they can send in their
  documents.

One caution about the source here. The recording attaches the letter of the
acronym to a different part at different points, so the letters in it are not
reliable. The four parts above are the ones that recur, and they are described
here by what they do rather than by which letter they carry.

### Ten ways to come across as confident and attentive

1. Give good service to every consumer, whether they are nice or not and whether
   their request is reasonable or not.
2. Know your services inside out, so that you can talk with confidence and
   without hesitation — and so that at least you know you can find the answer you
   do not have.
3. Make yourself familiar with the case system knowledge articles and how to
   navigate them, so that the consumer is not waiting while you look.
4. Use active listening, focusing on what they are saying rather than on how you
   will reply.
5. Paraphrase what they said, to show that you have followed their account of
   the situation.
6. Empathise, which shows that you are listening and that you care.
7. Acknowledge the frustration, and then follow it with an action statement such
   as "let me see what is going on".
8. Show confidence in your ability to help, with positive word choices rather
   than "I am not sure what is happening".
9. Be direct, but not blunt. Directness is a different quality from bluntness.
10. Do not push the consumer.

### When the caller asks for your supervisor

Refusing the request outright is poor practice. The training suggests responses
that let you attempt to help before the call is escalated: offer to assist with
the request and transfer them to a supervisor afterwards if they still want one;
ask what the problem is so that you can transfer them to the correct supervisor;
or get your supervisor while you find out why they called. If you do identify the
situation, you can follow up by telling the consumer that your supervisor is able
to take the call.

Then, if you show empathy, listen properly and reflect the caller's words back,
explain what is happening, and show that you can solve it, the escalation may not
happen at all. And whichever way it goes, work to the agency's Escalation Task
Guide, reference SC.212, whenever a call is escalated.
