# The Reasonable Opportunity Period, and Reinstatement After It

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Name the reasonable opportunity period, say what the shorthand ROP stands for, and say what the period is for
- Give the training's figures for how long a consumer has to upload documents, and say which document gets the longer time
- Explain why an extension is a judgement call rather than an entitlement, and say what closes the extension route
- Say what happens to a case when the window closes with nothing acceptable uploaded
- Take a ROP termination through the reinstatement work: the window, the provable error, the places the pieces come from, and the lead line
- Say what an approved reinstatement does for the consumer's coverage, and what it does not do for their bill
- Explain why a termination for ROP is not a qualifying life event, and what the consumer can do instead

## Stage 1: The reasonable opportunity period, and what the shorthand stands for

CalHEERS is built to check a consumer's details electronically. Where it can, the
case moves on without the consumer lifting a finger. Where it cannot — income the
tax data does not match, an immigration status no record covers — the consumer
falls into a **reasonable opportunity period**. That is the window in which the
consumer sends the document themselves, and the coverage carries on while they do
it (01:36:06–01:36:20).

The training names it in full and then shortens it: "reasonable opportunity
period. Again, ROP" (01:35:45, 01:38:08). ROP is the shorthand the service centre
uses, and this material uses it in two ways:

| the shorthand         | what it points at                                                                         |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| the period            | the window in which the consumer has to upload what the system could not check for itself |
| a termination for ROP | the case that ran out of time, was ended by the system, and can be put back               |

The long session is the one that expands the name. The short session works on a
case that has already been terminated for it.

**The period protects the consumer.** During it the consumer does not become
ineligible because the system could not check. The training's line is that the
consumer stays eligible until the documents are in (01:38:16–01:38:24). So the
question on the call is never "am I still covered?" — it is "by when does this
have to be sent, and what happens if I miss it?"

## Stage 2: How long the consumer has, and the extension

The training gives two figures, and it gives them twice. **95 days for most
documents. 155 days for proof of income** (01:36:12–01:36:31, 01:38:08–01:38:16,
and again in the summary at 02:00:09–02:00:24). On one pass the speaker said 145
before settling on 155 in the same breath; the figure to carry is 155.

**These are the training's figures, not published policy.** The official record
publishes no number of days at all: its pages on documents, on citizenship and on
income say what may be requested and what the document has to show, and say
nothing about how long the consumer has or how much longer they can get. Lesson 30
and lesson 41 both say the same thing — read the deadline off the record rather
than from memory.

**An earlier session gave the flat figure, and the two do not agree.** On 15 October
the training put the period at 95 days to submit documentation, with no carve-out
for income at all (session 2026-10-15 10-56-52, 00:52:43–00:53:10). A
representative trained on that version would tell a consumer they had 95 days for
their proof of income. The split — 95 days for most documents, 155 for income — is
the later statement, and it is the one to carry. Both numbers are the training's
own, and neither is published policy, so the deadline on the record is what you
read to the consumer.

### The extension is a judgement call

An extension is not an entitlement and it is not automatic. The training's guidance
is to offer one when the consumer asks for one, or when the deadline is close
(01:38:42–01:38:52) — and not to assume that there is plenty of time, because the
consumer may never have had the letter in front of them.

Two things close the door on it. The first is the deadline itself: once the item
shows as overdue the extension route is shut, and a consumer who never asked for
one before the deadline does not get one afterwards (01:40:56–01:41:26). The
second is that the training does not want the length treated as settled. It says
there is no figure anyone has to stand by, and that the answer is to use judgement
(01:40:02–01:40:17); it also says outright that nobody has to memorise the
figure, because it is written down in the knowledge article (01:36:31–01:36:39).

That is not the same as the length being unknown, because the recording names it.
The extension is a **60-day extension**: a consumer who has not had one yet is
described as not having "gotten the 60-day extension" (01:41:09–01:41:20), and
the route is given — go to manual verifications, and you give them 60 days
(01:41:35–01:42:04). The earlier sessions say the same thing from the other
direction: extensions of up to 60 days (15 October, 00:52:48–00:53:10), and two
of 30 days each, both requested before the 95 days run out (15 October,
02:35:00–02:35:49).

So the open question is not the length but how the two accounts fit together, and
95 plus 60 is 155 — the figure the later session gives for proof of income. The
recording never says that is the link, so do not present it as one. What it does
say is that the knowledge article holds the answer, and that a colleague who asks
how long an extension runs should be sent to it rather than to a remembered
number.

### Every applicable person, separately

An extension is recorded for every person it applies to, one at a time. The
training's own example is a household where one member's cover is through
Medi-Cal, and the answer is still that it is done for everybody
(01:43:15–01:43:38).

## Stage 3: What happens when the window closes

The system is built to end the coverage itself. When the deadline passes and the
documents are not there, the case is terminated automatically — no decision, no
judgement about the consumer, just the system doing what it was built to do
(01:38:24–01:38:42).

The termination lands at the end of the month the deadline fell in. In the
training's worked example the deadline was 1 October, so the coverage was ended
as of 31 October (02:01:54–02:02:36).

Two things elsewhere in this package already describe this case, and the consumer
will have met both of them:

- Lesson 37: a pending verification status means the system could not check
  something itself, and the consumer is given the window to send documentation
  instead. That pending status is where this period lives on the screen.
- Lesson 30: the document request is part of the record on the case. The request,
  what is due and by when, and the note that says so are what the next person
  reads.

The termination is the system's, not a judgement about the consumer. That matters
in Stage 4, because the case may have been ended for a reason that was nobody
else's fault.

## Stage 4: Reinstatement after a ROP termination

### The window

Normally the consumer has 30 calendar days from the termination date to get the
coverage reinstated (02:01:40–02:02:45). In the training's example the coverage
ended on 31 October, so the consumer had the rest of November to sort it out.
After that, reinstating is no longer available on this route: the consumer needs
another qualifying life event, or the open enrolment period
(02:06:20–02:06:33).

The first thing is the document. If nothing has been uploaded, getting it in is
the first job, and quickly (02:01:01–02:01:19).

### The provable error

There is one door that stays open past the 30 days. If the consumer uploaded the
document before the deadline and nobody passed it, the termination was Covered
California's own error, and the case can be reinstated even after the 30 days
have run (02:04:44–02:05:58). The condition is that the document that was
uploaded is one that is acceptable.

This is why the training is so firm about the lead line. A consumer who uploads a
document and then waits has done everything a consumer is asked to do; the
document sits on the account until somebody reviews it by hand, and if the
deadline then passes, the system terminates a case that was actually satisfied
(02:03:20–02:03:38, 02:05:10–02:05:24).

### The worked example

The short session walks through a real case on the screen. A consumer was
terminated for ROP because the marketplace could not verify their U.S. citizenship
or national status, and the letter says exactly that (00:23:26–00:23:41). Her
naturalization document was already in documents and correspondence. Her coverage
had been cancelled as of 31 July, the termination was dated 10 July, and the
letter followed on 11 July (00:20:47–00:22:38). One of her coverages was
untouched, because it was not affected by ROP.

### The shape of the work

The case is not read off one screen. The training's instruction is to work it out
from the places that hold the pieces (00:21:23–00:23:51, 00:27:56–00:28:14):

1. **Account information and case history** — the ROP marker, and the date the
   coverage was actually ended.
2. **The account home screen** — whether the coverage is in fact cancelled.
3. **Documents and correspondence** — what was uploaded and when, and the letters
   that were sent out.
4. **The letter itself** — it says what could not be verified.

That is the whole method: put the pieces together until the picture is good enough
to decide what happens next (00:22:42–00:22:59).

### The lead decides the document

This is the part people get wrong. A representative does not decide whether an
uploaded document is acceptable, and does not escalate first and ask later. The
lead pulls the document up and looks at it themselves; they either pass it or
explain why it will not do and say what the consumer can send instead
(00:24:53–00:26:26).

The two questions to the lead are the same two every time: is this an acceptable
document, and if it is, may I have approval to escalate for reinstatement
(00:30:44–00:30:59). The escalation comes after the answer, not before it.

## Stage 5: What reinstatement does and does not do

**What it does.** An approved reinstatement puts the cover back as if the
termination had never happened. In the worked example the consumer is reinstated
back to 1 August, the day after the 31 July end date (00:51:13–00:51:41).

**What it does not do.** It does not wipe the bill. The consumer is still
responsible for the premiums for the months that are reinstated — the training
names the three months after the end date in that case (00:51:21–00:51:45).

The training's way of putting it is the car insurance analogy: you do not get the
money back for a month in which you did not use the cover, and you are paying for
insurance whether or not you use it (00:52:22–00:52:45).

So a reinstatement is not a free pass. The document still has to be one that is
acceptable, and the case still has to go through the lead line and the
escalation.

## Stage 6: A ROP termination is not a qualifying life event

This is the trap that catches people, and the training wants it to be clear.

A loss of coverage that the consumer could have prevented is not a loss of
coverage qualifying life event. The training names four ways of losing cover that
fall outside it: non-payment, a reasonable opportunity period that ran out, a
voluntary cancellation, and a failure to upload the documents
(00:45:46–00:47:20). It is blunt about the ROP case (00:45:59–00:46:03): the
termination "is not a loss of coverage", and the reason given is that it is a
fault of the consumer's own.

The consequence for the consumer is direct. A consumer whose cover ended that way
cannot re-enrol on the ground that they have lost their coverage. They need
another qualifying life event, or the open enrolment period, or a route outside
Covered California (00:47:20–00:47:35).

Two lessons elsewhere in this package already carry part of this. Lesson 38
teaches that a voluntary cancellation is not itself a qualifying life event.
Lesson 10 sets out the qualifying life events themselves, so the consumer who
needs one has to have one.

Two notes on the sources behind this lesson, so that you do not over-read it.

First, the figures. The 95 days and the 155 days are the training's own, and the
official record publishes no number of days at all. The extension is stated as a
60-day extension and given a route, but the training says the length is not a
figure anyone has to stand by and that it is written down in the knowledge
article.
A learner who meets a live case should read the article rather than work from a
remembered one.

Second, the recording. The long session renders the expansion of the name in a
garbled way at one point, running it into an instruction about typing, so this
lesson uses the reading the training repeats afterwards. The dates in Stages 3 to
5 are that one case's dates, and no question in this lesson depends on them.
