# Navigating Covered California for the Consumer

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

## What you will be able to do

- Sort out a consumer whose browser is fighting the site, and say what you can
  offer instead.
- Send a consumer to local help by either of the two routes, and say what that
  search lets you filter by.
- Find your way around the CalHEERS account: representatives, case history,
  cost applications, and the letters the consumer is holding.
- Pick the right application view, and explain why the other one is only a
  backup.
- Handle the financial-assistance question as the single decision it is, and
  explain what saying no actually costs the consumer.
- Pause an application and come back to it, and edit one that has already been
  submitted.
- Check a consumer's consent to electronic verification and say what it means.

## Stage 1: When the browser is the problem

The training opens on the most common technical complaint a service centre
gets: the consumer's browser will not behave.

**Safari** is the one that gives the training the most trouble. The site and
its forms misbehave there often enough that the advice is simple — if a
consumer is in Safari, **suggest Chrome or Firefox** and let them try again.
Nothing is wrong with the consumer's account or their application.

The second thing to establish is the **language** the site is working in. The
Covered California website is available in **English and Spanish only**. A
consumer who wants the site in another language is not going to find a menu
option for it, and it is worth saying so plainly rather than letting them hunt
for a language picker that does not exist.

> **What to do.** Two questions, in this order: which browser are they in, and
> which language do they want. The first one has a workaround. The second one
> changes who you route them to.

## Stage 2: Two routes to local help

A consumer who needs the site in another language, or who would rather not
navigate it at all, needs a person. There are **two ways in** to that help.

| Route                          | Where it is                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The website**                | The **Find local help** link on the Covered California site                                  |
| **The consumer's own account** | The **question-mark symbol** at the top of the CalHEERS account, from inside the application |

The account route matters because it is the one a consumer sitting on hold
with you can actually find. It is the same help, reached from a different
door.

The local help page asks a question of its own first: **do you want an agent
or an enrollment counselor?** The training presented the agent route as the
broader of the two, listing Medicare, Medi-Cal, private insurance and Covered
California among the lines an agent is licensed for, while an enrollment
counselor is tied to Covered California. Treat that as the training's
description of how the two roles differ, not as a settled statement of state
policy.

Once you are on the page you can narrow the search by **language** and by
**ZIP code**. Both filters matter, and they matter together: a consumer who
speaks Arabic and lives in a particular ZIP code is a much narrower search
than either filter alone.

> **Why it matters.** "Find local help" is not a referral to a general
> hotline. It is a filtered directory, and the filters are how you find
> someone who can actually help this consumer.

## Stage 3: The CalHEERS account you are working in

The consumer's account is where you look before you start changing anything,
and it holds more than the application.

**Account information** tells you whether an **authorized representative** is
attached to the case — a family member, a friend, an agent. A representative
can do almost everything the consumer and their spouse can do, which means
the presence of one changes what you may do without asking the consumer
directly.

**Case history** is the record of who did what, and the labels on the entries
say whether a change came from staff, from a county, from an agent, or from
the consumer in their own account.

**Cost applications** holds the original submission with its results, and a
PDF of the original application together with every application submitted
since.

**Documents and correspondence** is the one that settles a disagreement. You
can open the actual letter the consumer is holding in their hand and go
through it with them rather than describing it from memory. The letters are
produced in the consumer's **preferred language**, and where you are not
authorised to read that language there is an option to view the letter in
English instead.

That last point carries a rule with it: if you are **not** authorised to read
the language, you need an **interpreter**. If you **are** authorised to read
it, you should **not** be given an interpreter for that language. Asking for
one anyway is not a courtesy — it removes the person who is qualified to
handle the language the consumer has asked to use.

> **Why it matters.** The representative check and the language check are both
> things you can do in about fifteen seconds at the start of a call, and both
> of them change what you are allowed to do later.

## Stage 4: Two views of the consumer application

The consumer's application can be opened in two ways, and the training is
unambiguous about which one comes first.

| View                            | What it looks like                                      |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Standard consumer view**      | The application **page by page**, one section at a time |
| **Flexible administrator view** | The whole application on **one continuous screen**      |

**Use the standard consumer view first, every time.** The flexible
administrator view is the **backup**, for the call where the standard view is
not working and you need to see the whole application at once to make progress.

The limitation that follows from this is worth stating plainly: **a consumer
cannot view or edit the application in the flexible administrator view.** So
when a consumer says they would rather change it themselves, and you hand
them the flexible view, you have handed them a screen they cannot use. Their
own account shows the standard, page-by-page application.

The application itself runs in this order:

1. **Introduction** — basic information: where the consumer lives, whether
   they are willing to have their information checked electronically, and
   whether the application should test them for financial assistance.
2. **Application menu** — household and individual information: household
   members, tax information, household income.
3. **Individual information** — phone, email, preferred language, citizenship.
4. **Review and submit.**
5. **Sign and submit.**

The Introduction page asks for the consumer's **ZIP code**, how they heard
about Covered California (optional), and the **date of the application** —
which is the date of the conversation you are having.

## Stage 5: One question about financial assistance

The Introduction section contains a question that looks like two questions and
is actually one.

The question asks whether the application should determine whether the
consumer qualifies for **financial assistance through Covered California** or
coverage through **Medi-Cal**. It is a **single combined yes-or-no decision**
about both programmes together. There is no way to answer it for one programme
and leave the other open, and there is no middle answer.

So when a consumer says they want to know about Covered California financial
help but will not have a Medi-Cal check done, the answer you record is
**no**.

What **no** does is worth being precise about, because it is more than a
missing discount:

- The application drops the financial-assistance questions.
- The **tax filing and income questions disappear** from the application.
- The plans that come back are **full price**.

The consumer still completes the application. Nothing stops them applying —
what they lose is the assistance.

> **The one thing you may say.** The training is clear that you can, and
> should, warn a consumer that choosing no means no financial help. This is
> not steering: the consumer decides, and the consequence is theirs to weigh.
> What you must not do is leave them to discover it later, when the prices
> appear during plan shopping and the shock has nowhere to go.

## Stage 6: Pausing an application, and editing one you already sent

Two different procedures, and they get confused with each other constantly.

### Pausing an application that is not finished

1. **Save and Continue**, then **Exit**. You land back on the homepage.
2. Later, choose **Continue Changes** from the homepage.
3. The application reopens at the **first section** — not where you stopped.

That last point is the trap. Someone who saved a long way through and expects
to land on the last page they filled in finds themselves back at the
beginning. If the change you need to make is the financial-assistance answer,
you are back at the Introduction section, where that question lives.

### Editing an application that has already been submitted

This happens when a consumer reaches the end, agrees to the disclaimers, and
then does not like the eligibility results they got. You go back and edit.

Two facts about it:

- **The disclaimers have to be heard again.** The consumer has not consented
  to the corrected application by agreeing to the first one. Some of the longer
  disclaimers play as recordings and only need reading aloud if the playback
  fails; the short consent disclaimer is always read aloud.
- **The original submission is not deleted.** The later submission is layered
  **over** the earlier one. Both stay on the record, and the original
  continues to be the application of record.

> **Why it matters.** "The original is deleted and replaced" is wrong in a way
> that matters. The consumer's first submission is still there, the
> correction sits on top of it, and the record shows both. A later correction
> does not erase what was filed first.

## Stage 7: Consent to verify information

Early in the application, the consumer is asked to consent to electronic
verification: permission for Covered California to check their information to
find out whether they qualify for financial assistance.

**How long the consent lasts is the consumer's choice, and the range runs
from zero to five years.**

**Zero years is not a middle option. It is no consent.** The consumer can
still submit the application without it — but they will most likely get full
price with no financial assistance. The training offered this as its own
estimate rather than a certainty, so put it to the consumer as the likely
outcome and explain the mechanism, which is what makes it land.

When a consent has **expired**, the application renews itself **without
financial assistance** until somebody updates it. Nothing announces this in
advance to the consumer; it shows up later as a price.

That makes it **your job on every call**:

1. Check whether the consent has expired, or is about to.
2. If it has, or is about to, explain what it means and offer to update it.
3. If the consumer declines, carry on with the call. A consumer who will not
   consent is still entitled to the rest of the service.

> **Where the pain lands.** The training's advice on explaining this is to
> tell the consumer plainly that the consequence shows up during plan shopping
> — the prices they see are the prices they pay. A consumer who understands
> the mechanism before they reach the shopping screen is far better placed than
> one who finds out at the screen.
