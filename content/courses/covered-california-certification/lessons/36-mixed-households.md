# Mixed Households: Two Programmes, One Family

> [!note] Study notes based on the owner's Covered California training recordings. Original explanations; not affiliated with or endorsed by any organisation.

Points marked ★ carry the training's own emphasis.

## What you will be able to do

- Say what the official record calls a household that is split across the two programmes, and what the training called it.
- Work out who is in the household and who is left out, and why.
- Explain the income limits that split a family between Medi-Cal and a Covered California plan.
- List what you may do on a mixed-program case, and the one thing you may not do.
- Refer a mixed-program family to the county, and say what happens while the decision is pending.

## Step 1: What a mixed-program family is

The official record's term is **mixed-program family**: a family where different members qualify for different health insurance programmes, some on Medi-Cal and others on a Covered California plan, sometimes with help towards the premium and towards what a member pays for care. The training's own phrase for the same thing was 'mixed household', so use the official term when you are writing or speaking to a consumer.

Two things the official record is explicit about:

- **It is common, and it does not mean anything is wrong with the application.**
- **One application covers both programmes.** When a household applies, every person's eligibility is checked for both Covered California and Medi-Cal, because eligibility depends on household details such as size, income and ages.

The cost consequence is worth saying out loud to a consumer. A Covered California plan charges a premium and cost-sharing such as copays and coinsurance; Medi-Cal can cover at low or no cost. Two people in one family can therefore have very different bills from the same application.

> **A different thing with a similar name.** A household whose members differ in immigration status is a **mixed immigration status family**. That is not what this lesson is about, and the two are not interchangeable.

## Step 2: Who counts as the household

The household is the **tax household**:

1. The tax filer.
2. The spouse.
3. Anyone claimed as a tax dependent.

Two rules travel with that, and they are the ones people get wrong:

- **A spouse or tax dependent is included even when they are not applying for coverage.** Their income still counts.
- **Anyone who is not claimed as a tax dependent is left out.** People who simply live in the same house are not part of the household unless the tax return makes them dependents.

**Financial help is worked out from the household's total income**, not only from the income of the people applying. That is why the household has to be right before the results can be.

And then: **everyone applies together, and each person is assessed individually.** One application, one household, separate results per person.

## Step 3: Why families split across the two programmes

★ **The most common cause is that Medi-Cal applies different income limits to adults and to children.**

The limits are expressed as a percentage of the **federal poverty level**, or FPL. That is the annual income measure that the Department of Health and Human Services sets, and it is what those limits are written against.

| Who         | Medi-Cal income limit                  |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Most adults | Household income up to 138% of the FPL |
| Children    | Household income up to 266% of the FPL |

**Worked example.** A family's household income lands at 250% of the FPL — above the adult limit and below the child limit. The children qualify for Medi-Cal. The adults do not, and they get a Covered California plan instead. That is the usual way a household splits, and it has nothing to do with the county running two programmes side by side.

A pregnant person adds a second set of bands. Medi-Cal for pregnant individuals runs to 213% of the federal poverty level, and the Medi-Cal Access Program, or MCAP, covers from over 213% up to 322%.

## Step 4: Two worked examples

**The Robinson family.** Four household members, household income at 266% of the FPL. The two children qualify for Medi-Cal. The two parents qualify for a Covered California plan with an advance premium tax credit.

**The Smith family.** Three household members. Mrs. Smith has recently found out she is pregnant. Household income at 220% of the FPL. Their child qualifies for Medi-Cal. Mrs. Smith qualifies for the Medi-Cal Access Program. Mr. Smith qualifies for a Covered California plan with an advance premium tax credit and cost-sharing reductions. For Medi-Cal and MCAP purposes the household counts as the three people plus the number of unborn children, so at least four.

> ★ **Let the application speak for itself.** The training's instruction was not to work out from the chart what a person qualifies for, especially when they fall between two bands. The chart shows you where the household sits; the eligibility result on the application shows you what each person actually gets.

## Step 5: Declining Medi-Cal, and declaring a pregnancy

**Declining Medi-Cal.** A consumer who is eligible for Medi-Cal can turn it down. What the county does then is move them across to Covered California — and in doing so makes them **automatically ineligible for financial assistance**. Declining is allowed and it has a price, and the consumer should hear that price before they say no.

**Declaring a pregnancy is optional.** You declare it if you want to see eligibility for Medi-Cal for pregnant individuals or for MCAP. A consumer who is not interested in either of those programmes and wants a Covered California plan instead simply does not declare it.

That is why the declaration matters twice over. It decides what the consumer sees, and it decides how long the pregnancy programmes last: because she qualified because she was pregnant, a consumer on MCAP has to call again after the birth to find out where she stands.

## Step 6: What you may do and what you may not

**Check on every call.** Before you work on a case, look at the eligibility results summary on the account homepage. It shows a result for each person in the household, and any Medi-Cal result against any member is what tells you that this is a mixed-program family.

**What you may do:**

- Adjust the advance premium tax credit with the APTC slider.
- Answer questions about the Covered California enrolment, the plan, the eligibility, or the notices the household has received.
- Raise a CalHEERS issue or an access-to-care request with the right team.
- Tell the consumer how to pay the first premium for a Covered California plan.
- Complete the opt-in or opt-out for dependents who are in automatic plan selection.
- Help with a name change, and only for the members who are on a Covered California plan.
- Help a consumer who has no changes to report choose a Covered California plan.
- Terminate Covered California coverage for the Covered California members.
- Update the tax filing attestation and the consent used for verification.
- Process manual verification items, including passing documents.

**What you may not do:**

- Redetermine eligibility.
- Resubmit the application.
- Handle a case that is in soft pause or carry-forward status.
- Process a notification of deceased form (the training named it HPEX 411, though the exact title of that form was not confirmed).
- Assist with a Medi-Cal renewal.
- Process a reported change.

> ★ **You can pass a document, but you cannot decide the eligibility again.** Passing a document is the first step; the second step is the redetermination, and the second step belongs to the county. That single rule explains most of the list above.

**The name change is the odd one out.** You may do it for the Covered California member. The moment the consumer asks for anything else — income, a phone number, an address — you stop and refer them to the county. The training's advice was to say so at the start of the call, so the consumer can decide what they want before they ask.

**A negative action.** If nobody in the household is on Medi-Cal, it is not a mixed-program family and you can try to lift it — though the consumer still has to ring the county themselves, or it goes back on. If the household _is_ a mixed-program family, only the county can lift it.

## Step 7: Referring the family to the county, and waiting for a decision

In almost all cases the consumer is referred to their county's Medi-Cal office, and you give them the **public phone number for that county's Medi-Cal office**. The training pointed at the task guide on sending Medi-Cal calls directly to counties for the process.

A consumer who makes the change themselves in their own account is advised to ring their county after five days and check that the change arrived.

**Why the caution.** The training warned that a representative making changes that affect Medi-Cal recipients can damage a case, so extra care is needed on these calls.

**While the decision is pending.** A household waiting for a Medi-Cal determination **is** a mixed-program family. The Covered California member can sit in a plan while the review is pending, and the county cancels that plan if it grants Medi-Cal:

- If the county decides before 1 January, it cancels the plan before the plan starts.
- If the decision arrives late — 8 January, say — the plan is cancelled on 31 January.
- For a year already running, a determination before the end of October cancels the plan at the end of October. A cancellation needs 14 days' notice, so the automatic end date becomes 30 November, and the consumer can ask for 31 October instead.
