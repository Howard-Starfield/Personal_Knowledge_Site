    revision: 1
    status: active
    type: free-recall
    prompt: "What makes a call an inquiry-only call, and which three changes does it not authorise?"
    sampleAnswer: "An inquiry-only call is one where the consumer's purpose is to find something out rather than to have a change made. The consumer wants to compare plans, and the case is left exactly as it is. The three changes such a call does not authorise are putting the consumer into a health plan, adding a dental plan, and combining or regrouping the household. The training refused all three, because a change made during an inquiry moves the premium and leaves a record saying something the consumer did not ask for, which the training described as the call becoming confused."
    explanation: "Look for the definition of the call, all three refused changes, and the reason that a change made during an inquiry confuses the record. An answer that leaves one of the three out has not drawn the boundary, and an answer that treats the call as permission to select a plan has drawn it in the wrong place. Lesson section: Step 1: When the call is inquiry-only."

    prompt: "A consumer asks what a deductible is and then says that a zero deductible sounds like free care. How would you answer both?"
    sampleAnswer: "The deductible is the part of the covered cost the consumer pays before the insurer starts paying, so yes, it is the money the company is asking them to pay. In the training's worked example the figures were a premium of $283, a $15 copay for a primary-care visit, a $0 annual deductible and a $5,000 out-of-pocket maximum for the year. So a zero deductible means the insurer starts paying straight away on covered care, not that care is free, because the $15 copay still applies at the visit. The training's agent also got it wrong on the call, speaking the $5,000 out-of-pocket figure as though it were the deductible, and then corrected it to $0 on the record."
    explanation: "Look for the definition of the deductible, the four figures of the worked example, and the correction the session made in the moment. An answer that says a zero deductible makes care free has repeated the exact mistake that was corrected on the call, and an answer that leaves the copay out has dropped the reason the care is not free. Lesson section: Step 2: What you compare, and what you say."

    type: free-recall
    prompt: "On an inquiry-only call, what do you put on the case, and what does closing that case mean?"
    sampleAnswer: "You record what the consumer asked, what you explained, and what you deliberately did not change and why. You finish the documentation in the window you get after the call, which the training named as four minutes, using the template from the knowledge base. The detail matters more here than on most calls, because the quality check takes points off for missing detail and because you may have to explain the call later. Closing the case records that the interaction is finished and what was done on it. It is not a statement that coverage was enrolled or changed, because on an inquiry-only call nothing was."
    explanation: "Look for the four-minute window, the template, the reason for detail, and the separation between finishing an interaction and changing coverage. An answer that treats a closed case as proof of an enrolment has taken the lesson's central point backwards, and an answer with no mention of the window has missed the practical part. Lesson section: Step 5: What you write when nothing changed."
