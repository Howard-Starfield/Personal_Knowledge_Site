    sampleAnswer: "Under the article, a caller states, on a call or in a written message, that they intend to hurt any person, themselves included, or that they intend to damage or destroy property belonging to the state, and that is a threat. The target may be the consumer themselves, people in their immediate area, the staff taking the call, other Covered California employees, county workers, anybody associated with Covered California insurance agents, with Medi-Cal or with the state of California itself, and a public official or a political leader, including California's governor and the president of the United States. It also covers damage to a Covered California office building, a service centre or another county, state or entity facility. What is not a threat: having a bad day, saying a complaint is coming, saying a bad review is coming, and grounding a child for not doing the dishes."

    prompt: "What does the procedure ask you to do in the first minutes of a call from someone who has made a threat?"
    sampleAnswer: "Take the threat seriously, stay as calm as you can, listen and respond to the caller, and keep the conversation going. Reach your immediate supervisor through Teams as soon as someone makes a threat — you can do that while you are still on the call, and you do not need to hand the call over first. Say something to hold the caller while you do it. If your supervisor has not replied within about five minutes, assume they are not available, whether they are on a break, on another call or in a meeting, and call the supervisor cue, which is the backup supervisor. Do not put the caller on hold if you can avoid it, because consumers disconnect; if you have to reach the supervisor cue the caller is placed on hold automatically, and that is the one case where it happens."
    explanation: "Look for the seriousness, the calm, the Teams message to the immediate supervisor, the five minute wait before the supervisor cue, and the rule about holding. An answer that says the supervisor is called straight away has missed both the waiting period and the instruction to keep the conversation going. Lesson section: Step 5: The first minutes of the call."

    prompt: "What does the training say a representative's job is when a consumer cannot afford to keep their coverage, and what is the modelled way to say it?"
    sampleAnswer: "Retention is not the job. The centre does not want to lose consumers, but worrying about retaining members is not part of the representative's job; the job is customer service and being helpful, and whether somebody cancels is not the representative's to control. The wrong response is to hold the consumer hostage and ask them to stay. The modelled response opens with plain acknowledgement, that you completely understand and that times are tough and things change. You may then state the state mandate once, as a heads-up, in case the consumer does not know that Californians are required to have health insurance and that there is a penalty, and then you let the consumer go, regretting that they are going and wishing them a plan B."
    explanation: "Look for three things in order: the boundary, that retaining members is not the job and that whether somebody cancels is not the representative's to control; the opening, a plain acknowledgement with no plea for the consumer to stay; and the one fact you may add, the state mandate, once, as a heads-up, with the penalty noted. An answer that treats keeping the member as part of the job has the boundary the wrong way round, and an answer that only acknowledges the difficulty and never mentions the state requirement has left out the heads-up. The penalty amounts are not taught in this stage, and the training's account of the 2027 penalty change in the same recording is not taught here either, because the recording does not own that rule. Lesson section: Step 9: Helping a consumer who cannot afford to stay."
