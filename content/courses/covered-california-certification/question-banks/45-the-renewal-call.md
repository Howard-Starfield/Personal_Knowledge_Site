    type: free-recall
    prompt: "A consumer reports a higher income on the application in November. Explain what happens to their premium and why the new figure jumps so much."
    sampleAnswer: "Reporting a higher income on the application reduces the tax credits the consumer is eligible for through the Affordable Care Act, so less subsidy is applied and more of the premium comes out of the consumer's own pocket. The application works out the subsidy already received for the year up to October and the subsidy left for the remaining months on the new income, and then spreads whatever subsidy is left over the months that remain in the year. So one month of higher income changes every remaining month, and the figure bites hardest at first because a whole year's worth of subsidy has to be recovered. In the training's example the premium went from $285.83 to $472.61 from 1 November, on a reported income of $45,000. The reverse holds: less income means more subsidy and a lower premium."
    explanation: "Look for the direction of the subsidy, the spreading of the remaining subsidy across the months left in the year, and the acknowledgement that the figure bites hardest first. An answer that describes a penalty, or that treats the income figure on the application as take-home pay, has missed the mechanism. Lesson section: Step 2: When income goes up, the subsidy goes down."

    prompt: "Why can you not give a consumer their January premium before their renewal application has been submitted, and what does submitting it actually do?"
    sampleAnswer: "The renewal application is what applies the new year's subsidy and the new premium, so until it has been submitted the January figure does not exist to be read back. Submitting it does three things at once: it puts the new year's income in front of the subsidy calculation, it produces the January premium with the new subsidy applied and any premium increase taken into account, and it opens the comparison, because the consumer can then look at other plans to change from 1 January. The training's call followed that order: permission was asked for, the renewal application was done, and only then was the result read back, at $475.03 a month from 1 January, on a base price of $516.26 with $43.65 of remaining tax credit applied towards it."
    explanation: "Look for the three effects of submitting the renewal, and for the order the training worked in. An answer that quotes a January price without the application having been submitted has inverted the sequence. Lesson section: Step 4: You cannot see January until the renewal application is in."

    prompt: "A consumer's access code keeps failing as they try to create an online account. What is actually wrong, and what do you do about it?"
    sampleAnswer: "Nothing is wrong with the code and nothing is wrong with the representative: the representative does not misspeak and does not give the wrong code. What is invalid is how the consumer is filling in the second page, the personal information. They have to enter their personal details — given name, family name, date of birth — exactly as they are on the application. Two instructions on those pages cause the failure. Entering the social security number makes the system say the consumer already has an online account, because the system can create duplicate accounts and duplicate CalHEERS cases, and choosing an email address or a text number tied to a different application produces the same pop-up, so the contact method has to be mail. And if the code itself is lost or misread, because a lowercase L and a capital I can look the same, you simply generate a new one."
    explanation: "Look for the shift of blame away from the representative and on to the second page, the exact-match rule for name and date of birth, and the two fields the consumer must skip or change. An answer that blames the representative for a misread code has accepted the wrong diagnosis. Lesson section: Step 7: When the renewal call turns into an account-access call."

    prompt: "What does the training mean by one-call resolution on a renewal call, and what record do you leave behind?"
    sampleAnswer: "One-call resolution means the consumer came to submit an application and the representative makes sure they are taken care of within that one call, even where the consumer did not ask for all of it: online account access, adding a plan, the mixed-household explanation, and the deadline for a 1 November start. The reason is that it minimises the chance of the consumer calling back to ask for help with online account access after the application has already been submitted. The pace is not rushed for it: a renewal call is allowed to be long, a supervisor may message around the 45-minute mark to check everything is all right, and the instruction was not to rush the consumer. The record is the notes, which the training advised writing in Word or OneNote first and pasting in, because a crash while typing straight into the record loses the note; after-call work is three minutes with a hard ceiling of four; and the consumer gets the reference number for the call."
    explanation: "Look for the offer of unasked help, the reason it reduces callbacks, the explicit instruction not to rush the consumer, and the three parts of the record. An answer that reads one-call resolution as rushing the consumer has inverted it. Lesson section: Step 10: One call, and the record you leave."
