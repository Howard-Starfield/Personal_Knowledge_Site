    type: free-recall
    prompt: "A consumer says they never received a tax form. Set out the routes you work through and the question you ask first."
    sampleAnswer: "The first question is not what the consumer wants but whether they should have received a form at all, and if they should not, the job is to explain which rule stops it and point them at the body that issued their cover. If they should have received one, the next question is whether the document already sits in their online account, because the fastest remedy is access rather than another document. After that, a correct document that the consumer wants again is a reprint, and an incorrect document is a dispute. Filing a dispute where a reprint would do is the classic mistake, because it puts the case onto a list that staff would rather keep clear of."
    explanation: "Look for the order of the questions, the first question being whether a form should exist at all, and the warning about filing a dispute where a reprint would do. An answer that jumps straight to the dispute has skipped the cheapest fix, and an answer that never asks whether a form should exist will file disputes for people who were never owed one. Lesson section: Step 1: Reprint, dispute, or no form at all."
  - id: "96d1b12a-abc4-4b59-b783-98a2118e8d42"

    type: free-recall
    prompt: "Which changes go through a report of change, which go through a 1095-A dispute, and what can neither of them undo?"
    sampleAnswer: "Demographic data such as a name, a social security number, a date of birth and the contact details on the account goes through a report of change, while coverage dates, monthly premiums and the financial assistance totals go through a 1095-A dispute. There is no button that edits a form that has already been produced, so every change travels through one of those two processes. A demographic correction updates the current year, and once the change is completed a corrected 1095-A comes out for the benefit year before it, but neither process can undo the financial assistance the consumer has already received for that year, and income and tax filing status cannot be changed retroactively either."
    explanation: "Look for the split between demographic data and the figures on the form, and for the limit that a prior year's assistance cannot be erased. An answer that says a report of change can remove a tax obligation has over-promised, and an answer that treats every field as disputable has missed the split. Lesson section: Step 4: Changing the record, and what a change cannot undo."
  - id: "4feceb68-b043-4f7d-aeda-2459e9102723"
    revision: 1
    status: active
    type: free-recall
    prompt: "A consumer was terminated for non-payment and wants both their cover restored and their tax form corrected. What has to happen, and what does not follow from it?"
    sampleAnswer: "Reinstatement and a tax-form dispute are two separate decisions. The carrier waits out its grace period, then terminates for non-payment and reports it; a reinstatement request requires the consumer to pay back every past-due premium and bring the account up to date; and the carrier decides, so it can refuse even when the consumer has the money ready. A dispute starts a review of the form, and where reinstatement is refused the consumer uses the form as it stands and settles the assistance at tax time. The dispute also has to say that reinstatement is requested and record the consumer's agreement to pay the listed past-due premiums, because a comment that merely says two columns look wrong does not carry that request."
    explanation: "Look for the carrier's decision as the one that settles reinstatement, the requirement to pay back past-due premiums, and the fact that the comment has to contain the reinstatement request and the agreement to pay. An answer that treats a filed dispute as restoring cover has promised more than the process delivers, and an answer that leaves out what the comment must say has sent a dispute that will be denied. Lesson section: Step 5: Nonpayment, reinstatement and the carrier's decision."
