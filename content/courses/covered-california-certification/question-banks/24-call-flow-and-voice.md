    prompt: "Walk through the standard greeting. What should it contain, how soon after the call starts should it happen, and what does a good greeting actually do for the caller?"
    sampleAnswer: "Greet the caller within five seconds of the call starting: thank them for calling Covered California, give your own first name or a nickname that you use in the same way on every inbound and outbound call, and then ask for the caller's name. Say all of it in a clear, confident tone. The greeting does two jobs. It creates a positive first impression, so the caller decides whether you are trustworthy, and it gives the caller a moment to refocus while you explain what you are going to do, because a caller who has just been navigating a phone menu needs that moment. The timing matters because researchers put the time it takes a person to form an impression at somewhere between 4 and 15 seconds, and the training works with a figure of up to 30 seconds for making a first impression with a caller."
    explanation: "A complete answer gives the five-second timing, the three parts of the greeting, and both jobs the greeting does: the positive first impression and the moment for the caller to refocus.     prompt: "Explain the four steps of putting a caller on hold, and then explain the difference between a warm transfer and a cold transfer, including what you say before you make each one."
    sampleAnswer: "The hold has four steps: ask the caller's permission to hold and give the reason, wait for their reply, thank them if they agree, and keep the hold to four minutes or less, checking back in with them every four minutes at most. You hold when the call needs transferring, when you need to consult a lead, when there is a temporary system issue, or when you need to document the call in the case system. A warm transfer is one where the person taking the call over is on the line with you and has been briefed on the situation; the briefing can happen with the consumer waiting, or you can bring the consumer, yourself and the other person together on one line, and you stay long enough to be sure the consumer reaches an actual person. Before you dial you explain why you are passing the call on, ask whether that is acceptable, and wait for the reply. A cold transfer is one where the customer is told that the call is passing to somebody else, the number is dialled, and the representative leaves the line, so the new representative has no idea the customer has already spoken to you; the service centre seldom does this and usually gives the customer a number to call instead."
    explanation: "A complete answer gives the four hold steps in order, including the four-minute limit and the check-in, then defines the warm transfer by the briefing and the continued connection and the cold transfer by dropping off the line. It should also say that the service centre seldom makes cold transfers, because that is the surprising part and the one a caller is most likely to repeat back to somebody else. Lesson section: Stage 2: Holding, transferring and calling back"

    prompt: "Explain why a consumer asks to speak to a supervisor, what you can do to handle that request without handing the call over straight away, and the wording that makes an escalation more likely rather than less."
    sampleAnswer: "Consumers ask to be put through to a supervisor, or to someone at the next level of support, for one of three reasons: the representative's own behaviour on the call triggered the request, the consumer does not like the answer the representative gave, or the consumer does not want to wait for an answer. You do not have to refuse the request outright. The training suggests answers that let you try first: offer to assist with the request and transfer them to a supervisor afterwards if they still want one, ask what the problem is so that you can transfer them to the right supervisor, or get the supervisor while you find out why they called. If you then show empathy, listen properly and reflect the caller's words back, explain what is happening, and show that you can solve it, the escalation may not happen at all, and the escalation follows the agency's Escalation Task Guide, reference SC.212. The wording that makes things worse includes saying you have no authority to do what was asked, saying there is nothing further you can do, telling the consumer their supervisor will only repeat what you have said, blaming the consumer for the problem, and saying that it is agency policy."
    explanation: "A complete answer names the three reasons, gives at least one of the responses that lets the representative try to help first, and identifies the wordings that create an escalation rather than defusing one. The three reasons are diagnostic, because knowing which bucket the consumer is in tells you what to change. The wording matters because an excuse is defensive and hands the consumer an argument for the supervisor. Lesson section: Stage 5: Why consumers escalate, and the words that start it"
